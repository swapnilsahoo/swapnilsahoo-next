## LinkedIn draft 1: growth can reduce profit

A lunch business sells at ₹150. Each lunch costs ₹90 to make and deliver. Monthly fixed costs are ₹12,000.

It needs 200 lunches to break even on those listed costs. Now consider a ₹120 discount: contribution falls from ₹60 to ₹30, and break-even rises to 400 lunches. The kitchen can deliver only 300.

The useful question is not simply “Will more people order?” It is “Can the extra orders cover the costs within our actual capacity?”

I have added a free, editable example to the Learning Lab. Change the price, costs and capacity, then explain your decision. These are fictional teaching figures, and expected orders are assumptions to test.

## LinkedIn draft 2: make an AI brief reviewable

“Help with customers” gives an AI assistant a task without a boundary.

Consider a fictional receptionist who receives a request for 5:30–7 pm. The room closes at 6 pm, and no availability or price sheet is provided. A confident booking confirmation would be unsupported.

A useful brief specifies five things: the task, permitted evidence, output, missing-fact rule and human reviewer. It authorises a draft explanation and clarifying questions while leaving booking approval with the receptionist.

The free Learning Lab lesson now includes an editable task brief and a five-point review checklist. No AI account is needed: a classmate can test whether your instructions are clear enough.

### 1. The discount that needs more capacity

**Subject:** Before discounting, calculate the extra work

Tiffin Trail’s fictional lunch earns ₹60 contribution at a ₹150 price and ₹90 variable cost. With ₹12,000 monthly fixed costs, 200 lunches cover the listed costs. A ₹30 discount halves contribution and doubles the break-even quantity to 400. Capacity is 300.

Try the calculator with the discounted price. Then change one cost assumption and explain the evidence you would need before relying on it. Keep founder effort and delivery work in the calculation. A spreadsheet can show the consequence of an assumption; it cannot establish customer demand.

### 2. A confident reply can make the wrong commitment

**Subject:** Give your AI brief a missing-fact rule

A receptionist’s assistant has a room policy, but no dated availability or authorised price. Its draft confirms a booking anyway. Tone and speed cannot repair the missing evidence.

Open the task-brief template. Replace the fictional context with an invented version of your own task. State what the assistant may use, what it may prepare and which facts it must flag. Add a reviewer who checks the result before any external action.

Test the brief with a deliberately awkward request. Write down what you changed after the test.

### 3. Ask about the last time

**Subject:** Replace “Would you buy?” with a useful question

“Would you use a convenient clothing-repair app?” invites encouragement. “Tell me about the last item you needed repaired. What did you do next?” invites an account of behaviour.

Use the interview-guide builder to name the people and recent task you want to understand. Follow the answer without suggesting your preferred problem. Ask about alternatives, difficulty, decision-making and actual time or money spent. Approximate amounts are enough.

Keep reported behaviour separate from your interpretation. Six conversations can challenge an assumption; they do not measure an entire market.

### 4. A choice becomes strategy when something is excluded

**Subject:** What would you decline to keep your promise?

A fictional delivery business promises predictable scheduled routes to shops. An urgent one-off order offers an attractive fee, but serving it would delay the route using the same riders.

Before accepting, name the customer promise, supporting activities and the sacrifice the team would make. “Work faster” does not add a rider or remove a conflict. Then state what repeated evidence would make you reconsider the position.

## Volunteer pilot script

Invite adults who can decline freely. Avoid tying participation to grades, admission, references or paid access. Suggested invitation:

> I’m improving a free Learning Lab lesson and would value a voluntary 20–30-minute trial. Choose one lesson, try the exercise and tell me where the instructions or result are unclear. Participation is optional. Please use the supplied fictional examples and avoid personal or confidential business information. I will not publish your name, work or comments without separate permission.

Choose one task per volunteer: explain the discounted break-even decision; revise the AI brief so it cannot invent a booking; or produce five neutral customer questions. Observe the first attempt before helping. Ask what the learner expects each control to do, where they hesitate, what the result means and what they would do next. Record the difficulty and the smallest useful revision.

Use an anonymous participant code and keep only necessary observations. Record permission separately before quoting someone or publishing work. Do not infer learning gains from satisfaction alone. If using before/after tasks, keep difficulty and the assessment rubric comparable and report how many participants supplied both attempts.
