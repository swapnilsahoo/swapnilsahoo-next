Official Session 04 · Beyond the Five Forces

The Five Forces treat rivals as enemies.
What if your biggest rival is also your most important partner?

Porter's framework was built for industries with clear boundaries — car makers compete with car makers. It says almost nothing about a world where Apple both competes with and depends on Spotify, Epic Games, and thousands of app developers simultaneously. That's the gap this session fills: ecosystems, business models, and game theory.

The Assigned Reading

Courtney, H., Kirkland, J., & Viguerie, P., "Strategy Under Uncertainty," Harvard Business Review. Their key insight: most strategists assume industry structure is either fully knowable (traditional Five Forces analysis) or a total unknown — but most real decisions fall into two useful middle categories: a range of alternate futures, or a set of discrete scenarios. Ecosystem strategy lives almost entirely in this uncertain middle ground, because a platform's value depends on the independent decisions of thousands of complementors nobody controls.

Case · Tesla (MIT) + The Platform Wars

Complementors: The Sixth Force Porter Underweighted

Tesla's Supercharger network

Tesla built its charging network as a proprietary complement — a resource that made Tesla ownership more valuable without competing for the same customer dollar. Opening the network to rival EVs in 2023–24 turned a competitive moat into an industry-standard complement, trading exclusivity for scale and federal funding eligibility. That's a game-theoretic bet: is the network more valuable as a monopoly advantage or as the plumbing every EV depends on?

App-store economics, 2026

Apple and Epic Games are simultaneously rivals (over app-store commission rates) and interdependent (Epic's Fortnite needs iOS's user base; iOS needs games like Fortnite to stay valuable). Neither the "competitor" box nor the "complementor" box from a static Five Forces diagram captures a relationship that shifts between the two by the quarter, based on regulatory rulings.
