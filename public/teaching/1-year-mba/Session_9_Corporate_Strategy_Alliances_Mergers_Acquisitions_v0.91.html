Session 9 · Corporate Strategy: Alliances, Mergers & Acquisitions

Elon Musk paid $44 billion
for a company he could have built for a fraction of the price.

Before you ask "should we acquire, partner, or build this ourselves," you need a framework for the decision — not just a gut call. That's what this session gives you, tested against the most expensive, most public corporate acquisition decision of the decade.

The Assigned Framework

Capron, L., & Mitchell, W., "Finding the Right Path" and "The Resource Pathways Framework: Build, Borrow, or Buy," Harvard Business Review. Their core diagnostic: ask two questions about the resource you need. First, how well does your existing organization fit the new resource — can you absorb it without a culture clash? Second, how relevant is the resource to your core business — is it something you'll need repeatedly, or once? High fit + high relevance → build. High relevance + low fit → buy (acquire). Low relevance, needed short-term → borrow (alliance, license, joint venture).

First Principle Question

If a resource is genuinely critical to your strategy and you can't build it fast enough yourself, is buying the company that has it actually cheaper than it looks on the term sheet — once you count the integration risk?

Case · The Twitter Acquisition, 2022

A $44B "Buy" With No Integration Plan

First Principle Question

Musk tried to walk away from the deal mid-process over bot-account concerns, then was legally forced to close it under a "specific performance" court order. Does a buyer who tries to exit before closing still owe the seller good-faith integration effort afterward — or does the coercion change what "success" should even mean here?

Why "Buy," per Capron & Mitchell's own logic

Musk wanted an established, global real-time-conversation platform with an existing user base and advertiser relationships — high relevance to his stated goal of building "X, the everything app." Building that from scratch (as he'd have to for the network effects alone) would take years he didn't have. Borrowing (a partnership) wasn't available — Twitter's board wasn't going to license its user graph to an outsider. That left buy as the only path matching the resource's relevance.

Where the "fit" question got skipped

Capron & Mitchell's framework also demands an honest fit assessment — and by most public accounts, this is exactly what didn't happen. Roughly 80% of staff left or were laid off within months, the content-moderation and trust-and-safety teams were gutted, and major advertisers (including several Fortune 500 brands) paused spending over brand-safety concerns. The "buy" decision may have been sound; the fit assessment and post-close integration plan were, by most measures, not.
