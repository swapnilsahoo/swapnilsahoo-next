From formulation to implementation

A strategy becomes real through everyday decisions

Sessions 7–11 established how to compete, which businesses to own and where to operate. Implementation asks whether people can make the required decisions with the information, authority and incentives available to them. A new organisation chart can help, but a box cannot decide a customer complaint.

Organisational inertia: routines, commitments and power arrangements can persist after the environment changes. What once made a firm efficient can make a different strategy difficult to execute. Diagnose the mechanism: sunk assets, a familiar metric, a promotion rule or an approval habit.

Two required readings

Who can act—and what will colleagues reward?

Start with decisions and information

Neilson, Martin and Powers identify four building blocks: decision rights, information, motivators and structure. Their research gives particular weight to clear decision rights and information flow. Reorganisations often address visible symptoms while leaving the mechanisms of work untouched.

Ask who has final authority, whether market information reaches the right people, whether decisions are routinely reopened, and whether information moves across units. Accountability is weak when managers are judged for outcomes they cannot influence.

Apply it: follow one delayed decision from customer to frontline team to headquarters. Mark every hand-off, every missing fact and every person who can overturn it.

Read the article

Culture is visible in repeated behaviour

The authors map culture by how people interact—independently or interdependently—and how they respond to change—flexibly or with an emphasis on stability. Their eight styles are caring, purpose, learning, enjoyment, results, authority, safety and order. Styles carry advantages and tensions; there is no universally best culture.

Apply it: compare what leaders say with who gets promoted, what happens after a failed experiment and whether colleagues can challenge a senior decision. A poster is weak evidence of a lived norm.

Design choices

Which trade-off is your structure built to manage?

StructureUseful when…Watch for…
Simple / founder-ledA small operation needs rapid coordination and responsibilities remain manageable.The founder becomes the queue for every decision; tacit agreements stop scaling.
FunctionalShared expertise and efficient specialist work are central to a relatively focused business.A customer problem crosses functions but nobody owns the whole outcome.
MultidivisionalDistinct businesses or markets need accountable leadership and different operating choices.Duplicated work, internal competition and weak sharing of useful capabilities.

Mechanistic does not mean wrong

Stable, repeatable work may benefit from clear procedures and specialist roles. The question is whether the design supports the work and its risks, not whether the chart looks modern.

Organic does not mean ungoverned

Exploration benefits from discretion and fast feedback. Teams still need priorities, spending boundaries, owners and a way to stop an experiment when evidence turns against it.

Make the design concrete

Four questions before you redraw the chart

01 · Decision rights

Name the decision, one accountable owner, the people consulted and the conditions for escalation. “Everyone owns customer experience” offers no answer when two teams disagree.

02 · Information

Define the evidence needed at the decision point: customer signal, cost, quality and risk. A dashboard arriving after the decision is a report, not a control.

03 · Motivators

Ask what behaviour the reward actually induces. A team paid only for launch speed may bypass reliability work. Pair the desired outcome with a guardrail and inspect the process.

04 · Structure

Group work so necessary coordination is affordable. Decide what belongs in shared services and what remains close to a business. Structure should reinforce the preceding choices.

Three complementary controls: output controls assess results; behavioural controls specify how sensitive work is done; cultural controls shape shared expectations. A sales target, a required safety review and a norm of speaking up do different jobs.

Case evidence · Amazon in 2024

Would one operating rule fit all of Amazon?

The case describes cloud services, online retail, physical grocery, advertising, devices and healthcare. It does not provide a complete organisation chart or a measured culture profile. Use these businesses to design a proposal and label it as your recommendation.

Culture in practice

What behaviour would make your strategy possible?

Use the reading’s vocabulary to describe a desired emphasis, then translate it into a concrete practice. These are original design prompts, not additional claims from the study.

Exploration with accountability

A new service team might need learning and openness. Protect small experiments, require a written hypothesis and review disconfirming evidence. Funding every idea indefinitely is not learning.

Reliability without silence

A repeatable operation might need order and safety. Use standard work, record exceptions and reward early escalation. A tidy dashboard built by suppressing bad news is fragile.

Performance with cooperation

A results focus can motivate improvement. Ask whether one unit’s success transfers cost to another. Add a shared customer measure when the value proposition crosses organisational boundaries.

Changing a norm

Choose one behaviour to reinforce. Leaders must demonstrate it, selection and promotion should support it, and repeated routines should make it easier. Measure the observed behaviour instead of announcing a new value.

Discussion challenge: centralise or decentralise?

Propose one Amazon decision that should remain local and one requiring a group-wide standard. For each, identify where relevant knowledge sits, how reversible the decision is and who bears consequences outside the team. Defend the boundary.
