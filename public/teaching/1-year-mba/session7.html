Sustaining Competitive Advantage

Deterrence, preemption, and a company handed to the planet

Session 6 asked how a firm opens a gap between what customers will pay and what it costs to serve them. This session asks what keeps that gap open once rivals have seen it. The evidence is unkind: in one large sample of businesses, more than nine-tenths of the profit gap between the best and the worst had closed within ten years.

Ghemawat and Pisano’s answer is that advantage survives only behind barriers, and that the barriers which last are built from commitments that are hard to undo and capabilities that are slow to copy. Patagonia’s 2022 decision to give its voting stock to a trust and the rest to a nonprofit is the test case: the least reversible move the company has made, read here as strategy rather than as philanthropy.

Carried forward · Sessions 1, 5 and 6

What you bring into this session

You already know how to find an advantage and how to test whether it is valuable, rare and hard to copy. Today the question changes from is there an advantage? to what stops it being competed away, and for how long?

Session 6 · Creating advantage

An advantage is a wider wedge

Ghemawat and Pisano open their note with the standard view of advantage: open the widest possible gap between the benefits delivered to customers and the costs the organisation incurs (1997, p. 1). Session 6 built that wedge for Patagonia. This session asks why wedges so rarely stay wide.

Session 5 · Resources and VRIO

“Costly to imitate” becomes the whole subject

In Session 5 the I in VRIO was one test among four, and isolating mechanisms were the reasons a resource might pass it. Here we ask what makes imitation costly, who pays to keep it costly, and what happens to the value even when nobody imitates.

Session 1 · Porter (1996)

Best practice gets copied; fit is harder

Operational effectiveness diffuses quickly, and fit among many activities is harder to match than any one of them. Ghemawat and Pisano accept that argument (p. 12) and push past it: commitments and capabilities mean rivals do not all face the same productivity frontier (p. 13). Revisit Session 1

The trap this session sets

“Hard to copy” is not the same as “lasting”. An advantage nobody can imitate can still be drained by the people who supply it or wasted inside the firm that owns it. For every barrier on this page, ask two questions: who pays to keep it up, and who could take the value it protects?

The problem · Ghemawat & Pisano (1997), pp. 1–11

Why most advantage does not last

An advantage is necessary for superior performance, but far from sufficient. Ghemawat and Pisano’s first example is the real mousetrap industry: spring traps, repeating traps and glueboards all succeeded in the market without making their inventors rich, because they attracted imitators as well as mice (p. 1). Before you read on, commit to a number.

It is not one study

Fruhan followed large companies whose return on equity averaged more than 25% in 1976–82. Their median ROE ran 21% above the S&P 400 average in that period, but only 2% above it in 1989–93 (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, p. 2). A McKinsey study that defined excellence as ROE plus sales growth reached the same verdict about its impermanence.

Some convergence is sensible; most is not

A business earning 39% would be unwise to demand 39% from every new investment, so part of the fall is value-maximising choice. Ghemawat and Pisano argue that by far the larger part is unwanted and even unanticipated (p. 2). Managers understand regression to the mean; they misjudge how far and how fast it runs (p. 1).

Four ways an advantage leaks

A superior position pays off over time only if it keeps two properties: scarcity (rivals cannot simply have the same thing) and appropriability (the owners, not someone else, pocket the value). Each is threatened twice (pp. 2–3).

Scarcity · threatened from outside

Imitation

Rivals copy the position.

Competitors learn the details of most new products within a year; imitation tends to cost about a third less than innovation and to be about a third quicker (p. 3). As filters took over 80% of the US cigarette market, varieties rose from 18 in 1951 to more than 80 by 1967, and the advertising-to-sales ratio from 3.8% to 8.7% (p. 4).

Substitution

Rivals go around the position.

Changes in technology, demand and input prices, in that order, open the way (p. 6). In PBX switches AT&T harvested its old base, Rolm spent to defend its position, and InteCom skimmed the market and recouped ten to a hundred times its investment (p. 7). Sometimes the right move is to take the money and run.

Appropriability · threatened from inside the value chain

Holdup

Non-owners divert the value.

How complementary a party is sets the ceiling; its best alternative to a deal sets the floor (p. 8). NFL team revenue rose 77% in real terms in 1970–84, yet operating income fell by a third: players’ share of revenue went from 35% to nearly 55% (pp. 8–9).

Slack

The value is wasted inside.

Estimates put it at 10% to 40% of revenues, and the richest positions leave the most room for it (p. 10). By the late 1970s Xerox was dissipating at least 20% of its sales: about $5 billion over five years, more than its operating income for the period (p. 10).

All four threats grow with the value a position creates, and all four take time to bite (p. 3). That is why a fat margin is not evidence of a durable one.

Analysis Keep the four for the case. Patagonia’s 2022 ownership change says little about imitation or substitution, and a great deal about who can divert or dissipate the value Patagonia creates.

The outline’s question · Grant (2019), ch. 7, pp. 162–165

How is competitive advantage sustained?

Grant borrows Richard Rumelt’s term isolating mechanisms for the barriers that stop superior profitability being competed away, and organises them around the steps an imitator must take. To copy a rival, a firm must identify the advantage, have an incentive to imitate, diagnose what causes it, and acquire the resources and capabilities to reproduce it. At each step the incumbent can put a barrier in the way (Grant, 2019, p. 162).

  1. STEP 1 · IDENTIFY

    Barrier: obscure superior performance

    A private company need not disclose its results. Few food processors saw how profitable pet food was until the UK Monopolies Commission revealed that Mars’s Pedigree Petfoods earned a 47% return on capital employed (p. 162). Limit pricing, setting price just below the level that attracts entry, gives up short-term profit for the same end (p. 163).

  2. STEP 2 · INCENTIVE

    Barrier: deterrence and preemption

    Persuade rivals that imitation will not pay, or occupy the niches before they arrive (p. 163). Both work on the rival’s expected payoff rather than its ability to copy, and both are taken apart in the next section.

  3. STEP 3 · DIAGNOSE

    Barrier: causal ambiguity

    When advantage rests on a complex bundle of resources and capabilities, rivals cannot tell which part matters, so imitation is uncertain (Lippman and Rumelt). Where activities are tightly linked, an imitator that gets the configuration slightly wrong can land in the valley next to the peak (pp. 163–164).

  4. STEP 4 · ACQUIRE

    Barrier: immobile, hard-to-replicate resources

    Having diagnosed the advantage, the imitator must buy or build what it needs. Limits on transferring and replicating resources are the barrier here (pp. 164–165). Session 5’s costly-to-imitate test lives at this gate.

Two vocabularies, one problem

Our mapping

Ghemawat and Pisano list five early-mover advantages that impede imitation (pp. 3–4). Each one works at one or more of Grant’s gates.

The logic worth remembering

An imitator must clear all four gates. The incumbent therefore needs only one gate that genuinely holds. That is the same arithmetic Porter applies to fit in Session 1: a rival copying a system must match every piece, so the defence is as strong as its strongest link, not its weakest.

Set that against the PIMS evidence. Grant reads the other side of the record: profit differences between competitors often persist for a decade or more, which suggests isolating mechanisms can be very effective (2019, p. 162). Both are true; the question is which barriers you have.

Deterrence and preemption · the incentive gate

Making imitation look like a bad bet

Both work on the second of Grant’s conditions. Deterrence changes what the rival expects will happen if it moves; preemption removes the opportunity before it can move (Grant, 2019, p. 163). Neither stops a rival that is able to imitate. Both try to make it unwilling.

Deterrence

Threaten, and be believed

Grant’s three tests: “For deterrence to work, threats must be clearly signaled, backed by commitment, and credible” (2019, p. 163).

1 · SIGNALLED

The rival knows what will happen if it moves.

2 · COMMITTED

Something already done makes backing down costly.

3 · CREDIBLE

Carrying it out would not hurt you more than the rival.

A threatened price war usually fails the third test, because it would damage the incumbent more than the entrant; capacity already built is more persuasive (Grant, 2019, p. 92). Ghemawat and Pisano are blunter: “Talk of retaliation is, however, cheap” (1997, p. 4). A threat needs both the ability and the willingness to retaliate, shown either because retaliation pays directly or because the firm has already been tough at a loss.

  • •Reputation. Monsanto’s costly price war against the Holland Sweetener Company, after its NutraSweet patents expired, gave it a reputation for aggression that deterred other entrants (Grant, 2019, p. 163). Procter & Gamble’s long share wars in diapers and detergents protect it in other markets too (p. 94).
  • •Size. Scale deters only through the rival’s fear that matching it would leave supply well above demand (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, pp. 3–4).

Preemption

Be there first, and leave no room

Occupy existing and potential niches so the challenger has fewer places to invest (Grant, 2019, p. 163). Grant names three forms:

  • •Product proliferation. Between 1950 and 1972 the six leading US breakfast-cereal makers introduced 80 new brands.
  • •Underused capacity. Monsanto invested heavily in aspartame plants to protect NutraSweet.
  • •Patent proliferation. In 1974 Xerox held a wall of more than 2,000 copier patents, most unused; when IBM launched its first copier, Xerox sued it over 22 of them.

The cleanest case in the reading · Du Pont

Du Pont’s titanium dioxide process used cheaper feedstock and gave it a 20% cost advantage. Mastering it took $50–100 million and several years of testing in an efficiently scaled plant. Du Pont also built a large new plant that crowded out rivals’ large-scale expansion, and twenty years later it was still the only firm in the world running the low-cost process (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, p. 14).

Moving first is no panacea

In four of the five filtered-cigarette segments, a top brand’s order of entry matched its share rank; the odds of that by chance are under 1 in 250. But in plain filters, the largest segment, first-mover Viceroy was overtaken by Winston and then by third-mover Marlboro, repositioned with a cowboy image and a flip-top box (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, pp. 4–5). Their conclusion borrows Alfred P. Sloan: “If you are late, you have to be better” (p. 5).

What makes both work

Commitment. A deterrent is credible only if it is backed by commitment, and commitment means eliminating your own options. Grant’s example is Cortés destroying his ships on arriving in Mexico in 1519: there was no going back (2019, p. 92). Preemption is a commitment too: Ghemawat and Pisano credit Du Pont’s position to its aggressive capacity commitments (1997, p. 14). That is the bridge to Ghemawat and Pisano’s main argument, below.

Building the barriers · Ghemawat & Pisano (1997), pp. 12–18

Commitments and capabilities

The resource view and the activity view both explain why returns might stop converging. Neither, Ghemawat and Pisano argue, explains how a firm gets ahead in the first place (p. 12). Their dynamic view joins them up: the resources a firm has today limit what it can commit to; its commitments shape the activities it performs; and both feed back into the resources it will have tomorrow (pp. 12–13). That gives two ways to build a lasting lead, and both mean rivals no longer face the same efficiency frontier (p. 13).

Route 1 · commitments

A few large, lumpy, hard-to-reverse decisions

“Irreversibility, not the amount of money involved, is the correct measure of whether a particular decision is commitment-intensive or not.”Ghemawat & Pisano (1997, p. 13)

SUNK COST → LOCK-IN

Specialised money that cannot be recovered. At one point Boeing’s net investment in the 747 exceeded its net worth (p. 13).

OPPORTUNITY COST → LOCK-OUT

Options that are costly to revive. Reynolds closed a refinery for good; Pepsi’s late move abroad shows that not investing commits you too (pp. 13–14).

TIME LAG

Years to change course. Coors’s national roll-out needed the better part of a decade (p. 14).

Against the four threats, commitments preempt imitation (Du Pont); they can fight substitution or fund a switch around it (Intel’s commitment to CISC had so far held off RISC); and they move bargaining power against holdup. The American who imported Cuisinart food processors owned the brand, so he could take manufacturing to East Asia and keep most of the value (pp. 14–15). They do least against slack.

Under uncertainty, build scenarios rather than raising the hurdle rate; do not try to stay totally flexible, which brings lock-out and mediocre results; and prefer commitments with a high learn-to-burn ratio, the rate at which you learn whether the commitment is a plum or a lemon divided by the rate at which you sink costs into it. Pilots, staging, milestones and kill triggers raise it (p. 15).

Route 2 · capabilities

Many small investments that compound

A capability is what an organisation can do within a predictable range of proficiency, built over many projects and many years (p. 16). Honda and Toyota in the 1980s aimed the Accord and the Camry squarely at Detroit’s core, at similar prices: no creative positioning. Their edge was developing a comparable model in about half the time with half the engineering resources, and building it for about $2,000 less per car (pp. 16–17).

  • •Against imitation: the knowledge is tacit, learned by doing and slow to accumulate (p. 17).
  • •Against substitution: upstream capabilities let Microsoft move fast into internet software, within limits; it did far less well in internet content (p. 17).
  • •Against holdup: capabilities spread across many people cannot walk out with one of them. A start-up the note calls Genes’R’Us was left with rights worth almost nothing when its lead scientist left; Amgen and Genentech lose talented scientists routinely and carry on (pp. 17–18).

The catch: superiority is hard to demonstrate, so managers call everything they care about a core capability; coherence is nibbled away by drop-in-the-bucket choices; and specific capabilities bring specific rigidities (p. 18).

The impossibility theorem

No firm can sustain an advantage without superior specialised resources or knowledge. Commitments and capabilities are therefore necessary for sustained success, and not sufficient (p. 18).

In-class simulation · Project Chacabuco

Who should own Patagonia?

It is late 2021 in the role-play. In the winter of 2019 Charles Conn and Kris Tompkins concluded that Chouinard was serious about succession and set up a working group, named after Douglas Tompkins National Park in Chacabuco, Chile (case pp. 6–7). By 2020 Ryan Gellert, Hilary Dessouky and Greg Curtis were on the team, and it met every two weeks for nearly two years (pp. 1, 7). The family has now called the meeting that decides.

A transfer of the stock could trigger a tax of about 40% of the company’s value, easily over $1 billion. With almost all their wealth in Patagonia shares, the Chouinards would have to sell stock to pay it, bringing in outside owners who could dilute the mission (p. 7). The family also wanted to stay involved: Yvon and Malinda were in every day, and both children worked at the company (p. 7).

Irreversible, in Ghemawat and Pisano’s sense

Analysis

Sunk: the family gave away the economic value of the company and paid $17.5 million to do it. Lock-out: the stock “would not be transferred again” (p. 12), so the sale, the listing and the heir who wants the cash are gone as options. Compare 2011: charter commitments that only a unanimous shareholder vote could change (p. 5) bound a family that held every share (p. 6) only to itself. The limit: the trust could still direct Holdfast to sell some or all of its stock if that served the purpose (p. 12). Hard to reverse, then, not impossible.

What it does to imitation

Analysis

Rivals can copy the materials, the repairs and the giving, and Patagonia wants them to: sharing best practice is in its charter (p. 5). What a shareholder-owned rival cannot copy cheaply is an owner that takes nothing out. For that firm it is not an operating decision; its owners would have to give away their claim on the business. The structure blocks Grant’s fourth gate. It is not deterrence: nothing in it threatens a rival.

The first gate, kept half shut

Analysis

Grant: “One of the attractions of private company status is avoiding disclosure of financial performance” (2019, p. 162). Patagonia did not publicly share its financial data (case p. 2); a public listing would have meant publishing accounts. With the stock settled for good in a trust and a nonprofit, a listing is now unlikely, so the opacity is locked in as well. The price: customers, staff and the Holdfast Collective also see only what Patagonia chooses to show.

Holdup and slack after 2022

Analysis

Holdup and slack live in the gap between ownership and control (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, p. 8). The new owners cannot take value for themselves: “The purpose trust is the decision-maker, but it doesn’t get any of the financial benefits” (Dessouky, case p. 12). That closes the classic routes of extraction. But the dividend is whatever is left after management’s own reinvestment recommendation (p. 11), and no outside shareholder now prices the firm. Rich positions that are hard to monitor are where slack grows (Xerox, pp. 10–11). The question for Gellert follows: who presses for discipline when the dividend is whatever management says is left after reinvestment, and 98% of it goes to the Holdfast Collective (p. 11)?

Deterrence, preemption, or neither? · Analysis

Read strictly, neither. Nothing in the structure threatens a rival or occupies a niche a rival wanted. It is a commitment aimed inward and forward: at future owners, and at Patagonia’s own managers. If it preempts anything, it preempts Patagonia’s own future options. Argue the other side in the discussion.
