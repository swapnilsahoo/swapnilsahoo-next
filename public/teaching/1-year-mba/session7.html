Business Strategy: Differentiation, Cost Leadership & Blue Ocean

Two ways to win, one way to lose, and a company that keeps saying no

In a world of rivals, why can’t a business just be good at everything? Porter’s answer is that there are only two basic types of competitive advantage, lower cost and differentiation, each pursued across a whole industry or in a narrow part of it, and that a firm reaching for every kind of advantage usually ends up with none. Kim and Mauborgne reply that the trade-off binds only firms fighting over demand that already exists. Ghemawat and Pisano add that whichever position you choose lasts only as long as the commitments and capabilities behind it.

Session 6 measured Patagonia’s advantage as a wedge between the willingness to pay it generates and the costs it incurs, wider than its rivals’ (Ghemawat & Rivkin, 1998, p. 3), and asked when shared value widens it. There are two ways to widen a wedge: raise willingness to pay by more than cost, or cut cost by more than willingness to pay. Today you choose. Patagonia sells about 1,400 products and holds the largest share of US outdoor apparel, yet it caps its own growth and once told customers not to buy its jacket (case, pp. 2–5). Which generic strategy is that, and has it opened a blue ocean or simply priced its difference?

Question 01 · Porter (1985) and Rothaermel (2024, ch. 6)

Why must a firm choose how it will win?

A firm may have many strengths and weaknesses against its rivals, but Porter argues that each matters only through its effect on relative cost or on differentiation. Combine those two types of advantage with the scope of the target, the whole industry or a narrow segment of it, and you get three generic strategies, the third of which has two variants (1985, pp. 11–12). “Generic” is the point: the actions differ by industry, but these are the routes to advantage worth probing in any industry.

The textbook puts the same choice in the language of Session 6. A firm’s strategic position is its profile on value created and cost incurred; because higher value tends to require higher cost, managers must make trade-offs between a cost position and a value position (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 212). The professor’s opening question puts it more bluntly: try to be everything to everyone and you become nothing to anyone, a commodity.

Cost leadership · pp. 12–14

Be the low-cost producer

Broad scope, often across many segments, because breadth itself feeds the cost advantage. Sources include scale, proprietary technology and preferential access to raw materials, and the product is usually standard or no-frills. It is more than going down the learning curve: the leader must find and exploit all sources of cost advantage.

Differentiation · p. 14

Be unique where buyers care

Choose one or more attributes that many buyers see as important and position uniquely to meet them; the reward is a premium price. Caterpillar built its position in construction equipment on durability, service, spare-parts availability and its dealer network. The means differ in every industry.

Focus · pp. 15–16

Serve a segment to the exclusion of others

The focuser optimises for its target and wins there even without an advantage overall. It works only if the segment is genuinely different: buyers with unusual needs, or a production and delivery system unlike the rest of the industry’s. Narrowness alone is not enough.

The confusion Porter flags · p. 15, n. 8

Broad differentiation or differentiation focus?

The two strategies most often confused in practice. The broad differentiator builds on attributes valued widely across the industry (his example: IBM in computers); the focuser finds segments with special needs and meets them better (Cray Research). Hold this distinction: it is the Patagonia question.

What each strategy must also get right

Every generic strategy has a second condition, on the dimension it does not compete on. Most failures in Porter’s examples come from that second condition, not the first.

Cost leader

Parity or proximity in differentiation

If buyers do not see the product as comparable, the leader must discount below rivals and the cost advantage drains away. Texas Instruments in watches never closed the gap and left the industry; Northwest Airlines saw the problem and invested in marketing and service. There is room for only one cost leader: several aspirants fight over every point of share (pp. 13–14).

Differentiator

Parity or proximity in cost

The premium must exceed the extra cost of being unique, and a markedly inferior cost position cancels the premium. So the differentiator cuts cost wherever it does not affect uniqueness. Several differentiators can prosper in one industry if buyers value different attributes (p. 14).

Focuser

A segment that is different, and attractive

Royal Crown focused on cola, but Coca-Cola and Pepsi could serve cola drinkers while serving everyone else, and their broader lines gave them the advantage. Some segments are also much less profitable than others (p. 16).

Differentiation · the professor’s question

“How can you convince a customer to pay more when cheaper options exist?”

Spend on what buyers value so that V rises by more than C. The textbook’s condition is ΔV > ΔC: a value driver adds advantage only if it lifts perceived value by more than it lifts cost (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 217).

Cost leadership · the professor’s question

“If you and a rival sell the same product, how do you build a system that wins a price war?”

Keep value acceptable and drive C below everyone else’s. Walmart, as the low-cost leader, took share from Kmart, which later filed for bankruptcy (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 219). Question 2 asks where the low cost comes from.

Read the fourth pair carefully

Higher value and lower cost is what Kim and Mauborgne call value innovation. Porter says it happens, but only under conditions that do not last (pp. 18–20). Question 3 and the stuck-in-the-middle section argue it out.

Required · Q3

Red oceans, blue oceans, and the trade-off they reject

Red oceans are the industries that exist today: boundaries are accepted, the rules are known, and firms fight for a larger share of existing demand until products turn into commodities. Blue oceans are market space not yet in existence, where demand is created rather than fought over. A few are wholly new industries, as eBay’s online auctions were; most are carved out of a red ocean when a firm moves an industry boundary, as Cirque du Soleil did between circus and theatre (Kim & Mauborgne, 2004, pp. 2–3).

The defining claim is aimed straight at this session. Conventional strategy says a firm can create more value at higher cost or reasonable value at lower cost, so strategy is a choice between differentiation and low cost. Blue-ocean creators pursue both at once (pp. 14–15). Buyer value comes from utility and price, the firm’s value from price and cost, so the leap happens only when utility, price and cost are aligned as one system (p. 18). Behind it sits a worldview: structure is not destiny; players can reconstruct market boundaries (p. 19).

Required · Q2

An advantage is necessary, and far from sufficient

The note opens with the standard view of advantage: open the widest possible gap between the benefits delivered to customers and the costs incurred. Then it spoils it. New mousetraps (spring traps, repeating traps, glueboards) succeeded in the market without making their inventors rich, because they attracted imitators as well as mice (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, p. 1).

Further · the framework

Ends and means: the wheel of competitive strategy

Before the generic strategies, Porter’s 1980 book defines competitive strategy as a broad formula for how a business will compete, what its goals should be and which policies will carry them out. His “wheel” puts it on one page: in the hub, the goals (how the business wants to compete, and its economic and non-economic objectives); on the spokes, the key operating policies. The spokes must radiate from the hub and connect with each other, or the wheel will not roll (Porter, 1980, Introduction, Figure I-1).

Different is not the same as differentiated

Firms are often different without being differentiated, because they pursue forms of uniqueness buyers do not value (Porter, 1985, p. 119). His list of pitfalls reads like a checklist for any premium brand (pp. 160–161):

  1. 1Uniqueness that is not valuable to the buyer, or not perceived as such.
  2. 2Too much differentiation: quality or service above what buyers need.
  3. 3Too big a price premium: buyers abandon it, and the premium drifts up when cost is not kept close to rivals’.
  4. 4Ignoring the need to signal value, which opens the door to a rival who understands how buyers judge.
  5. 5Not knowing the cost of differentiation, so it costs more than the premium recovers.
  6. 6Focusing on the product instead of the whole value chain.

Durability matters too. Stouffer’s differentiation in frozen food rested on factors rivals would have to invest heavily to replicate, and lasted; Hanes’s new pantyhose packaging and grocery distribution were policy choices with no barrier behind them, were widely imitated and earned no significant premium (p. 160).

Further · the textbook, chapter 6

Levers on V, levers on C, and what happens if you pull both

Value drivers · pp. 216–218

  • Product features: OXO’s patented soft grips turn a utensil into a differentiated product.
  • Customer service: Zappos has offered free shipping both ways since 1999 and treated it as marketing; Amazon bought it for over $1 billion.
  • Complements: a smartphone is worth less without a service plan; Google pairs its phones with Google Fi.

Cost drivers · pp. 220–228

  • Cost of inputs: Gulf carriers’ cheaper fuel, capital and labour.
  • Economies of scale, up to minimum efficient scale; beyond it, diseconomies.
  • Learning curve: cost falls by a steady rate each time cumulative output doubles.
  • Experience curve: process innovation jumps to a new, steeper curve.

Blue ocean gone bad · pp. 231–235

Should a firm pursue both at once? “In general the answer is no” unless value innovation reconciles the trade-offs (p. 231). Fail, and the firm is stuck in the middle with neither a clear differentiation nor a clear cost profile (p. 234): JetBlue and JCPenney, below.

Stuck in the middle · Porter (1985), pp. 16–20; Porter (1980), ch. 2

Stuck in the middle, and the case for doing both

A firm that engages in each generic strategy but achieves none of them has no competitive advantage. In any segment a cost leader, a differentiator or a focuser is better placed, and if the stuck firm stumbles on a profitable product or buyer, rivals with a real advantage soon take it away. Porter adds that in most industries quite a few competitors are in this position (1985, p. 16).

How firms get stuck · p. 17

An unwillingness to choose

The different types of advantage require inconsistent actions. Successful firms get stuck too, when they compromise for growth or prestige. Laker Airways began as a no-frills cost focuser on the North Atlantic, then added frills, services and routes, blurred its image and went bankrupt.

The focuser’s temptation · p. 17

Success invites blurring

Focus means deliberately limiting potential sales. Once a focuser dominates its segment, it is most tempted to trade the focus for growth. Porter’s advice: grow by taking the same generic strategy into new industries, not by diluting it in the old one. Keep this card for Patagonia.

When being stuck pays · pp. 17–18

Only with luck, or separation

A stuck firm earns attractive profits only if its industry is highly favourable or its rivals are stuck too. A firm can run two generic strategies only in strictly separate units, as Trusthouse Forte did with five hotel chains aimed at different segments (p. 18).

Porter’s first book drew the stuck firm as a curve. In some industries the smallest firms (focused or differentiated) and the largest (cost leaders) are the most profitable, and the medium-sized firms the least. His example was US fractional-horsepower motors, where large GE and Emerson and focused Baldor and Gould did well (Porter, 1980, ch. 2).

He also said where the curve fails: in bulk commodities with no room for focus or differentiation, cost is the whole game; where cost hardly matters to buyers, share and profit can even run in opposite directions. The professor’s later material adds the modern caveat: some research finds “hybrid” strategies can succeed, especially in dynamic markets, if the balance is managed. That is Kim and Mauborgne’s territory.

The textbook joins the two ideas: a blue-ocean attempt that fails to reconcile value and cost leaves the firm stuck in the middle, with neither a clear differentiation nor a clear cost profile (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 234).

Can a firm be low-cost and differentiated at once?

Porter does not say never. Differentiation is usually costly, so the two usually conflict, but if a firm achieves both the rewards add up: a premium price on a lower cost base. Crown Cork & Seal did it in the “hard to hold” uses of steel cans, with service and technical help on one side and dedicated, modern plants on the other (1985, pp. 18–19). He names three conditions, and each is temporary.

CONDITION 1 · P. 19

Rivals are stuck in the middle

Nobody pushes the firm to the point where cost and uniqueness conflict, until a competitor chooses one strategy and executes it well.

CONDITION 2 · P. 19

Cost depends on share or interrelationships

A big share lead lowers cost enough to pay for extra uniqueness, until a capable rival matches the share or the interrelationship.

CONDITION 3 · P. 20

The firm pioneers a major innovation

A new technology or practice lowers cost and lifts quality together, but only while the firm is the only one with it.

“A firm should always aggressively pursue all cost reduction opportunities that do not sacrifice differentiation.” Porter (1985, p. 20). He adds the mirror rule, to pursue every differentiation that is not costly, and then insists the firm must be prepared to choose.

The debate: integration, or “best cost”

Porter’s position

Cost reduction is not cost advantage

Many firms cut cost while raising differentiation, and a firm that has never tried can find large savings with no loss of uniqueness. But against capable rivals also chasing cost, the next saving eventually costs some differentiation. That is where the strategies become inconsistent and the firm must choose (p. 18).

Ghemawat & Pisano’s challenge

Not every firm faces the same frontier

Honda’s Accord and Toyota’s Camry sat squarely in the mass market of GM, Ford and Chrysler at similar prices: “No creative positioning here” (1997, p. 16). Yet they were developed in about half the time with half the engineering resources, cost about $2,000 less per comparable car and had higher quality (pp. 16–17). Capabilities meant rivals did not share one efficiency frontier (p. 13).

Where they agree

Both tie a cost position to commitment. Porter calls cost leadership a strategy particularly dependent on preemption (p. 14). Ghemawat and Pisano’s Du Pont example shows how: a low-cost process, and a large new plant that crowded out rivals’ expansions (1997, p. 14). The textbook’s Toyota example has lean manufacturing giving it an advantage for a decade or more, until the process diffused (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 238).

Stuck in the middle, in commitment language

Trying to stay totally flexible rarely pays: it risks lock-out and yields, at best, mediocre performance (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, p. 15). A firm that will not commit to one set of activities is Porter’s stuck firm seen from the other side. For class: is a capability lead like Toyota’s a lasting position, or Porter’s third condition, which ends when rivals catch up?

    Mini-cases · the professor’s deck and the textbook

    When strategy goes right, and wrong

    Why do some brilliant plans fail while others redefine industries? Three short cases on strategic consistency. Facts about JetBlue and JCPenney come from the textbook; the Tesla and SpaceX contrast is the deck’s argument, kept qualitative.

    Stuck in the middle

    JetBlue: a blue ocean that turned into a zigzag

    David Neeleman launched JetBlue in 2000 to fly at even lower cost than Southwest while offering more service than the legacy carriers: one aircraft type, point-to-point routes, leather seats, live television, later the Mint suites with lie-flat beds (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 209). It worked while the airline was small. Then the trade-offs caught up. After the 2007 “snowmageddon”, when about 1,600 flights were cancelled, the board replaced Neeleman; by 2014 JetBlue lagged the Dow Jones US Airline Index by more than 115 percentage points (p. 210).

    Robin Hayes, CEO from 2015, cut legroom and added seats on one side and expanded Mint on the other; in 2020 came an alliance with American, in 2022 an agreed purchase of ultra-low-cost Spirit for $3.8 billion. JetBlue ranked last on delays in 2019 and last in overall performance in 2022 (p. 210). The textbook reads its value curve as a zigzag: a symptom of a firm trying to be everything to everybody (pp. 214, 238).

    Blue ocean gone bad

    JCPenney: how it sailed into a red ocean

    In 2011 JCPenney hired Ron Johnson, who had built Apple’s retail stores, to move a coupon-and-clearance department store toward the high end: boutiques, more exclusive merchandise, a better experience. He removed the clearance racks, stopped the discount coupons and rolled the changes out to all 1,800 stores at once rather than testing them first (“We didn’t test at Apple”). Within twelve months sales had fallen 25%; Johnson was fired in under eighteen months, after the company lost roughly half its market value and took on more than $4 billion of debt. JCPenney filed for bankruptcy in 2020 (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 235–236).

    The professor’s reading: Johnson misread the value drivers. He took away what the existing customers actually valued, the thrill of the hunt for a deal, and replaced it with a proposition they did not want, while raising costs. The V–C equation broke on both sides.

    Strategic clarity

    Tesla and SpaceX: one founder, two generic strategies

    The deck’s contrast: both firms are led by Elon Musk, and neither mixes strategies. Tesla raises V: brand, software, performance and its charging network; scale and learning are supporting tools for margin, not the strategy. The textbook calls it focused differentiation at the time of writing and shows why cost still matters to a differentiator: the Model S unit cost fell from $140,000 at 1,000 cars to about $57,000 at some 12,000, against an average price of about $90,000 (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 213, 224).

    SpaceX, in the deck’s telling, cuts C: reusable rockets changed the process of launch itself, a jump to a new experience curve rather than a slide down the old one, with value kept “acceptable” and reliability as the threshold. The deck’s percentage claims about SpaceX’s prices are not repeated here: no course source supports them.

    Why is Tesla, which manages cost hard, not stuck in the middle like JetBlue? Because one strategy is primary and the other serves it; JetBlue’s moves pull in opposite directions.

    Question 02 · Ghemawat & Pisano (1997)

    Where does a cost advantage come from, and what stops rivals copying it?

    The cost leader aims to be the low-cost producer, which is why the industry has room for only one (Porter, 1985, pp. 13–14). It reduces cost below rivals’ while still offering acceptable value: Kia offers models that compete directly with GM’s at lower cost and backs them with a five-year, 60,000-mile warranty (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 218–219).

    “We have economies of scale” is a conclusion, not an explanation. Porter pushes the analysis down to each value activity and asks which structural factor makes it cheap; he calls these factors drivers. Then Ghemawat and Pisano ask the harder question: once you are cheapest, why would rivals not simply copy you? Their answer is commitments that are hard to reverse and capabilities that are slow to build.

    Scale, learning, and the jump to a new curve

    Scale has a ceiling · pp. 221–222

    Unit cost falls with output up to minimum efficient scale, then flattens, then rises as size brings complexity. Gore keeps plants to about 150 people; Airbus’s A380 proved too large to be efficient and was discontinued in 2021.

    Learning lives in people · p. 227

    If the cost advantage comes from scale, worry about production runs; if it comes from complex learning, worry when a star engineer leaves. Hold that thought for Ghemawat and Pisano’s holdup threat below.

    An Indian cost leader · Narayana Health, pp. 226–228

    Dr. Devi Shetty brought the cost of heart surgery from $100,000 or more to an average of about $2,000 with equal or better outcomes: surgeons doing two or three procedures a day, six days a week; a 1,000-bed hospital against a US average of 160; local sourcing and shared services. His summary: “What health care needs is process innovation, not product innovation.” Learning curve and experience curve at once. The deck’s parallel was SpaceX’s reusable rockets.

    Two ways to make a lead stick: commitments and capabilities

    Route 1 · commitments · pp. 13–15

    A few large, lumpy, hard-to-reverse decisions

    SUNK COST → LOCK-IN

    At one point Boeing’s net investment in the 747 exceeded its net worth: sunk unless the plane flew.

    OPPORTUNITY COST → LOCK-OUT

    Reynolds closed its Hurricane Creek refinery for good. Not investing commits you too: Pepsi lagged Coke abroad and earns less than a tenth as much as Coke from soft drinks outside the US.

    TIME LAG

    Coors’s national roll-out needed the better part of a decade: distributors in 39 new states and its first new plant since 1873.

    Why this is a generic-strategy point: choices of competitive position are themselves commitment-intensive, because they involve many hard-to-reverse commitments to performing particular activities in particular ways. If positions were easy to reverse, a firm could try one and switch for free (p. 14).

    Against the threats: commitments preempt imitation. Du Pont’s titanium-dioxide process gave it a 20% cost advantage; mastering it took $50–100 million and years of testing; a large new plant crowded out rivals’ expansions, and twenty years later Du Pont was still the only firm in the world running the low-cost process (p. 14). Commitments can fight substitution (Intel’s CISC had so far held off RISC) and shift bargaining power against holdup: the American who imported Cuisinart food processors owned the brand, so he could move manufacturing to East Asia and keep most of the value (pp. 14–15). They do least against slack.

    Under uncertainty, build scenarios rather than raise the hurdle rate; do not try to stay totally flexible; and prefer commitments with a high learn-to-burn ratio (how fast you learn whether it is a plum or a lemon, divided by how fast you sink cost into it), raised by pilots, staging, milestones and kill triggers (p. 15).

    Route 2 · capabilities · pp. 16–18

    Many small investments that compound

    A capability is what an organisation can do within a predictable range of proficiency, built over many projects and many years; Intel accumulated its microprocessor know-how across hundreds of projects (p. 16). Some biotechnology firms could develop and scale up a process in about a third of the time others took, and the difference lay in processes, not spending (p. 16). Honda and Toyota are the cost example: same position, lower cost, higher quality.

    • •Against imitation: the knowledge is tacit, learned by doing, hard to observe and slow to accumulate (p. 17).
    • •Against substitution: basic capabilities let Microsoft move fast into internet software; it did far less well in internet content (p. 17).
    • •Against holdup: capability spread across many people cannot walk out with one of them. A start-up the note calls Genes’R’Us was left with rights worth almost nothing when its lead scientist left; Amgen and Genentech lose talented scientists and carry on (pp. 17–18).

    The catch: superiority is hard to demonstrate, so managers call everything they care about a core capability; coherence is nibbled away by drop-in-the-bucket choices; and specific capabilities bring specific rigidities (p. 18).

    The impossibility theorem · p. 18

    No firm can sustain an advantage without superior specialised resources or knowledge. Commitments and capabilities are therefore necessary for sustained success, and not sufficient.

    Scarcity · threatened from outside

    Imitation

    Rivals learn the details of most new products within a year; imitation costs about a third less than innovation and is about a third quicker (p. 3). As filters took over 80% of the US cigarette market, varieties rose from 18 in 1951 to more than 80 by 1967, and advertising-to-sales from 3.8% to 8.7%: a prisoners’ dilemma ended only by the 1971 broadcast-advertising ban (p. 4).

    Substitution

    Changes in technology, demand and input prices, in that order, open the way (p. 6). In PBX switches AT&T harvested its old base, Rolm spent to defend, and InteCom skimmed the market and recouped ten to a hundred times its investment (p. 7). Sometimes the right move is to take the money and run.

    Appropriability · threatened from inside the value chain

    Holdup

    Complementarity sets the ceiling, the other side’s best alternative the floor (p. 8). NFL team revenue rose 77% in real terms in 1970–84, yet operating income fell by a third: the players’ share of revenue went from 35% to nearly 55%, about four-fifths of the increase (pp. 8–9).

    Slack

    Estimates put it at 10% to 40% of revenues, and the richest positions leave the most room (p. 10). By the late 1970s Xerox was dissipating at least 20% of its sales: about $5 billion over five years, more than its operating income for the period (p. 10).

    Four gates an imitator must pass

    Grant borrows Richard Rumelt’s term isolating mechanisms for the barriers that stop superior profit being competed away, and organises them around an imitator’s four steps. At each one the incumbent can put a barrier in the way (Grant, 2019, p. 162).

    1. STEP 1 · IDENTIFY

      Barrier: obscure superior performance

      Few food processors saw how profitable pet food was until the UK Monopolies Commission revealed that Mars’s Pedigree Petfoods earned 47% on capital (p. 162). Limit pricing gives up profit for the same end (p. 163).

    2. STEP 2 · INCENTIVE

      Barrier: deterrence and preemption

      Persuade rivals that imitation will not pay, or occupy the niches first (p. 163). Both work on the rival’s willingness, not its ability.

    3. STEP 3 · DIAGNOSE

      Barrier: causal ambiguity

      When advantage rests on a complex bundle, rivals cannot tell which part matters; get the configuration slightly wrong and you land in the valley next to the peak (pp. 163–164).

    4. STEP 4 · ACQUIRE

      Barrier: immobile, hard-to-replicate resources

      The imitator must buy or build what it needs; limits on transferring and replicating resources stop it (pp. 164–165).

    The logic worth remembering

    An imitator must clear all four gates, so the incumbent needs only one that genuinely holds. It is Porter’s fit argument from Session 1 in another form: a rival copying a system must match every piece.

    Making imitation look like a bad bet: deterrence and preemption

    Both work on the incentive gate. Deterrence changes what the rival expects will happen if it moves; preemption removes the opportunity before it can move (Grant, 2019, p. 163). Neither stops a rival that is able to imitate; both try to make it unwilling.

    Deterrence

    Threaten, and be believed

    “For deterrence to work, threats must be clearly signaled, backed by commitment, and credible” (Grant, 2019, p. 163). A threatened price war usually fails the last test, since it hurts the incumbent more than the entrant; capacity already built is more persuasive (p. 92). Ghemawat and Pisano are blunter: “Talk of retaliation is, however, cheap” (1997, p. 4). Monsanto’s costly price war after its NutraSweet patents expired bought a reputation for aggression (Grant, p. 163); Procter & Gamble’s share wars in diapers and detergents protect it in other markets (p. 94).

    Preemption

    Be there first, and leave no room

    Occupy existing and potential niches: the six leading US cereal makers introduced 80 brands between 1950 and 1972; Monsanto built aspartame capacity it did not need; Xerox held over 2,000 copier patents in 1974 and sued IBM over 22 of them (Grant, 2019, p. 163). Du Pont’s titanium-dioxide plant is the cleanest case: cost leadership defended by preempting capacity (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, p. 14).

    Moving first is no panacea

    In four of the five filtered-cigarette segments, a top brand’s order of entry matched its share rank; the odds of that by chance are under 1 in 250. But in plain filters first-mover Viceroy was overtaken by Winston and then by third-mover Marlboro, repositioned with a cowboy image and a flip-top box. The conclusion borrows Alfred P. Sloan: “If you are late, you have to be better” (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, pp. 4–5).

    What makes both work

    Commitment, which means eliminating your own options. Grant’s example is Cortés destroying his ships on arriving in Mexico in 1519; Airbus advertised its A380 heavily in 2000–02, before the design was finished, to win orders and discourage Boeing (2019, p. 92).

    Question 03 · Kim & Mauborgne (2004)

    When does being different, or narrow, pay, and must it cost more?

    Porter’s differentiator is unique at something buyers value and is paid a premium for it, and differentiation is usually costly: to be unique, Caterpillar deliberately elevates its costs (1985, pp. 14, 18). The focuser does the same for a segment whose needs differ, accepting a cap on volume (pp. 15–17).

    Kim and Mauborgne answer the second half of the question with a no. Cirque du Soleil found that much of what circuses competed on was unnecessary and costly; by eliminating it while adding what theatre audiences valued, it achieved differentiation and low cost together (2004, pp. 15–17). Your job is to tell when that is real, and when it is a premium product with a story.

    The trap this question sets

    Different is not the same as differentiated: firms are often unique at things buyers do not value (Porter, 1985, p. 119). And a high price is not a strategy, any more than a low price is a cost advantage. For every claim, ask which driver makes it true and what it costs.

    Uniqueness drivers · Porter, 1985, pp. 124–127

    Nine reasons an activity is unique

    Policy choicesLinkagesTiming LocationInterrelationshipsLearning and spillovers IntegrationScaleInstitutional factors

    Listed roughly in order of prominence. Policy choices, such as features, services, advertising intensity and the quality of inputs, are “perhaps the single most prevalent”, so much uniqueness is discretionary (p. 124). That cuts both ways: a policy is also easy to copy.

    The decks’ list of differentiators

    Where buyers see the difference

    Unique features or performance; superior service or experience; brand and reputation (a commodity like salt can be branded); continuous innovation; convenience; exclusivity or scarcity, as with luxury goods kept deliberately hard to obtain; and an ethical or social stance, as Nike and Patagonia take. The textbook’s three levers, features, service and complements, sit underneath most of them (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 216).

    Some drivers last longer

    Policy choices may be easier to imitate than uniqueness from interrelationships or exploited linkages (p. 127). Learning differentiates only while it stays proprietary (p. 126).

    Uniqueness usually costs money

    Rockwell’s water meters outlast rivals’ because they use more bronze (pp. 127–128). Some uniqueness is cheap, such as better coordination of linked activities; different but more desired features usually cost less than more features (p. 128).

    A driver can switch sides

    Scale lets Hertz offer more pick-up points and faster field service, a source of uniqueness, yet scale can also make fashion-related firms slower to respond to buyers (p. 127). Remember it when you compare Patagonia with firms built for volume.

    Focus, and the three things blue ocean is not

    Focus · Porter, 1985, pp. 15–17; Rothaermel, p. 214

    Breadth of target is a matter of degree. What makes a focus strategy is that the target differs from the rest of the industry, so broadly targeted rivals serve it badly (Porter, p. 15). BIC pursues focused cost leadership with disposable pens; Mont Blanc pursues focused differentiation with “writing instruments” priced at several hundred dollars (Rothaermel, p. 214). Focus means deliberately limiting potential volume, which is why the dominant focuser is the firm most tempted to blur (Porter, p. 17). The decks name the focuser’s other risks: a small market that caps growth, and a niche whose needs shift.

    Red ocean traps · Kim & Mauborgne, 2015, ch. 11

    In the expanded edition of their book the authors list ten traps that keep firms in red water. Three are about this session’s vocabulary. It is a misconception that blue ocean strategy and differentiation are synonymous; that it is a low-cost strategy focused on low pricing; and that it is a theory of marketing and a niche strategy. Value innovation is defined by both sides at once, and by new demand.

    Test 1 · demand

    Name a noncustomer and the reason they stay out: price, inconvenience, unfamiliarity, or a poor fit with their needs. A larger share of existing buyers is not new demand. The book sorts noncustomers into three tiers: “soon-to-be” noncustomers on the edge of the market, “refusing” noncustomers who choose against it, and “unexplored” ones in distant markets (Kim & Mauborgne, 2015, ch. 5).

    Test 2 · economics

    Specify what disappears from the activity system as new benefits are added. Raise every factor and the proposal is expensive differentiation; reduce every factor and it is simply a low-cost offer. Value innovation needs the eliminate-and-reduce side to pay for the raise-and-create side.

    The strategy canvas · Kim & Mauborgne, 2015, ch. 2

    List the factors an industry competes on along the horizontal axis; mark how much each player offers on each. Join the dots and you have each player’s value curve. The canvas is both a diagnosis of the current state of play and a tool for action. In the US wine industry of 2000, more than 1,600 wineries offered essentially the same curve: different in the same way.

    Three tests of a good curve

    • Focus: it does not spread effort across every factor.
    • Divergence: its shape differs from rivals’, because it looked across alternatives rather than benchmarking competitors.
    • A compelling tagline: [yellow tail]’s was “a fun and simple wine to be enjoyed every day”.

    6. Look across time.

            A segment with special needs, and a cap on volume

            • The apparel began as gear for climbers: rugby shirts that stood up to abrasion from rock, then a pile fleece meant to beat a wool sweater that grew heavy and slow to dry on climbs (pp. 2–3).
            • Since 1991, growth has been limited to pre-set targets (p. 3). Porter: focus means deliberately limiting potential sales (1985, p. 17).
            • The 2022 philosophy says growth must never outpace the ability to make products with intention and quality, and that Patagonia should not encourage consumption (Exhibit 9, p. 19).
            AnalysisIt depends where you draw the industry

            Breadth of target is a matter of degree; what makes a focus strategy is a target that differs from the rest of the industry (Porter, 1985, p. 15). Inside US outdoor apparel, where the case measures its share, Patagonia looks like a broad differentiator. Inside apparel as a whole, the fashion industry the case describes on page 4, it serves the buyers who want clothes that last, get repaired and come from a firm they trust on the environment.

            Your answer commits you. Call it broad differentiation and you must show its attributes are valued across the industry and that it keeps cost close to rivals’ outside them. Call it differentiation focus and you must show its buyers’ needs really differ, then face Porter’s warning that a dominant focuser is the firm most tempted to blur (p. 17). One of the professor’s decks files it as a broad differentiator; the textbook does not place it.

            What Patagonia refused

            A position is defined as much by what a firm will not do. Each card states a refusal from the case, with its page; the grey line is this page’s reading of what was kept and what it cost.

            Refused · growth for its own sake

            In the 1991 recession a lender cut its credit line and Patagonia laid off 20% of its 600 staff to reduce costs. Chouinard concluded the company had been growing for the sake of growing; from then on growth was held to pre-set targets (p. 3).

            Kept: a stable workplace. Cost: scale it chose not to have.

            Refused · conventional cotton

            A 1994 assessment found cotton fields used 22.5% of chemical insecticides on 2.5% of cultivated land. Chouinard gave the company eighteen months to move all 166 cotton products to organic cotton or stop selling sportswear, then 30% of the business. Sales were back to their earlier level within two years (pp. 3–4).

            Kept: product integrity. Cost: a bet with 30% of the business on it.

            Refused · selling more units

            On Black Friday 2011 a full-page New York Times ad showed its fleece with the words “DON’T BUY THIS JACKET” and launched the Common Threads Initiative: reduce, repair, reuse, recycle, reimagine (p. 5).

            Kept: credibility on its claims. Cost: sales it asked customers not to make.

            Refused · the disposable garment

            Worn Wear repairs Patagonia clothing free of charge; a buyback scheme resells or recycles used items for store credit. The Reno facility repaired 100,000 items a year (p. 4).

            Kept: the durability promise, made usable. Cost: free repairs.

            Refused · wholesale on any terms

            It has a large wholesale business (p. 2), but the 2022 philosophy says partners, wholesale included, should mirror Patagonia’s values and complement its direct business rather than compete with it (Exhibit 9, p. 19).

            Kept: control of how the brand reaches buyers. Cost: reach it could buy elsewhere.

            Refused · profit as the scorecard

            Since 1986 it has given the greater of 1% of sales or 10% of profit to environmental causes (p. 3). The 2022 philosophy adds that financial success should enable good but does not equal success, and that marketing should not be judged on ROI (Exhibit 9, p. 19).

            Kept: a purpose buyers can check. Cost: a slice of every year’s sales.

            Differentiation in layers, built over fifty years

            The professor’s earlier deck read Patagonia’s differentiation as an organisational philosophy in four layers. Open each, then check the timeline: a layer built over decades is a different imitation problem from a feature launched last year.

            Operations

            Planet-first decisions that Chouinard says made money: “every time I have made a decision that’s best for the planet, [Patagonia has] made money” (p. 3). Organic cotton; Worn Wear; an early onsite daycare centre and paid parental leave before they were common; growth held to targets; B Corp certification and California benefit-corporation status (pp. 3–5).

            Product design

            Useful, versatile, long-lasting, repairable and recyclable, with an ideal of regenerative products that give back to the earth as much as they take (Exhibit 2). Products meant to be timeless, essential, best quality and multifunctional where possible (Exhibit 9). Material innovation from pile fleece to organic cotton (pp. 3–4).

            Brand messaging

            “DON’T BUY THIS JACKET”; the 2016 “100% for the Planet” Black Friday, which gave away all $10 million of that day’s sales; opposing the US withdrawal from the Paris accords and suing the government over Bears Ears (p. 5). The philosophy wants Patagonia itself to be “an act of activism” and marketing judged on reputation, not ROI (Exhibit 9).

            Corporate mission

            From “Make the best product, cause no unnecessary harm…” (1991) to “We’re in business to save our home planet” (2018), and in 2022 an ownership structure built to protect the purpose for the long run and send profits to the environmental crisis (pp. 1, 3, 6, 11–12).

            Analysis

            The proximity test the case cannot settle

            Porter’s differentiator must keep cost close to rivals’ in everything that does not create its uniqueness (1985, p. 14). The case shows activities that add cost on purpose: organic cotton, free repairs, 1% of sales given away. It shows none that hold cost down outside them, because Patagonia does not publish its accounts. The media estimates imply a net margin of roughly 10%, but two estimates divided give only a rough figure.

            Analysis

            Commitments behind the position

            Positions are commitment-intensive (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, p. 14), and Patagonia’s are unusually hard to undo. Since 2011 its environmental and stakeholder commitments sit in its articles and bylaws and cannot be changed without a unanimous shareholder vote (case, p. 5). The 2022 transfer, in the exhibit workshop below, made them harder still. A rival copying the products would not be copying these.

            Analysis

            Where the position could crack

            Porter’s pitfall of too big a price premium: buyers abandon a differentiator whose premium outruns its value, and the premium drifts up when cost is not kept close to rivals’ (1985, pp. 160–161). Every cost Patagonia adds on purpose widens the gap its prices must cover. The test is whether buyers go on valuing what the extra cost buys.

            The question the room usually splits on

            “Every time I have made a decision that’s best for the planet, [Patagonia has] made money” (Chouinard, case, p. 3). Is that a firm that has escaped the cost–differentiation trade-off, Kim and Mauborgne’s value innovator, or a differentiator whose buyers pay for the cost of its uniqueness, Porter’s reading? Both are defensible, and they predict opposite things: a value innovator should be bringing in noncustomers and shedding cost somewhere; a premium differentiator should be watching its price gap.

            Take a side before class, and name the number the case does not give that would settle it. A position you cannot imagine losing is not an argument; it is a preference.

            What the numbers do and do not settle

            The family gave up billions now for about $100 million a year, and paid $17.5 million to do it. In Ghemawat and Pisano’s terms that is a sunk commitment with lock-out: the stock “would not be transferred again” (case, p. 12), so the sale, the listing and the heir who wants cash are gone as options. Conn’s verdict: a better solution than releasing more money and “as a result, selling your soul” (p. 12).

            What the numbers cannot settle is whether the lock-in protects a differentiation buyers keep paying for. The trust could still direct Holdfast to sell stock if that served the purpose (p. 12), and no outside shareholder now prices the firm. Rich positions that are hard to monitor are where slack grows (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, pp. 10–11).
