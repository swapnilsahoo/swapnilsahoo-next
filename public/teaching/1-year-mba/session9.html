Corporate Strategy: Vertical Integration & Diversification

Which businesses to be in, what the centre adds, and where a chip designer’s boundaries should stop

The preceding sessions examined how a firm creates and sustains advantage. Corporate strategy asks two different questions: which businesses the firm should be in, and how the corporate office should manage them. Porter studied 33 large US companies from 1950 to 1986 and found that most had divested many more acquisitions than they kept (1987, p. 1). Corporate strategy must make the whole worth more than its parts; Porter’s historical sample challenges easy claims of synergy.

NVIDIA designs chips it does not make. It bought Mellanox for almost $7 billion, tried and failed to buy ARM for $40 billion, and now sells 375-pound AI systems and rents cloud servers, in competition with the customers that buy its chips (case, pp. 5–7). Today you put those moves through Porter’s tests and through transaction-cost logic, and decide where its boundaries belong.

Read the argument · compare the lenses

Value creation is necessary. Being the best owner is harder.

Does the scope move create more value than it costs?

Attractive industry economics, an entry price that leaves room for returns, and a concrete better-off mechanism are separate tests. Passing only one is not enough. Corporate strategy should reinforce the competitive position of its businesses, rather than merely collect businesses under one name.

Distinguish a portfolio manager, a restructuring parent, a transfer of skills, and shared activities. Each has a different reason for ownership and a different coordination burden. A skill transfer may create a one-time gain; a shared activity may need continuing coordination. Ask when ownership adds something a contract would not.

Reading lens: Porter (1987), From Competitive Advantage to Corporate Strategy; assigned reprint. Applications and model assumptions below are learning exercises.

Case question: For NVIDIA’s networking, systems or cloud activities, which specific activity creates the better-off effect? What do the firm’s customers or partners give up as NVIDIA’s scope expands?

Could another parent do more with the same business?

Parenting advantage asks whether this parent creates more net value than alternative owners. Match its capabilities to a business’s improvement opportunities, while testing where its habits could damage that business. Product similarity alone is not enough.

Heartland: useful parenting and little harmful misfit. Ballast: understood, but little scope to help. Value trap: an apparent improvement opportunity with dangerous misfit. Alien territory: weak opportunity and high misfit. Edge of heartland: promising fit with unresolved gaps. Treat these as evidence-led judgments, not permanent labels.

Case question: If NVIDIA has strong chip and software capabilities, what additional capabilities would it need to operate a very different downstream business? Compare with a specialist alternative owner.

Core question · who should own the business?

A shared capability is a starting hypothesis

Make the ownership argument specific. List the unit’s critical success factors, identify an improvement opportunity, and name the parent’s relevant skill. Then look for a harmful mismatch: control systems, time horizons, channels, talent practices or customer relationships. The parent’s contribution must survive both the cost of coordination and comparison with another owner.

Vertical integration: adjacent stages

Owning an upstream input or a downstream route changes the boundary along a value chain. Compare supplier hold-up and coordination problems with the capital, incentive and operating burdens created inside the firm. Long contracts or alliances may secure some benefits without full ownership.

Diversification: additional businesses

Related diversification needs a transferable competence or a valuable shared activity. Unrelated scope needs another defensible parenting mechanism. Reduced volatility alone does not show that the corporate parent improves the businesses.

The NVIDIA case lets you contrast a designer’s dependence on manufacturing partners, the extension from chips into systems, and the use of a common computing platform across markets. These are different scope choices; do not give them one generic “synergy” score.

Extensions: Vertical integration and firm boundaries · Diversification and corporate parenting.

From how to compete to where to compete

The first part of the course was business-level strategy: how one business wins in one industry. Rothaermel puts the switch in one line: business strategy is about how to compete in a single product market, corporate strategy is about where to compete (2024, p. 298). Sessions 6 to 8 · advantage in one business

Only business units compete

Session 8 read NVIDIA as a technology and platform story inside one industry. Porter’s first premise keeps that work central: diversified companies do not compete, their business units do, so a corporate strategy must grow out of and reinforce each unit’s competitive strategy (p. 6).

The trap this session sets

Bigger is not the same as more valuable. Porter says executives most often skip the test of whether a new unit is better off inside the company, partly because they confuse company size with shareholder value (p. 11). And a hot industry is not an attractive one: “Industries are profitable not because they are sexy or high tech; they are profitable only if their structures are attractive” (p. 9). Hold both thoughts when you reach NVIDIA.

Corporate strategy · Porter (1987); Rothaermel (2024), ch. 8

Two levels of strategy, three dimensions of scope

A diversified company has two levels of strategy. Business-unit strategy is about creating competitive advantage in each business the company is in. Corporate strategy answers two other questions: what businesses the company should be in, and how the corporate office should manage the set of units (Porter, 1987, p. 1). The textbook frames the same choice as the firm’s boundaries, set along three dimensions (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 298).

Vertical integration · p. 298

In what stages of the industry value chain should we take part?

The industry value chain runs from raw materials to finished goods and services in distinct stages. Owning more of it is backward or forward integration; Vertical integration extension takes it apart with Amazon.

Horizontal diversification · p. 298

What range of products and services should we offer?

Single business, dominant business, related or unrelated diversification, and the discount markets apply to conglomerates, are for Diversification extension.

Geographic scope · p. 298

Where should we compete: regional, national or global markets?

Global strategy and the multinational are Session 11. Today the three dimensions are a map for describing NVIDIA, not yet a theory of each.

Four ideas the textbook uses to decide where to compete

Core competencies

Unique strengths embedded deep in the firm that let it differentiate, or offer comparable value at lower cost. What the firm knows how to do well belongs in-house; payroll and facility maintenance can go outside (p. 301).

Economies of scale

Average cost per unit falls as output rises. A reason to grow within a business until minimum efficient scale is reached (pp. 300–301).

Economies of scope

Producing two or more outputs together costs less than producing each on its own with the same resources and technology. The economic case for related businesses (p. 302).

Transaction costs

All the costs of an economic exchange. They decide whether it pays to widen the firm’s boundaries or to use the market; the subject of the make-or-buy section below (p. 302).

The record: Porter’s sober picture

Porter set aside announcement-day stock reactions and shareholder returns as measures: a profitable base business can hide a poor diversification record, as at CBS and General Mills. He counted instead how many acquired units each company kept (1987, pp. 2, 6).

Three premises Porter treats as facts of life

01

Competition happens at the business-unit level

Diversified companies do not compete; their units do. A corporate strategy that does not put each unit’s success first will fail, however elegant it looks (pp. 6–7).

02

Diversification adds costs and constraints

Beyond allocated overhead, units explain decisions upward, comply with planning systems and corporate policies, and lose the chance to motivate staff with direct equity. These costs shrink but never vanish (p. 7).

03

Shareholders can diversify themselves

They can buy shares at market prices and avoid acquisition premiums, so they often diversify more cheaply than a corporation can (p. 7).

The consequence. Corporate strategy succeeds only if it truly adds value: to units, through tangible benefits that outweigh their lost independence, and to shareholders, through diversification they could not copy on their own (p. 7). The three tests turn that into a checklist.

Passing the essential tests · Porter (1987), pp. 7–11

Three tests every diversification move must pass

Porter turns “add value” into three conditions under which diversification creates shareholder value. Most companies make sure their plans pass some of them; his study found that ignoring one or two produced disastrous results (p. 8). Notice that the first test is about the industry, the second about the price of getting in, and only the third about the company.

Test 1 · pp. 8–9

Attractiveness

The industry must be structurally attractive, or capable of being made so.

Its structure must support returns above the cost of capital; otherwise the entrant must restructure the industry or win an advantage well above the average. The industry need not be attractive already, since early entry can reshape it. Companies drop this test for three reasons: a comforting sense of “fit” (Shell and other oil majors in chemicals), a low price, or fast growth mistaken for profit potential, which burned many entrants to personal computers, video games and robotics.

Test 2 · pp. 9–10

Cost of entry

The cost of entry must not capitalise all the future profits.

An acquirer faces an efficient market for companies, where premiums price in the target’s prospects. Philip Morris paid more than four times book value for Seven-Up, so profits had to more than quadruple to hold its return; they did not, and it sold. A start-up instead pays the entry barriers in full, and attractive industries are attractive because those barriers are high. “The more attractive a new industry, the more expensive it is to get into” (p. 10).

Test 3 · pp. 10–11

Better-off

The new unit must gain advantage from its link with the corporation, or the corporation from it.

Some benefits arrive once, as when a parent overhauls strategy or installs a strong team; then the parent should sell the unit when the results show. Others last, as when the new unit sells through sister units’ distribution, a logic behind the Baxter Travenol and American Hospital Supply merger. Spreading corporate risk does not count: shareholders can do it themselves.

Concepts of corporate strategy · Porter (1987), pp. 11–27

Four ways a corporate centre can add value

From the companies he studied, Porter identifies four concepts of corporate strategy in use. Each rests on a different mechanism for creating value and asks the company to organise itself differently. The first two need no connections between business units; the last two depend on them (p. 12). His conclusion is directional: a company creates more value as its strategy moves from portfolio management towards sharing activities (p. 26).

The outline’s three approaches, mapped onto the readings

The course outline names a portfolio approach, a synergy approach and core competencies. Porter’s four concepts and the textbook cover all three, from different angles.

Portfolio approach

The centre as banker and reviewer

This is Porter’s portfolio management, with restructuring as its active cousin; in both, the centre is “a selector, a banker, and an intervenor” (p. 18). The textbook’s tool is the BCG growth-share matrix, which sorts units by relative market share and market growth into dogs, cash cows, stars and question marks, each with an investment rule (Rothaermel, pp. 331–332). Internal capital markets create value only if headquarters allocates capital better than outside markets would (p. 332).

Synergy approach

Links between units, defined precisely

Porter’s transferring skills and sharing activities. He warns that annual reports make almost anything look related, and that “imagined synergy is much more common than real synergy” (p. 18). GM bought Hughes Aircraft because cars were going electronic; he calls that paper synergy. Real synergy is defined at the level of value activities, with the conditions below.

Core competencies

What the firm knows how to do well

The textbook’s idea, not Porter’s word. Core competencies are unique strengths deep in the firm (p. 301). They set the firm’s boundaries, and the textbook’s diversification types turn on whether units share them (p. 321). Its core competence–market matrix, adapted from Hamel and Prahalad (1994), crosses existing or new competencies with existing or new markets (p. 324).

Synergy, on Porter’s conditions

Transferring skills · p. 20

Similarity is not enough. Three conditions must hold.

  1. 1. Similar enough to matter. The activities are close enough for shared expertise to mean something. Broad likeness, such as being marketing-intensive or bending metal, is not a basis for diversifying.
  2. 2. Important to advantage. The skills concern activities that drive competitive advantage, not peripheral ones such as government relations or real estate.
  3. 3. Advanced and proprietary. For the receiving unit, the skills are a significant source of advantage, beyond what competitors can do.

The transfer must be specific, staffed and rewarded; it does not happen “by osmosis” (pp. 20–21).

Sharing activities · pp. 22–24

A cost–benefit test, activity by activity

Benefits

Lower cost from scale, fuller use of capacity, faster learning; more differentiation, or differentiation at lower cost. Sometimes the shared activity can be redesigned entirely.

Costs

Coordination, and above all compromise: a salesperson selling for two units works in a way neither would choose alone. If the activity is not scale-sensitive, coordination costs win.

The activity shared must matter to advantage, as P&G’s distribution does for bulky, costly-to-ship diapers and paper towels. Sharing only corporate overhead is rarely, if ever, a reason to diversify (p. 23).

Parenting advantage · the outline’s second reading

Is this parent the best owner of this business?

The parenting lens adds a comparison with alternative owners. Use the reading lenses and parenting studio to test both useful intervention and harmful misfit. Begin with three questions:

  1. 1. Could this business raise capital, talent and legitimacy just as easily on its own?
  2. 2. Does the parent actively intervene, with capital, talent or coordination, rather than just collect dividends?
  3. 3. Would the unit be worth more inside the group than its standalone market value?

An easy standalone option raises the burden of proof for ownership. Set the three questions beside Porter’s better-off test and his second premise, that belonging to a group always costs a unit something (pp. 7, 10). The deck is linked under Go deeper.

Analysis

Where core competence and transferring skills meet

Porter’s third condition for transferring skills, that the skills be advanced and proprietary enough to be beyond competitors (p. 20), describes something close to what the textbook calls a core competence (p. 301). Read together, they give one hard question for any scope move: which specific competence does the new business borrow, and would a rival entering the same business lack it?

The answer for NVIDIA is not obvious. Its GPU and software competence is shared across gaming, data centres, workstations and cars, yet the case reports that its revenue outside gaming stayed low from 2012 to 2020 (p. 5).

Transaction costs and the scope of the firm · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 302–318

Make, buy or ally: where the firm’s boundary should sit

Porter asks whether a business should be inside the company. Transaction cost economics asks the same of an activity. Ronald Coase started the line of work with a blunt question: if free markets are so efficient, why do firms exist at all? The answer is that markets and firms are two ways of organising an exchange, and each has its own costs (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 302).

Using the market · p. 303

External transaction costs

Searching for someone to contract with, then negotiating, monitoring and enforcing the contract.

Using the hierarchy · p. 303

Internal transaction costs

Recruiting and paying people, space and equipment, supervising work, and the administrative cost of coordinating units: transfer prices, capital budgeting, resource allocation. They rise with size and complexity.

The rule · p. 303

Cin-house < Cmarket → make

When an activity costs less to organise inside the firm than to buy through the market, the firm should integrate it. When the market is cheaper, buy.

Firms and markets each fail in their own way

Administrative costs of bureaucracy; low-powered incentives such as salaries; the principal–agent problem, where managers pursue their own interests (pp. 304–305).

Specialised assets · pp. 305, 315–316

Why specific investments invite hold-up

An asset is specialised when it is worth far more in its intended use than in the next best one. It can be specific to a site, to one customer’s engineering needs, or to the know-how of one organisation. Once such an investment is made, a partner can withhold cooperation to gain bargaining power. The textbook’s remedy for hold-up in supply is a second source, “of, say, semiconductors” (p. 305); backward integration is the other.

Competence sets the limit · pp. 301, 316–317

Integrating what you cannot do well

Integration can raise costs, lower quality, reduce flexibility and invite legal trouble. The main reason to integrate is vertical market failure: the market is too risky and the alternatives too costly. The textbook reports that integrating two or more steps away from a firm’s core competency fails two-thirds of the time (p. 317).

Middle routes · p. 318

Taper integration and strategic outsourcing

A tapered firm makes some of an input and buys the rest, or sells through its own outlets and others’, which keeps in-house units exposed to market comparison. Strategic outsourcing moves an activity outside the boundary. The full benefits and risks of vertical integration belong to Vertical integration extension.

The case · Yoffie & von Bargen (2024)

NVIDIA in 2024: chips, systems, software, and a question of scope

Once a niche supplier of graphics chips for video games, NVIDIA had become a diversified semiconductor, systems and software company by 2024 (case, p. 6). Huang knew semiconductors were cyclical and wanted to widen the footprint from GPUs and networking to complete systems and cloud services (p. 6). The case ends on the scope question: how far up or down the value chain should NVIDIA play, with a single supplier on one side and concentrated, increasingly hostile customers on the other (p. 12)?

Are NVIDIA’s competencies shared across its businesses?

The professor’s teaching plan names three pillars: GPU architecture and parallel processing, the CUDA ecosystem, and strategic vision. Porter would ask of each whether it is an activity the businesses share, a skill they transfer, or neither. How CUDA became a platform is Session 8’s story; here the question is only what it does for scope.

Analysis

GPU design: a shared activity

One design capability supplies chips for gaming consoles, PCs and servers (case, p. 3), and parallel processing found uses in science, crypto and AI (pp. 3–5). Technology development is one of Porter’s value activities (p. 19), so this is sharing, and it passes his condition that the shared activity matter to advantage.

Analysis

CUDA: shared, and proprietary

CUDA ran only on NVIDIA GPUs and its SDKs spanned industries (p. 4). That meets Porter’s strictest test, expertise beyond competitors’ reach. The threat is that it stops being proprietary: OpenAI’s Triton let developers run code on other chips (p. 11).

Analysis

Networking: bought, not grown

Mellanox brought chips for fast links between GPUs and a place deep inside data centres, a new route to customers (p. 5). That is the better-off test passed through a shared channel. It serves one business, data centres, so it deepens scope there more than it widens it.

Analysis

What kind of diversified firm is NVIDIA?

By the textbook’s revenue rule, a firm earning 70–95% of revenue from one business is a dominant-business firm (p. 321). With data centres at 78% of fiscal-2024 revenue and almost 90% in the second quarter of 2024, NVIDIA fits that band, even though the case calls it diversified.

That reframes Huang’s worry about cycles. Porter’s premise says spreading risk is something shareholders can do for themselves (p. 7). So the argument for adding systems, cloud or cars has to be the better-off test, not a smoother revenue line.

Applying transaction costs · case pp. 3–13; Rothaermel (2024), pp. 301–317

Make, buy or ally at NVIDIA

The case puts the boundary question twice: should NVIDIA hedge its dependence on TSMC, and should it keep moving forward into its customers’ businesses (p. 1)? Before taking either, work through the activities one at a time.

The two boundary decisions the case leaves open

Upstream · the TSMC question

One supplier, no in-house competence

Case TSMC made over 90% of the world’s leading-edge logic chips and could demand that big customers prepay billions to reserve capacity (p. 4). It had raised prices in past booms, its production sat in Taiwan, and the one alternative, Intel, was behind in process technology and also a competitor (p. 12). TSMC took 18–24 months to build a fab; Huang wanted a new product every year (p. 7).

“In the event that we have to shift from one fab to another, we have the ability to do it. We won’t be able to get the same level of performance or cost, but we will be able to provide supply.” Jensen Huang, quoted in the case (p. 12)

The options the case leaves open (pp. 1, 12): stay with TSMC alone; move part of the volume to Intel, the most likely alternative, to build a second source; or integrate backward into making chips.

Analysis This is the textbook’s hold-up problem in its own example industry, and its remedy is a second source (p. 305). Making chips would mean integrating into an activity NVIDIA has always left to others: it built its business on design, with TSMC fabricating (case, p. 3). The three tests on building a fab show why supply security alone is not a reason.

Downstream · partner or competitor?

Selling systems and cloud to the customers who buy the chips

Case DGX systems put NVIDIA against Dell, HP and Lenovo, whose margins on NVIDIA-based servers were already thin because the chips cost so much (p. 13). DGX Cloud put it against the hyperscalers that rented out its GPUs profitably, and that were designing their own chips to replace it (pp. 1, 10). Amazon’s goal was to reduce the “Nvidia tax” (p. 10).

The choice, as the case puts it (p. 1): keep integrating forward and go head-to-head with its biggest customers, or stay a component supplier. Between the two lie stopping points along the chain mapped above: chips only; chips and systems; or chips, systems and cloud.

Analysis On Porter’s terms, the systems business may pass the better-off test and still fail his first premise, if it weakens the chip business that has to compete for those same customers. Transaction costs add a second warning: DGX Cloud runs on capacity inside the data centres of the firms it competes with, an alliance whose partners have every reason to limit it. How far forward to integrate is the Vertical integration extension debate.

Decision workshop · 8 minutes

Should an AI-chip company own the next stage?

Fictional board exercise, not a reported NVIDIA proposal: a chip company considers buying a small AI application business. The application team could use the parent’s engineering support, but it must sell through rival hardware ecosystems to grow. Which recommendation can you defend?

Consider the consequences

Engineering fit may create benefits, but mandatory hardware exclusivity could damage the application’s market access. Quantify both. Ownership should not be justified by a benefit while ignoring the parenting behavior that could destroy it.

Consider the consequences

A contract can test whether engineering support adds value while preserving distribution options. It may also leave coordination or intellectual-property problems unresolved. Set a measurable pilot and specify the evidence that would justify ownership later.

Consider the consequences

A categorical ban ignores potentially valuable skill transfers or shared activities. Separate the industry, entry cost and better-off tests, then ask whether this parent is better than a specialist owner. Relatedness is neither proof nor disproof.
