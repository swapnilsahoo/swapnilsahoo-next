Managing Technology & Innovation

Blue oceans, platforms, and a graphics-chip company that became an AI company

Session 8 ended on a claim Porter will not grant: that a firm can be low-cost and differentiated at once if it changes the game instead of playing it better. Kim and Mauborgne call the changed game a blue ocean. This session tests the claim on the hardest kind of strategy to plan, one built on a technology whose main use had not yet been found.

NVIDIA sold graphics cards to gamers. In 2006 it launched CUDA, a way to program those chips for other work, and for years few people needed it. By the second quarter of 2024 data centres brought in almost 90% of its revenue, and analysts put its 2023 share of the data-centre market at 98% (case, p. 6). Did NVIDIA create a blue ocean, and how long before rivals turn it red?

Carried forward · Sessions 7 and 8

Where Session 8 left the argument

Session 8 closed with Porter’s three conditions under which a firm can be both low-cost and differentiated, each of them temporary. The third is today’s starting point: a firm that pioneers a major innovation can lower cost and raise quality together, but only while nobody else has the innovation.

Session 8 · Porter (1985)

Choose, or be stuck in the middle

Uniqueness usually costs money, so cost and differentiation pull against each other, and the firm that will not choose gets neither. Kim and Mauborgne attack the premise: the trade-off binds firms that compete on the factors their industry already competes on, and breaks for firms that redraw them (2004, pp. 14–15).

Session 8 · Porter’s third condition

Innovation buys a temporary exemption

“The ability to be both low cost and differentiated is a function of being the only firm with the new innovation.” Porter (1985, p. 20)

Keep both readings open: is NVIDIA in a new market space with its own barriers, or in Porter’s temporary exemption?

Session 7 · Sustaining advantage

Every advantage leaks; ask what slows it

In Session 7 the barriers were commitments and capabilities. Kim and Mauborgne give blue oceans their own list: scale economies, network externalities, whole-system change and brand (2004, pp. 19–21). CUDA will test the second.

The trap this session sets

It is easy to call any spectacularly profitable new technology a blue ocean. Kim and Mauborgne say nearly the opposite: across a century of examples, blue oceans were seldom the product of technological innovation as such. They came from linking technology to what buyers valued, often by simplifying it (2004, pp. 11–12). Hold NVIDIA to that test. Was its blue ocean the GPU, or what CUDA let buyers do with the GPU?

Kim & Mauborgne (2004) · Blue ocean strategy

Red oceans, blue oceans and value innovation

Kim and Mauborgne split the business universe in two. Red oceans are the industries that exist today: boundaries are accepted, the rules are known, and firms fight for a bigger share of existing demand until products turn into commodities (pp. 2–3). Blue oceans are the industries not yet in existence, where demand is created rather than fought over (p. 3). A few are wholly new industries, as eBay’s online auctions were. Most are carved out of a red ocean when a firm moves an industry boundary, as Cirque du Soleil did between circus and theatre (p. 3).

The defining move

Value innovation

Conventional strategy says a firm can offer more value at higher cost or reasonable value at lower cost. Blue-ocean creators reject that tenet and pursue both at once (pp. 14–15). Buyers get value from utility and price; the firm gets value from price and its cost structure, so the leap happens only when utility, price and cost are aligned as one system (p. 18).

“Value innovation is the simultaneous pursuit of radically superior value for buyers and lower costs for companies.” Kim & Mauborgne (2017, p. 21; first published 1997)

Worked example · pp. 21–22

Ford’s Model T priced against the horse

Over 500 American carmakers built handmade luxury cars at about $1,500. Ford looked outside the industry for its price point, at the $400 horse-drawn carriage, and built a car for everyday use: one colour, few options, easy to drive and fix. Standard parts and the assembly line cut build time to four days against the industry’s 21.

Four findings that matter for a technology company

pp. 11–12

Not about technology as such

The underlying technology usually existed already; Ford’s line came from meatpacking. In computers, blue oceans came from linking technology to what buyers valued, often by simplifying it.

p. 12

Incumbents make them too

GM, Chrysler, IBM and Compaq were established players when they opened theirs, usually inside their core business. Blue oceans are often right next to you.

pp. 12–13

Study the move, not the firm

The same company can be brilliant at one time and wrong-headed at another. Compaq vanished into HP in 2001, yet its PC-server move created a multibillion-dollar market.

p. 19

Structure is not destiny

Red-ocean thinking treats industry structure as given, the structuralist view. Blue-ocean thinking assumes players can redraw it, the reconstructionist view.

Why blue oceans stay blue for a while

Creators usually go 10 to 15 years without a credible challenge, as Cirque, Home Depot, FedEx, Southwest and CNN did (p. 19). The barriers are economic and cognitive (pp. 19–21): scale that new volume brings fast (Walmart’s purchasing); network externalities, where more users make the offer more attractive (eBay); whole-system change that rivals’ internal politics resist (airlines copying Southwest); and brand, from the loyalty a leap in value earns (Intuit’s Quicken held off Microsoft for over ten years) to imitations that would contradict the copier’s own image (The Body Shop). Network externalities are the barrier to watch when we reach CUDA.

The tools · Kim & Mauborgne (2004, 2017)

The strategy canvas and the four actions

The 2004 article names the four moves behind value innovation: eliminate and reduce to cut cost, raise and create to lift buyer value (pp. 18–19). The tools that put those moves on one page come from two earlier articles by the same authors, reprinted in their Blue Ocean Strategy Reader (2017): the value curve and the four questions (pp. 10–12), and the strategy canvas (pp. 93–96).

Tool 1 · Reader, pp. 94–96

The strategy canvas

List the factors an industry competes on along the horizontal axis. On the vertical axis, mark how much each player offers on each factor, or, for price, how much it asks. Join the dots and you have each player’s value curve. One picture shows the industry’s factors, the rivals’ profiles and your own.

Three tests of a good curve

  • 1Focus. Southwest stresses three factors (friendly service, speed and frequent point-to-point flights) and prices against the car, not other airlines.
  • 2Divergence. Reactive rivals share one curve; Southwest’s competitors were so alike that the authors drew them as one line.
  • 3A tag line that is true. “The speed of the plane at the price of the car—whenever you need it” (p. 96). If you cannot write one, you probably lack a strategy.

Tool 2 · Reader, pp. 11 and 21

The four actions

Eliminate · cuts cost

Which factors the industry takes for granted should go? Tests whether they still deliver any value.

Reduce · cuts cost

Which should fall well below the industry standard? Finds what was over-designed in the race to match rivals.

Raise · lifts value

Which should rise well above the standard? Removes compromises the industry forces on buyers.

Create · lifts value

Which should exist that the industry has never offered? Breaks the industry’s boundaries.

Asking all four, not one or two, is what makes growth profitable (p. 21). A curve that keeps the industry’s shape at a slightly different level is still a red-ocean curve: rivals offering “a little more for a little less” rarely challenge the shape (p. 12).

Cirque du Soleil through the four actions

Assembled from the article’s text, Kim & Mauborgne (2004, pp. 15–17)

  • Three-ring shows

Reduce

  • The role of acrobats and thrill acts: kept, but shorter and more artistic

The result pulled in adults and corporate clients who had been going to the theatre and would pay several times a circus ticket (p. 2), while dropping the most expensive parts of a circus (p. 17). That is the test for every curve on this page: new buyers, and a lower cost base, at once.

When it goes wrong · Rothaermel (2024, p. 234)

A blue ocean attempt that fails to reconcile value and cost leaves the firm with neither a clear differentiation nor a clear cost profile: Session 8’s stuck in the middle. Rothaermel’s example is JCPenney, which tried to move upmarket and sailed into a red ocean instead (pp. 234–235).

After the leap · Reader, pp. 14–15

Imitators arrive eventually. Compaq’s first PC was a new value curve; then it spent years trying to beat IBM feature for feature, and by the late 1980s the two curves were nearly identical. The authors’ advice is to watch for converging curves and value-innovate again. Keep Compaq in mind for NVIDIA’s product cadence.

Innovation as strategy formulation · Rothaermel (2024), Grant (2019)

Types of innovation: what is new, the technology or the market?

A blue ocean describes a market outcome. To choose a technology strategy you also need to say what kind of innovation you are making. Rothaermel sorts innovations with two questions: does it build on existing technology or a new one, and does it target an existing market or a new one (pp. 274–275)? Four types follow, in two diagonal pairs.

Market

New market · existing technology

Architectural

Known components reconfigured to attack a new market. Canon redesigned the copier so small offices could run it without a service contract, a segment Xerox ignored (pp. 276–277).

New market · new technology

Radical

Novel methods or materials, often a new knowledge base. The jet airliner: de Havilland commercialised it first, Boeing rode it to dominance (p. 275).

Existing market · existing technology

Incremental

Steady improvement on an established knowledge base; most innovation is of this kind. Gillette added blades, one at a time, from one to six (p. 275).

Existing market · new technology

Disruptive

Starts as a cheaper, initially inferior answer to an existing need, improves faster than buyers need, and invades from the bottom up. Netflix streaming against the cable bundle (pp. 277–278).

Technology → existing · new

Rothaermel, pp. 275–276

Why leaders drift to incremental

Three forces: economic incentives (a leader protects the position radical change would threaten), organisational inertia (processes and power built around the current product) and the innovation ecosystem (suppliers, buyers and complementors that incremental change keeps happy and radical change disrupts). New entrants carry none of this.

Grant, pp. 222–225

Creating value is not capturing it

An innovation pays the innovator only if it keeps a share of the value it creates, its regime of appropriability. Four things decide that share: property rights, the tacitness and complexity of the technology, lead time, and control of complementary resources. Grant names Nvidia in graphics chips among the firms “brilliant at exploiting lead time” (p. 225).

The case · Yoffie & von Bargen (2024), pp. 1–6

Application of technology: from graphics chips to AI

On 18 June 2024 NVIDIA was briefly the world’s most valuable company, worth $3.4 trillion (p. 1). Its revenue had grown from $159 million in FY2000 to $60.9 billion in FY2024 (p. 2). The route was not planned in advance. Read it as one technology, parallel processing, looking for the applications that would pay for it.

  1. 1993 · p. 2

    Gaming as the killer app

    Games were among the hardest computing problems and sold in huge volume. Huang: “‘Video games’ was our killer app.”

  2. 1996–99 · p. 3

    RIVA 128, then the GPU

    With nine months of cash, NVIDIA cut an 18–24-month chip cycle to under six using software prototypes and sold a million RIVA 128s in four months. GeForce 256 (1999) was marketed as the first GPU.

  3. 2000s · p. 3

    Parallel, and fabless

    GPUs run many computations at once where CPUs work sequentially. NVIDIA designed the chips and left fabrication mostly to TSMC, escaping the capital a fab needs: up to $25 billion for one by 2024.

  4. 2006 · p. 4

    CUDA

    A C++-like language that became a platform for programming GPUs beyond graphics. Proprietary to NVIDIA hardware; free development kits for everyone.

  5. 2010–13 · p. 5

    The lean years

    CUDA downloads fell and, with the stock depressed, the board worried about raiders. Machine learning rescued CUDA.

  6. 2012–13 · p. 5

    AlexNet and the bet

    Three academics broke the ImageNet record using CUDA on NVIDIA GPUs. Huang then bet on deep learning while AI revenue was small: “we were no longer a graphics company,” one VP recalled.

  7. 2017–22 · p. 5

    The crypto bridge

    Miners bought GPUs by the millions; NVIDIA launched a mining line in 2017 to protect gaming supply. Mining revenue halved in the Q4 2018 crypto winter.

  8. 2022–24 · p. 6

    Generative AI

    ChatGPT launched in November 2022. OpenAI said training GPT-4 took 25,000 NVIDIA GPUs and $100 million; NVIDIA’s were the only chips able to train such models, and most developers used CUDA.

What the path teaches about applying technology

Hardware arrives first

Chip performance often arrives before the applications that can use it, and NVIDIA’s chips were among the most expensive to make (case, p. 4). CUDA was Huang’s answer: hand developers the tools to invent the uses. The technology choice was a market-creation choice.

Strategic patience

The professor’s teaching script calls innovation a marathon: invest in foundations before the killer app is obvious. CUDA had falling downloads for three years, and customers adopted deep learning slowly from 2012 to 2020 (p. 5). The bet survived on gaming and crypto revenue.

Serendipity, then commitment

AlexNet came from academics and crypto from miners; neither was NVIDIA’s plan. What NVIDIA chose was to commit once the signal came, which Session 1’s Mintzberg would call an emergent strategy made deliberate.

Where NVIDIA should sit in the value chain (complete DGX systems, the DGX Cloud rental business, the Mellanox purchase, the abandoned Arm deal and its dependence on TSMC) is a question of corporate scope, and it belongs to Session 10.

Platforms and ecosystems · case pp. 4, 11–12 · Rothaermel, Grant

CUDA: how a chip became a platform

A chip on its own is a pipeline product: design it, have it made, sell it. A platform, in Rothaermel’s definition, creates value by matching external producers and consumers on infrastructure the platform owner manages (p. 282). CUDA moved NVIDIA from the first to the second. It began in 2006 as a programming language, then NVIDIA let outside developers write programs for it and share them with other developers, with the development kits free (case, p. 4). By 2022 Huang counted over 150 kits serving fields from gaming and design to life sciences, quantum computing, AI, cybersecurity, 5G and robotics (p. 4).

The CUDA ecosystem, mapped onto Rothaermel’s four roles (p. 283)

Owner

NVIDIA. CUDA is proprietary: software written in it runs only on NVIDIA GPUs (case, p. 4).

Producers

Developers and researchers who write and share CUDA programs, like the AlexNet team (pp. 4–5).

Consumers

Buyers of accelerated computing: gamers, scientists, miners, then AI labs and data centres (pp. 3–6).

Providers

The cloud companies that rent NVIDIA GPUs to others, profitably, while designing their own chips (p. 1).

The professor’s flywheel, checked against the case

  1. 1GPUs process in parallel, which suits graphics and, it turned out, neural networks (pp. 3, 5).
  2. 2CUDA and its free kits make that power usable by developers (p. 4).
  3. 3Shared programs and libraries give the next developer more reason to build on NVIDIA (p. 4).
  4. 4Demand and premium prices fund R&D for the next GPU: $8.6 billion in 2023 (p. 6). Back to 1.

The teaching script calls this a walled garden. The case gives no count of CUDA developers, so this page does not either.

Grant, p. 234

Network externalities

A product is worth more to each user as more people use it. Two sources fit CUDA: complementary products (the libraries others write) and saved switching costs (code and skills that already work). Positive feedback can tip a market to one winner, and collective lock-in keeps a standard in place even after better ones appear.

Grant, p. 236

Open to grow, closed to earn

Platform owners trade market penetration against appropriation. JVC licensed VHS and beat Sony’s closed Betamax; IBM’s open PC won the market while Intel and Microsoft took the value. NVIDIA’s answer was to give the software away and lock it to the hardware it sells.

Kim & Mauborgne, p. 20

The eBay barrier

The more customers eBay had, the more attractive it became to buyers and sellers alike, leaving users little reason to go elsewhere. That is the blue-ocean barrier CUDA most resembles, and the one rivals in 2024 were spending most to break.

Moat or swamp? The 2024 attack on CUDA

The moat

Customers worried that code written in CUDA could not move to other chips. An AWS vice-president put the problem plainly: “Nvidia’s got great chips, and more importantly, they have an incredible ecosystem,” which made it very hard to get customers onto other chips (p. 11). AMD’s open alternative, ROCm, launched in 2016, never matched CUDA’s popularity (pp. 11–12).

The swamp

OpenAI’s open-source Triton (2021) lets researchers without CUDA experience write efficient GPU code that runs on many makers’ chips; by 2024 Microsoft, Meta and Google were investing in it. Intel’s CTO: “Essentially it breaks the CUDA lock-in.” Tenstorrent’s Jim Keller called CUDA “a swamp, not a moat,” too complicated, and predicted a UNIX-to-Linux ending (p. 11).

Notice who is backing Triton: three of NVIDIA’s largest customers. A platform’s complementors and buyers can also be its most motivated attackers, which is Rothaermel’s innovation-ecosystem point seen from the other side.

The case · demand and competition, pp. 4–12

AI demand, rivals and custom chips

Kim and Mauborgne expect a blue-ocean creator to go 10 to 15 years before a credible challenge (2004, p. 19). CUDA launched in 2006 (case, p. 4). By 2024 the challengers had arrived, and they were not all attacking the same thing.

Demand: how big, and for what?

  • Size. Some analysts saw a $1 trillion opportunity for chip makers over a decade; others feared a telecom-style overbuild, with no killer app yet and a query costing up to seven times a Google search (pp. 7–8).
  • Training or inference. GPUs were essential for training, with no good alternative in 2024. For inference there were several, often with lower total cost of ownership (p. 8).
  • Smaller models. Meta’s open-source Llama came in sizes from 8 billion parameters; Apple ran a small model on the iPhone and trained on Google’s TPUs, not NVIDIA’s GPUs (p. 8).
  • Power. One NVIDIA GPU could draw 1,000 watts or more, and analysts called power “the ultimate constraint” (p. 4).

Direct rivals: AMD and Intel

  • AMD. Its MI250X delivered about 380 teraflops against up to 1,000 for the H100, and sold for $8,000–10,000 against $25,000–40,000. It raised its 2024 data-centre GPU forecast from $4.0 to $4.5 billion but could get only a few hundred thousand GPUs from TSMC (p. 9).
  • Intel. Habana’s Gaudi2, bought for $2 billion in 2019, was priced near half an H100, but trailed on performance and its software was less mature than CUDA (p. 9).
  • Start-ups. Cerebras claimed inference 20 times faster at a fifth of the cost per token; Groq’s chips were fast but specialised. Venture capital put $6 billion into AI chip firms in 2023 (pp. 10–11).

Customer-competitors

  • Google. TPUs are chips designed specifically for neural networks. TPU v4 drew 200–250 watts against 250–400 for NVIDIA’s V100 and A100, and cost Google $2,000–3,000 each against about $15,000 for an NVIDIA chip (p. 10).
  • Amazon. Trainium trains models for firms such as Anthropic; Inferentia runs inference for Airbnb and ByteDance. Amazon claims up to 40–50% better price-performance and wants to cut the “Nvidia tax” (p. 10).
  • Concentration. Nearly half of NVIDIA’s revenue came from four large data-centre customers (p. 6).

          The verdict · Kim & Mauborgne against the case

          Did NVIDIA create a blue ocean, and is it turning red?

          Apply the reading’s tests, not the share price. A blue ocean creates new demand, makes rivals irrelevant and breaks the value–cost trade-off for buyers and the firm together. The case supports both sides.

          The case for a blue ocean

          • New buyers, not stolen ones. Scientists in the biosciences, computing and chemistry found NVIDIA GPUs did calculations in a fraction of the time of standard scientific computers (case, p. 5). They were not graphics customers. Cirque, too, won people who had never bought circus tickets (2004, p. 2).
          • Technology linked to value. Parallel processing already existed in gaming GPUs (p. 3). CUDA was what let buyers use it for their own problems (p. 4): value pioneering in the authors’ sense, not technology for its own sake (2004, pp. 11–12).
          • Uncontested for years. Rivals were slow to follow: Intel cancelled its high-end graphics chip after four years of work, and AMD’s answer to CUDA arrived only in 2016 (pp. 9, 11). In 2023 NVIDIA held an estimated 98% of the data-centre market (p. 6).

          The case against

          • No low-cost half. Buyers got a leap in performance at “outsized premiums” (p. 1): an H100 estimated to cost $3,100 sold for $25,000–40,000 (Exhibit 3). Customers call the difference the “Nvidia tax” (p. 10). The Model T took share by pricing against the horse.
          • Porter’s exemption, not a new logic. Being low-cost and differentiated at once lasts only while a firm is the sole owner of the innovation (Porter, 1985, p. 20). By 2024 rivals had chips, and Triton was going after the software (pp. 9–11).
          • Racing on the old factors. NVIDIA was moving to annual launches; Blackwell promised 2.5 times the performance for 25% more cost (p. 6). That is Compaq racing IBM on features, the convergence trap (Reader, pp. 14–15).

          Where this leaves NVIDIA

          The fairest reading of the case is that NVIDIA opened a blue ocean with CUDA and the deep-learning bet, priced it as the only supplier, and by 2024 was defending it with red-ocean weapons: speed, performance per dollar and lock-in. The authors would say the signal to value-innovate again is a curve that rivals are starting to copy. “Value innovation is about offering unprecedented value, not technology or competencies” (Kim & Mauborgne, 2017, p. 15). What the next move should be, and how far up the value chain NVIDIA should go to find it, is where Session 10 begins.

          Readings for Session 9

          The reading and the case are copyrighted Harvard Business School Publishing materials, and the textbooks are in copyright. Get them through the licensed course pack or the library; this page does not host or link them.

          Required · the reading

          Kim, W. C., & Mauborgne, R. (2004, October). Blue ocean strategy. Harvard Business Review (Reprint R0410D).

          Page numbers on this page follow the 23-page reprint in the course pack. Read it all; pages 11–21 carry the session.

          Required · the case

          Yoffie, D. B., & von Bargen, S. (2024). Nvidia, Inc. in 2024 and the future of AI (HBS No. 9-725-360). Harvard Business School Publishing.

          Pages 1–12 and Exhibits 1a, 3 and 7. The same case returns in Session 10 for NVIDIA’s corporate scope.

          Further · the tools

          Kim, W. C., & Mauborgne, R. (2017). The W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne blue ocean strategy reader. Harvard Business Review Press.

          “Value innovation: The strategic logic of high growth” (first published 1997), pp. 1–21, for the value curve and the four questions; “Charting your company’s future” (2002), pp. 93–107, for the strategy canvas.

          Further · course textbook

          Rothaermel, F. T. (2024). Strategic management (6th ed.). McGraw Hill.

          Chapter 7, sections 7.4–7.5, pp. 274–286, on types of innovation and platform strategy; chapter 6, section 6.5, pp. 231–235, on blue oceans and how they go wrong.

          Further · course textbook

          Grant, R. M. (2019). Contemporary strategy analysis (10th ed.). Wiley.

          Chapter 9, “Technology-based industries and the management of innovation”: capturing value from innovation, pp. 222–225, and standards, platforms and network externalities, pp. 232–236.

          Carried forward

          Porter, M. E. (1985). Competitive advantage: Creating and sustaining superior performance. Free Press.

          Chapter 1, p. 20: the passage on pioneering a major innovation, which Session 8 read with the rest of the chapter.
