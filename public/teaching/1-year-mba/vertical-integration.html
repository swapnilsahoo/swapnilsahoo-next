Vertical Integration

How many stages of the chain to own, and what owning them buys

Session 10 asked where a firm’s boundaries should sit. This session takes one of the three dimensions of corporate strategy, the industry value chain, and asks how many of its stages a firm should own. Vertical integration is ownership of the inputs a firm needs or of the channels that carry its output to customers (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 311). It can lower costs, raise quality and secure channels. It can also raise costs, lower quality and lock the firm in (pp. 315–316).

Amazon began in 1995 by selling books online. By 2024 it produced films, sold reading devices and voice assistants, designed AI chips, promised two-hour delivery in some cities, ran grocery stores and rented computing to other companies (case, pp. 1–3). Some of those moves are vertical and some are not. Today you sort them, weigh them, and test Osegowitsch and Madhok’s (2003) claim that firms now integrate forward mainly to learn from their customers.

Carried forward · Session 10

From the boundary of the firm to the stages of one chain

Session 10 built the tools: Porter’s tests for whether a business belongs in the company, and transaction costs for whether an activity belongs inside the firm. Today narrows the second tool to one question. Along a single industry’s value chain, which stages should the firm do itself?

Session 10 · transaction costs

Make when the hierarchy is cheaper

If an activity costs less to organise inside the firm than to buy through the market, the firm should integrate it; between the two ends sit contracts and alliances (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 303, 306). Today that rule is applied stage by stage, with the specific benefits and risks the textbook lists for vertical moves.

Session 10 · the three dimensions

One axis at a time

Corporate strategy sets the firm’s boundaries along vertical integration, horizontal diversification and geographic scope (p. 298). This session takes the vertical axis. Session 12 takes geography and Session 13 takes diversification, both with the same Amazon case.

Sessions 9 and 10 · NVIDIA

A firm that chose not to integrate

Session 10 framed NVIDIA as a designer that does not make its chips: it keeps design and sales and outsources manufacturing, mostly to TSMC. That avoided the capital of a fab but gave it less control of its supply. Amazon has the opposite habit. It keeps building stages for itself.

The trap this session sets

Not every move into something new is vertical. A move is vertical only if it takes the firm into a stage that supplies its existing business or carries that business’s output to customers. Entering a neighbouring industry, such as advertising or healthcare, is diversification, which is Session 13’s subject. Even the textbook counts AWS as both a backward vertical move and an example of horizontal diversification (pp. 297, 301). Keep the two questions apart before you judge any move.

Vertical integration along the industry value chain · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 310–314

Upstream, downstream, and how far a firm reaches

The industry value chain, also called the vertical value chain, is the sequence of stages that turns raw materials into finished goods and services. Each stage is usually a separate industry with its own competitors, so a firm that integrates into a new stage takes on a new set of rivals (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 311–312). The degree of integration can be measured by value added: the share of a firm’s sales generated inside its own boundaries, which tends to track the number of stages it works in (p. 311).

Degrees of integration: from grape to glass, or design only

Few firms are fully integrated. Most concentrate on a few stages and some on just one (p. 312). The textbook’s three examples span the range.

Fully integrated · p. 312

E&J Gallo Winery

Owns its vineyards, bottling plants and distribution and logistics network, and sells online where the law allows. Its value added approaches 100%. Gallo cites scale economies, coordinated scheduling and better control of quality. The price is a different set of competitors at every stage.

Largely disintegrated · p. 312

Apple

Concentrates on design, marketing and retailing, and outsources everything else. The textbook adds that the stages differ in profit potential: Apple captures about 95% of the value created with an iPhone and Foxconn, which assembles it, about 5% (p. 314).

Moving in both directions · p. 313

HTC

Began as a no-name contract manufacturer, then integrated backward into designing phones and forward into branded sales. It learned the adjacent skills from the brand owners it built for, which had to share their technology to get phones made at scale.

Analysis

Where is Amazon on its own chain?

Backward and forward only mean something once you fix the firm’s home stage. Amazon started as an online bookseller with a virtual store, unlimited inventory and low fixed costs (case, p. 1). This page takes retail, the online store, as its home stage. Upstream of the store sit the things it sells and the inputs it runs on: goods, films and shows, computing. Downstream sit the steps that get a purchase to the customer and the devices through which the customer buys.

Fix a different home stage and the labels change. That is why two careful readers can call the same Amazon device backward or forward, as the next activity shows.

Two questions to keep apart

  1. 1. Is the move vertical? Does it take the firm into a stage that feeds its existing business or carries that business’s output to customers?
  2. 2. Is it a good move? Do the benefits beat the risks, and beat what a contract, an alliance or a partial stake would deliver?

The first question is classification. The second is strategy, and it is the subject of the rest of the session.

Classify before you judge · case pp. 1–3; Rothaermel (2024), pp. 297, 300–301

Eleven Amazon moves: backward, forward, or not vertical?

Jassy’s question in the case is whether Amazon should keep going into new businesses or focus on a few core ones (case, p. 1). Before anyone can answer it, the moves have to be sorted. Some move Amazon along its own value chain; others take it into new industries, which is diversification and belongs to Session 13. Taking the online store as Amazon’s home stage, classify each move. Feedback gives the page’s reading and its source; three items have two defensible answers.

        What the sort shows

        Most of Amazon’s vertical moves run forward, toward the customer: delivery, and devices that sit in the customer’s hand or home. That is the pattern Osegowitsch and Madhok described in 2003, when they observed that forward integration was far more prevalent (p. 31).

        What it does not show

        Classification says nothing about value. The Fire Phone and the Echo are both forward moves into devices; one was written down within months and the other sold over 500 million units (case, p. 2). The rest of the session is about telling such moves apart in advance.

        Where the labels blur

        AWS, Kindle and Whole Foods each have two readings. In each case the disagreement is about which stage is Amazon’s home, or about whether the move serves the store or starts a new business. Say which reading you use before you argue about value.

        Benefits and risks of vertical integration · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 315–317

        Five things integration can buy, four ways it can cost

        The textbook asks leaders to weigh the benefits against countervailing risks at the same time, and warns that the risks can outweigh the benefits (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 315–316). Several risks are the mirror image of a benefit: integration can lower costs or raise them, improve quality or reduce it. Which one you get depends on the conditions, not on the move.

        Benefits · p. 315

        1. 1. Lowering costs

          Gallo’s full integration gives it scale economies and efficiency from coordinating its stages (p. 312).

        2. 2. Improving quality

          HTC bought One & Co., a San Francisco design firm, to secure scarce design talent and make better phones (p. 315). Gallo says integration lets it control quality (p. 312).

        3. 3. Facilitating scheduling and planning

          Forward integration into sales lets a firm plan for and respond to changes in demand; HTC sells to carriers and unlocked phones on its own site (p. 315).

        4. 4. Facilitating investments in specialised assets

          Assets worth far more in their intended use than in the next best: site-specific (bauxite mining next to aluminium smelting), physical-asset-specific (moulds for Gallo’s trademarked bottles) or human-asset-specific (skills in one organisation’s routines). Such investments invite opportunism; backward integration is often the answer (pp. 315–316).

        5. 5. Securing critical supplies and distribution channels

          Delta bought an oil refinery from ConocoPhillips in 2012. It estimates the refinery supplies 80% of its fuel and saves about $300 million a year; fuel is some 40% of its operating cost (p. 316).

        Risks · p. 316

        1. 1. Increasing costs

          An in-house supplier always has a buyer, so it has less reason to cut costs. Outside suppliers serve a larger market and reach scale the captive unit cannot. Complexity adds administrative costs, such as setting transfer prices between in-house supplier and buyer.

        2. 2. Reducing quality

          The same guaranteed buyer weakens the incentive to improve quality or innovate. Outside suppliers, with more customers, learn faster.

        3. 3. Reducing flexibility

          When steelmaking technology changed, integrated mills such as U.S. Steel and Bethlehem Steel were tied to their models and could not switch; many went bankrupt, while non-integrated mini-mills such as Nucor and Chaparral took share.

        4. 4. Increasing the potential for legal repercussions

          US regulators tend to allow vertical integration because it usually makes firms more efficient, but monopoly concerns mean it has not gone unchallenged.

        The common thread

        The first two risks come from removing market discipline: a captive unit no longer has to win its buyer. The third comes from commitment: assets that suit today’s technology can trap the firm when it changes. The fourth comes from the market power that controlling several stages can bring.

        When does vertical integration make sense?

        The textbook reads the question through history, then gives one main reason to integrate and one warning (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 317).

        Ford · p. 317

        Owning the whole chain

        Short of materials and parts, Ford came to run mines, rubber plantations, freighters, blast furnaces and glassworks. At River Rouge, raw materials went in at one end and cars came out the other. Over time the costs undid the savings and cost Ford its flexibility.

        Waves · p. 317

        In, out, and back in

        In the 1970s Texas Instruments moved downstream into watches and calculators, while Bowmar tried to move upstream into chips and failed. In 2009 GM tried to reacquire Delphi, a parts maker it had sold in 1997. PepsiCo and Coca-Cola bought bottling plants in the 2010s and later divested them.

        The main reason · p. 317

        Vertical market failure

        McKinsey advised clients that the main reason to integrate is that transactions along the chain are too risky and the alternatives to integration too costly or hard to administer. The textbook notes that this is the same answer transaction cost economics gives.

        The warning · p. 317

        Distance from the core

        Research summarised by The Economist suggests that a company that integrates two or more steps away from its core competency fails two-thirds of the time. Few clothing makers want to own textile mills, with their pollution risks and slim profits.

        “An innovator who can figure out how to eliminate annoyances and poor interfaces in the chain can build an incredible advantage, based on the customers’ desire for that unique solution.” Rita McGrath, quoted in Rothaermel (2024, p. 317), on why integration tempts firms that want to change the customer’s whole experience
        Analysis

        Integration tends to create value when…

        • the market for the stage fails: specialised assets, few alternative partners, and contracts that cannot specify or enforce what matters (pp. 305, 317);
        • the stage sits next to a core competence, one step away rather than two (p. 317);
        • owning it yields coordination or learning a contract cannot carry, which is Osegowitsch and Madhok’s argument for the customer interface (2003, p. 32).
        Analysis

        …and tends to destroy it when

        • the stage is served by competitive, contractible suppliers who have more scale and learn faster than a captive unit would (p. 316);
        • technology or demand is volatile, so a fixed commitment becomes a trap (p. 316; Osegowitsch & Madhok, 2003, p. 27);
        • the in-house unit is shielded from comparison with the market, so nobody can tell whether it is still competitive (pp. 316, 318).

        The two lists are this page’s synthesis of the cited pages, not a checklist from either source. The textbook’s own summary sets the same benefits as sources of value and the same risks as sources of cost (Exhibit 8.14, p. 330).

        Why firms still integrate · Osegowitsch & Madhok (2003), pp. 25–33

        “Vertical integration is dead, or is it?”

        Osegowitsch and Madhok wrote against the mood of their time. Equity markets pushed firms to focus and outsource; the academic view was that integration shared the poor record of unrelated diversification; consultants urged firms to “unbundle” (2003, p. 25). Yet integration had proved resilient in pharmaceuticals, defence, utilities, computers and cars, and in services such as media and engineering. Their explanation: the old motives had weakened, and new ones, above all learning from customers, had taken their place (pp. 25–26).

        Traditional motive 1 · p. 26

        Strategic: power and position

        Integration to win the power play against actual and potential rivals:

        • foreclose input or output markets to competitors, or raise their costs;
        • cross-subsidise one stage from another to squeeze focused rivals;
        • raise barriers to entry;
        • keep proprietary knowledge from suppliers and customers who might turn competitor.

        Partial, “tapered” integration can be enough: it makes the threat of full integration credible and improves terms (pp. 26–27).

        Traditional motive 2 · p. 27

        Governance: transaction costs and agency

        Integration to cut exposure to opportunism where contracts fail. It pays under:

        • uncertain demand or prices;
        • uncertain quality of inputs or point-of-sale service;
        • poor coordination between stages;
        • failure in markets for knowledge and technology;
        • a partner’s performance that is hard to specify or measure.

        Contracting problems become acute under small-numbers bargaining, which arises when assets are specific to the transaction (p. 27).

        The case against · pp. 27–28

        Commitment in a turbulent world

        Critics point to higher performance risk where technology is volatile and demand uncertain. Integration can be a premature commitment, costly to undo when exit barriers are high. It also loses market incentives, raises production costs and breeds bureaucracy and loss of focus. On flexibility grounds, the critics say, firms should form alliances instead.

        Five contemporary motives

        From observation across many industries, the authors list the motives they saw driving integration at the time (pp. 28–31). Most of their examples move downstream.

        01 · p. 28

        Value migration

        With large installed bases, value shifts from making products to servicing them. GE’s energy division stopped seeing itself in a $12 billion turbine market and started competing for $40 billion of utilities’ operating and maintenance spending.

        02 · pp. 28–29

        Differentiation

        When the core product is good enough for most buyers, firms add downstream services and manage the whole brand. Ford and GM’s bids to enter car retailing aimed partly to remove the unpleasant dealer experience.

        03 · p. 29

        Integrated solutions

        A pull from customers who now outsource and want one supplier for the whole job. Bechtel and Fluor Daniel widened into finance, design, construction and even plant operation.

        04 · pp. 29–30

        Synergies

        Close links between stages: feedback from marketing to product development, suppliers inside customers’ inventory planning, design-build firms whose builders advise designers.

        05 · pp. 30–31

        Emerging industries

        A pioneer may lack the credibility to attract suppliers or customers, as early Ford did, or may need to own complementary stages to make a system work, as Time Warner did by owning both films and cable.

        Why the old motives weakened · pp. 31–32

        Most upstream benefits can now be had without ownership

        • Scheduling: just-in-time supply, vendor-managed inventory and electronic links to the point of sale optimise production across firms as easily as within one.
        • Co-location: savings once credited to integration came from putting stages side by side, which independent suppliers and buyers now do.
        • Technology markets: brokers, consultants and experience with alliances have eased the failures of buying technology from outsiders.
        • The Internet: it is cheaper to search for, compare and switch suppliers or distributors.
        • Globalisation: converging industries and abundant capital erode entry barriers; liberalised trade makes foreclosure futile in many industries.

        Benetton and Nike, they note, achieved tight integration with suppliers without owning them (p. 32).

        The thesis · p. 32

        Downstream integration is about learning, and learning cannot be outsourced

        Data such as the level of demand are easy to collect. Knowledge of why customers value an offering, how best to provide it and what they will want next comes only from close learning relationships with them. Much of that knowledge is tacit and contextual, so it cannot be delegated to channel partners and fed back in a spreadsheet.

        “It is this ‘untradeability’ of learning that explains the vitality of downstream VI.” Osegowitsch & Madhok (2003, p. 32)

        So the right questions about scope become: do the current vertical boundaries let the firm acquire and process knowledge, and capture its value? If not, would extending them help?

        The Internet prediction · p. 26

        Some predicted that the Net would break firms up by cutting transaction costs. The authors saw a contingent picture instead: some industries de-integrated, others re-integrated, and the Net had helped broad-based incumbents, the “clicks-and-bricks” operators, more than the pure plays.

        Their caveats · p. 33

        Integrating firms must keep strong market incentives and flexibility. Where they taper, they must keep existing buyers and suppliers on side. A partial move into retailing may capture the learning without abandoning independent dealers (note 2).

        Amazon, in 2003 · p. 33

        In a footnote the authors already name Amazon, with Charles Schwab, as a portal that could find out within hours whether customers accepted new offers, prices or page designs (note 3). The customer interface was the asset they had in mind.

        Analysis

        Two lenses that do not always agree

        The textbook’s checklist asks whether integration lowers costs, raises quality, secures channels and protects specific investments, against higher costs, lost flexibility and legal exposure. It is a governance lens: integrate where the market fails.

        Osegowitsch and Madhok add a reason the checklist barely names: owning the customer interface to learn. On their lens a forward move can be worth making even when a contract would deliver the goods more cheaply. Hold both when you reach Amazon’s devices: the Kindle, the Fire Phone and the Echo were all bids for the customer interface.

        Alternatives to vertical integration · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 306–310, 318–319

        Between owning a stage and buying from it

        Firms would like the benefits of integration without its risks. The textbook names two alternatives, taper integration and strategic outsourcing (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 318). The hybrids on Session 10’s make-or-buy continuum, from long-term contracts to joint ventures, are the third family.

        Taper integration · p. 318

        Make some, buy the rest

        The firm is integrated backward but also buys some supplies outside, or integrated forward but also sells through outside distributors. Apple and Nike own retail outlets and also sell through other retailers, physical and online.

        • In-house units face market comparison, and the firm keeps its competencies rather than hollowing them out.
        • Flexibility: when demand falls, cut deliveries to outside retailers and keep stocking your own stores.
        • Internal and external knowledge combine, which can lead to innovation.

        In a study of 3,500 product introductions in the computer industry, firms that tapered outperformed both the more integrated and the more outsourced on innovation and financial results.

        Strategic outsourcing · pp. 318–319

        Move an activity out

        Moving one or more value chain activities outside the firm to other firms in the industry value chain. It lowers the degree of integration. Firms hand human-resource systems to specialists that have deep competencies and scale.

        Outsourcing to another country is offshoring: Infosys serves many Fortune 100 firms from Bangalore, and US law firms send routine legal work to India. Where to locate activities is Session 12’s question.

        Contracts and alliances · pp. 306–309

        Commit without owning

        Short-term contracts give suppliers no reason to invest in specific assets. Long-term contracts such as licensing, equity alliances and joint ventures support those investments without the internal costs of ownership (pp. 306–307). An equity stake is a credible commitment: difficult and costly to reverse (p. 309).

        Osegowitsch and Madhok add that partial integration can make the threat of full integration credible and win better terms (2003, pp. 26–27).

        The case · Gupta & Rodriguez (2024)

        Amazon in 2024: the vertical moves, one at a time

        Amazon’s revenue grew 12% in 2023, from $514 billion to $575 billion (case, p. 1). Andy Jassy, CEO since 2021, has to decide whether to keep entering seemingly unrelated businesses or focus on a few core ones (p. 1). The case closes on the charge that Amazon was “defying the basic principle of business – focus” (p. 4). This session answers only part of that charge: the moves along Amazon’s own value chain. Whether the portfolio as a whole is spread too thin is Session 13’s question, and its international reach is Session 12’s.

        Forward Case · pp. 1–3

        Fulfilment and delivery: the promise behind Prime

        • Prime launched in 2005: free two-day shipping for a $79 annual fee. The fee rose to $99 in 2014, $119 in 2018 and $139 in 2022, and membership kept growing (pp. 1–2).
        • By 2023 Amazon had more than 200 million Prime members, who spent more than four times what other customers spent (p. 2).
        • Prime Now offered free delivery of tens of thousands of daily essentials within two hours, in several US cities (p. 2). Amazon Business added 24-hour delivery to business buyers (p. 3).

        Textbook To deliver millions of products in two days or less in the United States, Amazon built a large network of fulfilment centres: a large-scale commitment that lets it exploit economies of scope (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 302).

        Analysis This is forward integration one step from the store, into the stage that carries a purchase to the door. The benefits that apply are quality the customer sees (speed), scheduling stock against delivery, and a secured channel (p. 315). The risk is fixed facilities that cannot shrink if demand falls (p. 316).

        Where the case is silent. It does not say who carries parcels the last mile or what delivery costs Amazon. Question: if Amazon owned that last stage too, which benefits would grow, and which risks?

        Forward, contested Case · p. 2

        Devices: Kindle, Fire Phone, Echo

        • Kindle launched in 2007 at $399 and sold out in under six hours; after the iPad arrived in 2010, its price fell to about $100. It gave customers a convenient way to shop for and download books and movies from Amazon’s store.
        • The Fire Phone, July 2014, had a “Firefly” button that identified objects to buy on Amazon. Three months later Amazon announced a $170 million write-down.
        • Echo’s assistant, Alexa, answered questions and placed orders. Over 500 million Echo devices were sold by May 2023, yet the Wall Street Journal reported in 2022 that the devices unit had an operating loss of more than $5 billion a year.
        • The Dash Button, for reordering household items, was discontinued in 2019 for weak demand.

        Case · p. 2 Amazon also built an AppStore that rivalled Google’s and Apple’s. While the devices unit was reportedly under review in 2022, a spokesperson said Amazon was “as optimistic about Alexa’s future today as we’ve ever been”, and Amazon planned to bring generative AI to Alexa.

        Analysis Every device is a bid for the customer interface, which is Osegowitsch and Madhok’s strongest case for integrating forward (2003, p. 32), and for a sales channel Amazon controls (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 315). The Fire Phone shows the limit: owning the interface did not make customers buy the phone. The case reports disappointing sales and is silent on why.

        Question. Kindle’s price fell from $399 to about $100, and Alexa places orders on Amazon. Should a device be judged on its own loss, or on what customers buy through it? What evidence would settle it?

        Backward, then outward Case · pp. 1, 3–4

        Computing: AWS and its own chips

        • AWS launched in 2002, offering companies elastic IT infrastructure that could grow at low cost as needed. Bezos kept investing although analysts questioned it. In 2023 AWS earned over $90 billion in revenue and over $24 billion in operating income, in a fast-growing but highly competitive market (p. 1).
        • For generative AI, Jassy described a stack of three layers, “each of which is gigantic, and each of which we’re deeply investing”: custom chips for training and inference at the bottom, the Bedrock managed service in the middle, and applications such as the Rufus shopping assistant and Alexa on top (pp. 3–4).

        Textbook Rothaermel counts the creation of AWS as a backward integration move (p. 301) and also cites AWS as an example of horizontal diversification (p. 297). The two decks under Go deeper describe AWS as having started as Amazon’s internal IT; the case itself does not say how AWS began.

        Analysis On the backward reading, Amazon owns the computing its own businesses run on. Selling that computing to other firms made the input a business in a new industry, which is diversification and goes to Session 13. The chips repeat the pattern one stage further up, a backward move by the cloud business itself. Contrast NVIDIA in Session 10, which designs chips but leaves fabrication to others; the case does not say who makes Amazon’s chips.

        Question. Does selling to outside customers make the in-house unit stronger, because it must match the market (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 318), or does it pull the unit away from serving Amazon?

        Backward, tapered Case · pp. 1–2

        Content: Amazon Studios behind Prime Video

        • Amazon launched Unbox for instant video in 2006; Prime Video later offered a large selection of films and shows. Amazon Studios started in 2011 to produce original content, while licensing deals brought in Viacom shows and, in 2014, select HBO shows for over $200 million (p. 2).
        • In 2014 Amazon also launched Prime Music, ad-free music streaming for Prime subscribers (p. 1).
        • In 2018 the Studios earned 10 Golden Globe nominations and two wins (p. 2).
        • In 2023 Amazon spent over $19 billion on TV and music content, including licensing, against Netflix’s $13 billion, as the streaming war intensified (p. 2).

        Analysis Tapered backward integration: Amazon makes some of the content its video service needs and licenses the rest (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 318). The risk is the textbook’s first, higher costs (p. 316), here in a bidding war for content.

        Where the case is silent. It reports content spending and awards but no profit for video, and it does not separate what content and what delivery each do for Prime. Question: should Studios be judged as a film studio, or by its effect on Prime membership and spending (pp. 1–2)? What evidence would tell them apart?

        Mostly not vertical Case · p. 3

        Whole Foods: a store, not a stage

        In June 2017 Amazon paid $13.4 billion, a 27% premium, for an upscale chain of 460 stores with high-income customers. Whole Foods had a 1.2% share of a US grocery market of about $800 billion, against Walmart’s 14.5%; AmazonFresh had achieved limited success, and Amazon had not passed a 0.2% share in ten years. Many experts expected the deal to help Amazon into grocery; others wondered whether a technology company with a soaring share price should enter so thin-margin an industry.

        Analysis

        Retail is the stage Amazon already occupies, so buying a grocer is mainly horizontal: a new category and a new format. The textbook offers the forward reading: Amazon’s physical stores, including those within Whole Foods, move it closer to the end customer (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 300–301).         What the case lets you conclude

        The case reports results for only one vertical stage, computing: AWS revenue and operating income (p. 1). For devices it gives a reported loss and a write-down; for content, only spending; for logistics, nothing. Most judgements of value here are inferences, and you should say so when you make them.

        Can a stage be judged on its own profit?

        Inside an integrated firm, what one stage earns depends partly on the transfer prices set between the in-house supplier and its buyer, which the textbook counts among the administrative costs of integration (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 316). Before you cite the devices loss (case, p. 2), decide which measure fits a captive stage: its own profit, or what the rest of the chain would lose without it.

        The pattern across moves

        Amazon’s forward moves reach for the customer: delivery and devices (case, pp. 1–2). Its backward moves secure inputs: content and chips (pp. 2–3). Owning a stage guaranteed nothing: the Fire Phone sold poorly and was written down within three months (p. 2).

        For class

        Six questions to argue

        Come with a position on each, and the page of the case or reading that supports it.

        01

        Is AWS backward integration, diversification, or both? The textbook says both (pp. 297, 301). Show how your evaluation of AWS changes depending on which reading you adopt, and say what you would need to know about how AWS began, which the case does not tell you.

        02

        The Fire Phone and the Echo were both forward moves into devices. What separated the one written down within three months from the one that sold over 500 million units? Could Amazon have told them apart in advance?

        03

        The devices unit reportedly lost more than $5 billion a year, yet Alexa places orders on Amazon and Kindle is a way to shop its store (case, p. 2). As Jassy, what evidence would you need before cutting or keeping devices?

        04

        Prime’s fee rose from $79 to $139 while membership kept growing. Is that evidence that integrating into fulfilment and delivery created value? What else could explain it?

        05

        Amazon makes some of its video content and licenses the rest. Using the benefits of taper integration, argue whether it should make more or less, given $19 billion of content spending in 2023.

        06

        Osegowitsch and Madhok say most upstream benefits can be had through closely aligned suppliers. Do Amazon’s own AI chips contradict them? Which benefit or motive would justify them, and what would you need to know that the case does not say?
