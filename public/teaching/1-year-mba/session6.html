Creating Competitive Advantage

Why some firms open a wider gap between what buyers will pay and what it costs to serve them

Session 5 took stock of what Patagonia has: its resources, its activities, a culture rivals find hard to copy. This session asks what those things are worth. A firm has a competitive advantage when it creates more value than its rivals: a wider wedge between what its customers are willing to pay and what its suppliers and employees need to be paid.

Patagonia is a hard test. For decades it made choices that looked expensive, from organic cotton to free repairs to giving away part of every sale, and it came to hold the largest share of US outdoor apparel. Your job is to say where on the wedge each choice landed, and whether it came from a change in the world or from Patagonia itself.

Five forces · Session 3

Industry structure decides who keeps the value

Porter’s forces are a theory of value capture. Powerful buyers keep more of the value an industry creates, powerful suppliers take more of it, rivalry and entry compete it away, and substitutes cap what buyers will pay (Porter, 1985/2004, p. 9). Today the question moves inside the industry: why does one firm open a wider wedge than its neighbours?

Resources · Session 5

An asset counts only if it moves a number

A culture, a brand or a supplier relationship is a source of advantage only if it raises customers’ willingness to pay, or lowers what it costs to serve them, by more than rivals manage. Session 5 asked whether Patagonia’s resources are rare and hard to copy. This session asks what they do to the wedge.

Value chain · Session 5

Activities are where the wedge is made

Willingness to pay and cost are both outputs of activities. As you read the case, trace each Patagonia choice to the activity it changed: procurement (organic cotton), service (free repairs), human resources (daycare and parental leave), firm infrastructure (the benefit-corporation charter).

The trap this session sets

Listing virtues is not analysis. “Patagonia has a strong brand and cares about the planet” says nothing until you say which point on the value stick it moves, against which rival, and what it cost to move it.

Identifying competitive advantage · the value stick

Competitive advantage is a wider wedge

Strategy writers define competitive advantage in three ways: by the profits it produces, by the profits it could produce, and by the value it creates. The three are compatible. The third is the one you can work with, because it tells you where the profits come from.

By outcome · Ghemawat & Rivkin

“A firm such as Nucor that earns superior financial returns within its industry (or its strategic group) over the long run is said to enjoy a competitive advantage over its rivals.”

Ghemawat & Rivkin (1999, p. 49), as quoted in Rumelt (2003, p. 2)

Note the qualifiers: within the industry, not against the economy; over the long run, not in one good year; and against rivals.

By potential · Grant

The potential to out-earn direct rivals

Grant defines it as a firm’s potential to earn a higher rate of profit than its direct competitors. He says potential because a firm may spend its advantage on market share, technology or customer loyalty instead of reporting it: Amazon made a net loss over 1998–2007 despite an obvious advantage in online retailing (Grant, 2019, p. 157).

By value created · Porter, Rothaermel

More value than rivals create

Porter roots advantage in value a firm creates for buyers that exceeds the cost of creating it, where value is what buyers are willing to pay (Porter, 1985/2004, p. 3). The course textbook compresses it to one line: a firm has a competitive advantage when it creates more economic value than its rivals do (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 186).

Four numbers on one stick

Hold a single jacket up against four numbers, from the top down. The distance from the top to the bottom is the value the transaction creates. Every party to it takes a slice.

TOP

Willingness to pay

The most a customer would pay for it. Rothaermel calls it the reservation price, set by the benefits the buyer perceives.

SECOND

Price

What the customer actually pays. It sits between cost and willingness to pay, or the sale does not happen.

THIRD

Cost

What the firm pays its suppliers and employees to make and sell the jacket.

BOTTOM

Supplier opportunity cost

The least those suppliers and employees would accept, given what else they could do with their materials, capacity and time.

Value created = willingness to pay − supplier opportunity cost. It splits three ways: customers keep willingness to pay minus price, the firm keeps price minus cost, and suppliers and employees keep cost minus their opportunity cost. A firm has a competitive advantage when its stick is longer than its rivals’. Rothaermel’s textbook version folds suppliers into cost and writes the same idea as value created = V − C, split into consumer surplus (V − P) and the firm’s profit (P − C) (2023, pp. 186–190).

Added value: the obituary test

What would disappear if the firm ceased to exist? Casadesus-Masanell, following Brandenburger and Nalebuff, suggests reading the newspaper a year after your death to see how the world has managed without you (2022, p. 9). A firm that customers, suppliers and complementors could replace easily has little added value, however large its sales. Brandenburger and Stuart (1996) show that what a firm can capture is limited by that added value (as summarised by Rumelt, 2003, p. 2).

Two ways to widen the wedge

Raise willingness to pay by more than you raise cost, or lower cost by more than you lower willingness to pay. Grant states the first condition plainly: the price premium a customer will pay must exceed the extra cost of being different (2019, p. 166). Session 8 develops both routes as generic strategies. Here you need only see that they are moves on the same stick.

Creating is not capturing

A firm can create a great deal of value and keep little of it. The key to capture is scarcity, and imitation and substitution erode scarcity (Casadesus-Masanell, 2022, p. 5). Price only moves value between customer and firm; it does not make the stick longer. Whether Patagonia can keep its slice once rivals notice is Session 7’s question.

External and internal sources · Grant (2019), ch. 7

Where the wedge comes from: change outside, innovation inside

Grant calls competitive advantage a disequilibrium phenomenon. It is created by change, and once it exists it sets off the competition that will wear it down (2019, p. 157). The change can start outside the firm or inside it. The figure below redraws his model of how advantage emerges.

External · the differential effect

A change creates advantage only if it hits firms differently

External change matters because firms differ. Grant’s example: by cutting subsidies for renewable energy, the US tax bill of December 2017 favoured fossil-fuel generators such as Duke Energy over wind and solar producers. The bigger the change and the more firms differ, the wider the spread of profits. The leading brewers face a stable market with similar resources, and their profits differ little. Toy companies are a mixed group facing unpredictable tastes, and their profits differ widely (2019, pp. 157–158).

External · responsiveness

Anticipation and agility

Every change creates an opportunity that goes to whoever exploits it best. Grant names two capabilities. Anticipation: IBM renewed its advantage by seeing major shifts in computing coming, where Hewlett-Packard was less adept. Agility: Zara’s integrated supply chain takes a garment from design to store in under three weeks, against an industry norm of three to six months (2019, p. 158).

Internal · innovation

New ways of creating and capturing value

Grant stresses strategic innovation over technology alone. He cites an IBM study’s three types of business model innovation: new industry models such as Dell’s direct sales, new revenue models such as Rolls-Royce’s “Power by the Hour” engine contracts, and new enterprise models such as the iPhone’s outsourced manufacture and network of app developers (2019, pp. 158–159).

Top of the stick · customers

Raising willingness to pay

  • A better product for the same need. The 1977 pile fleece was as warm as wool, lighter and more weather-resistant, and became iconic (p. 3).
  • Durability as a stated value. Patagonia defines its best product as useful, versatile, long-lasting, repairable and recyclable (Exhibit 2).
  • A product that is repaired, not replaced. Worn Wear repairs Patagonia clothing free; the Reno facility repaired 100,000 items a year, and returned items were resold or recycled (p. 4).
  • Values customers can see. From 1986 it gave the greater of 1% of sales or 10% of profit to environmental causes, nearly $200 million by fall 2020 (pp. 3, 6).

Middle of the stick · Patagonia

Choices that raised cost and risk

  • Organic cotton. A 1994 assessment found conventional cotton, grown on 2.5% of the world’s cultivated land, used 22.5% of chemical insecticides and 10% of agricultural pesticides. Chouinard moved all 166 cotton products to organic within eighteen months or would have stopped selling sportswear, then 30% of the business. Sales were back to their previous level within two years (p. 4).
  • Giving on every sale. The 1986 pledge meant at least 1% of sales went to environmental causes whatever the year’s profit (p. 3).
  • Growth held back. After laying off 20% of its 600 staff in the 1991 recession, Patagonia capped growth at pre-set targets (p. 3).

Bottom of the stick · employees and suppliers

Lowering the price of working with Patagonia

  • A job worth more than its pay. One of the first onsite corporate daycare centres in the US, paid maternity and paternity leave before they were common, daily surf forecasts and training in Yosemite (p. 3).
  • Stability. The growth cap of 1991 was meant to keep the workplace stable (p. 3), and a supportive work environment became a charter commitment in 2011 (p. 5).
  • Suppliers: the open question. Most products came from 45 suppliers in 40 factories in 16 countries (p. 2). The case does not say whether they deal with Patagonia on different terms from other brands.

Founder’s claim

“I know it sounds crazy, but every time I have made a decision that’s best for the planet, [Patagonia has] made money.” Yvon Chouinard, quoted in Trelstad et al. (2023, p. 3)

On the value stick this is a claim that each move raised willingness to pay, or lowered opportunity cost, by more than it raised cost. It is a founder’s summary, not a test. Ask which decisions it leaves out.

The other half of the bargain

“Because if you’re not successful financially, the experiment’s over.” Kris Tompkins, CEO from 1979, quoted in Trelstad et al. (2023, p. 3)

Creating value was never enough. Patagonia also had to capture a slice large enough to fund the giving, the repairs and the growth caps, which is why price and margin still matter in a purpose-led firm.

What the case cannot prove

There are no prices, unit costs, rival margins or turnover figures in the case. What it offers is a pattern: choices that looked costly, followed by growth from about $20 million in sales to about $1 billion and the largest share of its US market. A pattern is not proof that the wedge widened. Decide what data would convince a sceptic, and which part of the stick each item would measure.

For class

Discussion questions

Come with a written answer to the first two. The others are for the room.

  1. 01

    In 1994 Chouinard put sportswear, 30% of the business, at risk to move to organic cotton. State, on the value stick, the condition under which the switch created advantage. What in the case suggests the condition was met, and what would you need to confirm it?

  2. 02

    Grant says external change creates advantage only when it hits firms differently. Which of Patagonia’s resources made the shift towards sustainability help it more than it helped a fast-fashion retailer?

  3. 03

    “Every time I have made a decision that’s best for the planet, [Patagonia has] made money.” Is that evidence of a wider wedge, or of survivorship? What observation would falsify it?

  4. 04

    Does Patagonia’s workplace lower the opportunity cost of working there, or raise the cost of employing people? If both, who captures the difference: the employee or the firm?

  5. 05

    Apply the obituary test. If Patagonia disappeared tomorrow, what would its customers, employees and suppliers lose that another brand could not replace?

  6. 06

    If a large rival matched organic cotton, free repairs and 1% giving next year, how much of Patagonia’s wedge would survive? Write your answer down and keep it for Session 7.
