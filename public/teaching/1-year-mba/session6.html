Shared Value and Competitive Advantage

Can a better outcome for society also strengthen the economics of the firm?

Firms in the same industry earn very different returns. Ghemawat and Rivkin trace the difference to one measure: the wedge a firm drives between what buyers are willing to pay and what its suppliers and staff must be paid to take part, compared with the wedge its rivals manage. This session asks how that wedge is created, which of its sources lie outside the firm and which inside, and when solving a problem for society widens the wedge instead of merely spending it.

Session 5 opened Patagonia up and took stock: its resources and capabilities tested with VRIO, its value chain, and Collis and Montgomery’s tests of what makes a resource valuable. Today puts a number on those things. A culture, a repair service or an organic-cotton supply chain is a source of advantage only if it moves willingness to pay or cost by more than a rival can. Patagonia, which in 2022 handed its ownership to a trust and a nonprofit to fight the environmental crisis, is a hard test of whether doing good and doing well are the same wedge.

Question 01 · Ghemawat & Rivkin (1998)

How does a firm create a competitive advantage?

Ghemawat and Rivkin open with two firms. Over 1984–2002 the drug maker Schering-Plough produced more than $10 billion of economic profit, its accounting profit beyond the cost of its equity; U.S. Steel produced an economic loss of nearly $500 million (1998, p. 1). Part of the gap is industry: patents, differentiated products and growing demand mute rivalry in drugs, while excess capacity and powerful unions squeeze steel. But Schering-Plough was not a typical drug maker, nor U.S. Steel a typical steel firm. Research they cite finds that industry accounts for 10–20% of the variation in business profitability and stable differences within industries for 30–45% (p. 1).

Sessions 3 and 4 explained the first slice. This session explains the second. Ghemawat and Rivkin give it a precise form: a firm has a competitive advantage when it drives a wider wedge than its rivals between the willingness to pay it generates among buyers and its costs. They add two themes. To create an advantage, a firm must do something unique and valuable, so that if it disappeared someone in its network of suppliers, customers and complementors would miss it and nobody could replace it perfectly. And the advantage usually comes from the full range of its activities acting in harmony, not from one of them (p. 3).

Four numbers on one stick

Ghemawat and Rivkin’s worked example is Harnischfeger’s portal crane, which lifted whole logs off railcars in a woodyard. Each crane replaced a forklift fleet costing about $1.0 million and saved $6.5 million of operating costs over its life, and cost Harnischfeger $2.5 million to build and install (pp. 4–5). Read the stick from the top down.

TOP · $7.5M

Willingness to pay

The most a buyer would part with. International Paper would pay up to $1.0M + $6.5M; beyond that the forklifts are cheaper (p. 5).

SECOND · ?

Price

Bargained anywhere between cost and willingness to pay. The theory says nothing about where; the tougher bargainer pulls it their way (p. 5).

THIRD · $2.5M

Cost

What Harnischfeger actually paid its suppliers and staff to deliver one crane.

BOTTOM · $2.0M

Supplier opportunity cost

The least suppliers would accept, set by their next-best use of the resources. The authors’ own guess for the example (p. 5).

Value created = willingness to pay − supplier opportunity cost: $7.5M − $2.0M = $5.5M per crane. Suppliers keep cost minus opportunity cost ($0.5M), Harnischfeger keeps price minus cost, and International Paper keeps willingness to pay minus price (p. 5). The textbook writes the same idea as V − C, folding suppliers into cost: consumer surplus (V − P) plus the firm’s profit (P − C) (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 186–190). In practice, managers analyse actual costs because the data exist, assuming the two track each other (Ghemawat & Rivkin, p. 7).

The trap this session sets

Listing virtues is not analysis. “Patagonia has a strong brand and cares about the planet” says nothing until you say which point on the stick it moves, against which rival, and what it cost to move it. And a high price is not a measure of willingness to pay, nor a fat margin proof that customers or suppliers received nothing.

Two ways to widen the wedge

Raise buyers’ willingness to pay without a commensurate rise in supplier opportunity cost, or cut supplier opportunity cost without giving up commensurate willingness to pay (Ghemawat & Rivkin, p. 7). Toyota costs more to build than Hyundai, and earns more because the extra willingness to pay exceeds the extra cost (pp. 7–8). The first route they call differentiation, the second low cost; a few firms, such as Japanese makers cutting defect rates, manage both at once (p. 8). Session 7 turns both into generic strategies.

“Differentiated” is not “different”

A differentiated firm has raised the price buyers are willing to pay for its output. Hyundai is different from Toyota but not differentiated with respect to it, and charging a lower price is not differentiation either: a firm’s price does not usually change what customers are intrinsically willing to pay (p. 8). Keep this in mind when someone calls Patagonia “differentiated by purpose”: the claim needs a willingness-to-pay number behind it.

Creating is not capturing

Price only moves value between buyer and firm; it does not lengthen the stick. What a firm can claim is capped by its added value, and added value comes from scarcity: being unique in some valuable way and not readily replaced (pp. 6–7). The textbook puts it as two tasks, creating economic value and capturing as much of it as possible; Amazon, it says, creates a great deal for customers and has chosen to capture little (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 189).

How would you know a firm had one?

Rumelt surveyed the strategy literature and found one common theme, value creation, with little agreement on value to whom and when (2003, p. 1). Three definitions recur: by the profits advantage produces, by the profits it could produce, and by the value it creates. They are compatible, and the third is the one you can work with, because it says where the profits come from.

By outcome

“A firm such as Nucor that earns superior financial returns within its industry (or its strategic group) over the long run is said to enjoy a competitive advantage over its rivals.”

Ghemawat & Rivkin (1999, p. 49), as quoted in Rumelt (2003, p. 2)

Note the qualifiers: within the industry, not against the economy; over the long run, not in one good year; against rivals. In the 1998 note’s steel chart, Nucor is one of only five producers that earned more than their cost of equity over 1984–2002; U.S. Steel did not (Figure 2b).

By potential · Grant

The potential to out-earn direct rivals

Grant says potential because a firm may spend its advantage on share, technology or loyalty instead of reporting it: Amazon made a net loss over 1998–2007 despite an obvious advantage in online retailing (Grant, 2019, p. 157).

By value created · Porter, Rothaermel

More economic value than rivals

Porter roots advantage in value created for buyers beyond the cost of creating it (1985/2004, p. 3). The textbook: a firm has a competitive advantage when it creates more economic value than rivals (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 186). Porter’s own slide puts the order plainly: shareholder value is the result of creating real economic value (Porter, 2017, slide 4).

Three yardsticks, three blind spots. Accounting ratios are backward-looking and miss intangibles; in 1975 more than 80% of the average S&P 500 firm’s value was book value, by 2020 about 10% (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 182–183). Total return to shareholders looks forward but is noisy, moved by macroeconomics and mood (pp. 184–186). Economic value is the cleanest idea and the hardest to measure: willingness to pay must be inferred, and it shifts with income, taste and time (pp. 191–192). For Patagonia, a private firm that does not share its financial data (case, p. 2), only the third is even partly available.

Activity analysis: where the wedge is made

The activities a firm performs to design, produce, sell, deliver and service its product are what incur cost and generate willingness to pay. Differences in what firms actually do day to day produce the differences in the wedge (p. 10). So the note breaks the firm down in four steps.

  1. STEP 1 · CATALOGUE

    List the activities, using the value chain: primary activities that make and deliver the good, support activities that make them possible (p. 10).

  2. STEP 2 · RELATIVE COST

    Cost each activity, find its cost drivers, and use the drivers you can observe to estimate a rival’s costs (pp. 10–12).

  3. STEP 3 · RELATIVE WTP

    Find the real buyer, what it wants and pays for, how well each rival meets those needs, and which activities explain the gaps (pp. 14–15).

  4. STEP 4 · OPTIONS

    Search for changes in activities that widen the wedge, and drop activities that add cost without commensurate willingness to pay (pp. 16–18).

Where willingness to pay comes from

Almost any activity can move it (p. 14): product design (New Balance sells durable shoes in hard-to-find sizes and advertises itself as “endorsed by no one”), delivery (Calyx & Corolla’s fresher, faster flowers), after-sale service (buyers hesitate over a Fiat for fear of spare parts), signals (Nike’s advertising) and support activities (Nordstrom’s hiring, training and pay create the helpful staff that let it charge a premium).

No firm has a “willingness-to-pay calculator”: the number depends on perceptions, on activities interacting, and on intermediaries (p. 14). Market research once “proved” that telephone answering machines would sell poorly (p. 16). Analysis should hone insight, not replace it.

The whole versus the parts

Choices interact, so the landscape of possible strategies is rugged, with many local peaks. Small changes rarely reach a higher peak, and more than one peak can be viable: Merrill Lynch (big city offices, full product range, national advertising) and Edward Jones (thousands of one-broker rural offices, conservative securities, door-to-door selling) both succeed, very differently (pp. 18–19).

The corporation as a tree

Prahalad and Hamel open with two firms that started the 1980s with comparable portfolios. In 1980 GTE had sales of $9.98 billion and NEC $3.8 billion; by 1988 GTE’s were $16.46 billion and NEC’s $21.89 billion (1990, p. 79). The difference, they argue, is that NEC saw itself as a portfolio of competencies and GTE as a portfolio of businesses. Early in the 1970s NEC set out to exploit the convergence of computing and communications, “C&C”, and had entered more than 100 alliances by 1987 to build the competencies it needed, above all in semiconductors (p. 80).

Core competencies are the collective learning in the organisation, especially how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technology (p. 81). Sony’s miniaturisation and Philips’s optical media are examples. Unlike physical assets, a competence is enhanced, not used up, by being applied and shared, though knowledge fades if it is not used (p. 81).

What a core competence is not (p. 83)

  • • Outspending rivals on R&D: Canon passed Xerox in world copier unit share in 1983 on a fraction of its reprographics R&D budget.
  • • Shared costs, such as two units using one plant or sales force.
  • • Vertical integration, which looks along the supply chain rather than forward to new applications of skills.

Run Prahalad and Hamel’s three tests on nine of the article’s own cases in question 3.

Core products, not just brand share

Canon reputedly held 84% of world manufacturing of desktop laser-printer engines with a minuscule brand share; Matsushita about 45% of key VCR components against a 20% brand share (p. 85). Selling core products to others funds and speeds the competence.

How competencies are lost

Chrysler treated engines as one more component; its outsourced engines rose from 252,000 to 382,000 between 1985 and 1987 (p. 84). Firms that left colour TV as “mature” also shut the door on video-based competencies (p. 84). Once off the train, it is hard to reboard (p. 85).

The tyranny of the SBU

When the firm is only a set of business units, no unit invests in core products, talent is “imprisoned” in the unit that grew it, and innovation stays bounded by existing lines (pp. 86–87). The remedy is a strategic architecture: a road map of which competencies to build (p. 87).

Where this meets Ghemawat and Rivkin

A competence is upstream of the stick. It earns its place only when it reaches an end product as higher willingness to pay or lower cost, and it is defensible only when rivals cannot assemble the same harmonised bundle. A competence nobody would miss has no added value. The article’s last line of defence is people: Canon moves critical engineers between its camera, copier and optical-products businesses so the competence travels with them (p. 90).

Reading task

Return to Session 5’s competence map. Choose one capability hypothesis for Patagonia and trace it through an activity to a customer benefit or a cost change. The existence of a useful routine does not tell you how much value it creates or who captures it. Then say whether reusing the capability in another product spreads learning, adds coordination costs, or both, and bring one reason the transfer could fail.

Shared value: a bigger pie, not a fairer slice

The course textbook titles this chapter “Shared Value and Competitive Advantage” and builds it on Porter and Kramer, who define shared value as “policies and operating practices that enhance the competitiveness of a company while simultaneously advancing the economic and social conditions in the communities in which it operates” (Porter & Kramer, 2011, p. 6; Rothaermel, 2024, p. 176).

The key word is creating. Shared value is not about personal values, and not about sharing value a firm has already created. Porter and Kramer contrast fair trade, which pays poor farmers a higher price for the same crop, with investment in growing techniques and local suppliers: early studies of cocoa farmers in Côte d’Ivoire suggest the first raises incomes by 10–20%, the second by more than 300% (2011, p. 5). On the value stick, fair trade moves price; shared value lengthens the stick.

Two claims carry the argument. Societal needs, not only conventional economic needs, define markets; and social harms such as wasted energy, accidents or poor skills often create internal costs for the firm, so fixing them can raise productivity rather than cost (p. 5).

Why it belongs in a strategy course

Porter and Kramer place shared value inside strategy, not beside it: strategy is choosing a unique position and a distinctive value chain, and shared value opens new needs to meet, customers to serve and ways to configure the chain. They claim the advantages it creates will often be more sustainable than ordinary cost and quality improvements (2011, p. 16). That is a claim to test, not to assume.

Reading task · two outcomes, one causal explanation

Test a shared-value proposal with two linked claims: an improvement in a social or environmental outcome, and an improvement in the firm’s competitive economics. State how operating choices connect them, name any harm displaced, and compare with what would otherwise happen. A donation can create social benefit without changing the operating advantage; a profitable product can still worsen environmental outcomes. Patagonia’s ownership decision, repair activities and product choices therefore need separate arguments, not one “good company” verdict.

Further reading

Does “competitive advantage” mean anything?

Rumelt lines up the definitions of Porter, Peteraf, Barney, Ghemawat and Rivkin, Besanko and colleagues, Kay, Brandenburger and Stuart and others, and finds four areas of confusion: how value is conceptualised or measured, what rents are, how to use opportunity cost, and whether advantage means winning the game or holding enough distinctive resources to stay in it (2003, pp. 1–3).

His sharpest point is the factor-price fallacy. Two drug firms each earn $100 million. One owes it to patents, which could be sold or licensed, so textbook economics says its “profit” is really rent to the patents; the other owes it to managerial skill that no market prices, so it appears to earn economic profit. Defining advantage so that the second has it and the first does not is absurd (p. 3). Brandenburger and Stuart’s answer, which Ghemawat and Rivkin build on, is that what a player can appropriate is limited by its added value (Rumelt, 2003, p. 2).

Three items from Rumelt’s test (p. 4)

  1. 1. Firm A owns one asset: a vault full of silver. Does it have a competitive advantage in the silver business?
  2. 3. Firm C owns a mine with an unusually rich vein, so its cost per pound is the lowest in the world, though its mining is of ordinary efficiency. Does the firm as a whole have an advantage?
  3. 6. Firm H earns 15% after tax on invested capital, above the norm for all firms, but below the other four firms in its narrow branded-food industry. Does it have an advantage there?
Show one reading, using the value stick

Rumelt gives no answer key; this is our reading. (1) A vault creates no wedge between willingness to pay and supplier opportunity cost: the silver is worth the same to anyone, so no. (3) The mine widens the wedge on the cost side, but the advantage belongs to the ore, and its owner could capture it by selling or renting the mine; ask whether the question is about the firm or the asset. (6) Ghemawat and Rivkin compare a firm with its own industry, so a 15% return that trails all four rivals is not an advantage, however good it looks against the economy.

Shared value · Rothaermel (2024), ch. 5

Doing well by doing good, or doing good because you did well?

The textbook’s chapter on this session’s title frames the debate as a shift in what a firm is for: from shareholder capitalism, through corporate social responsibility, to creating shared value (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 171–180). Each position implies a different answer to the question on the stick: is a social benefit a cost to be justified, or a source of willingness to pay and productivity?

Shareholder capitalism
“There is one and only one social responsibility of business—to use its resources and engage in activities designed to increase its profits so long as it stays within the rules of the game, which is to say, engages in open and free competition without deception or fraud.”

Milton Friedman (1962), as quoted in Rothaermel (2024, p. 172)

The view rests on three assumptions: free markets are efficient, individual freedom is society’s first goal, and managers are the shareholders’ agents (p. 172). Porter and Kramer call it the old, narrow view in which a firm is self-contained and social issues fall outside its scope (2011, p. 6).

Stakeholder capitalism

In 2019 almost 200 chief executives signed a Business Roundtable statement that the purpose of a corporation is to promote an economy that serves all Americans, putting customers, employees, suppliers and communities alongside long-term shareholder value (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 169). BlackRock, with $10 trillion under management, has pressed the firms it invests in on ESG goals; its critics answer that such questions belong to the democratic process, not to unelected asset managers (pp. 178–179).

Indra Nooyi’s line at PepsiCo marks the shift from CSR to shared value: “Performance with Purpose is not how we spend the money we make; it’s how we make the money” (as quoted in Rothaermel, 2024, p. 195).

The triple bottom line

Profits, people and planet: a strategy that can be pursued over time without harming people or the planet scores on all three (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 197). Nooyi defined true profit as revenues minus costs minus costs to society (p. 196). Being early can pay: BMW lined up car recyclers and redesigned cars for quick disassembly before take-back rules arrived (p. 196).

Patagonia in the textbook

The chapter case calls Patagonia a pioneer of shared value and quotes Chouinard: businesses are responsible, fundamentally, to their resource base (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 169). It lists onsite child care, flexible hours and 1% of revenue to environmental causes, and reports that Patagonia will not sell co-branded vests to corporate buyers that are not B Corps or do not commit revenue to the planet (p. 170).

Where the textbook and the case differ

Use the HBS case for facts. The textbook dates Patagonia’s benefit-corporation status to 2012 (p. 170); the case says Patagonia certified as a B Corporation and registered as California’s first benefit corporation in 2011 (p. 5). The textbook says customers happily paid a premium for organic cotton (p. 180); the case says only that sales recovered within two years (p. 4).

Which changes outside the firm create advantage, and when can a social need become one?

Porter’s own summary splits a business unit’s performance in two: the structure of its industry and its positioning within that industry (Porter, 2017, slide 6). Grant adds time. Competitive advantage, he says, is a disequilibrium phenomenon: it is created by change, and once it exists it sets off the competition that wears it down (2019, p. 157). The differential effect

A change creates advantage only if it hits firms differently

By cutting subsidies for renewable energy, the US tax bill of December 2017 favoured fossil-fuel generators such as Duke Energy over wind and solar producers. The bigger the change and the more firms differ, the wider the spread of profits: the leading brewers, with similar resources in a stable market, earn much the same; toy companies, a mixed group facing fickle tastes, do not (Grant, 2019, pp. 157–158).

Responsiveness

Anticipation and agility

Every change goes to whoever exploits it best. IBM renewed its advantage by seeing major shifts in computing coming, where Hewlett-Packard was less adept; Zara’s integrated supply chain takes a garment from design to store in under three weeks against an industry norm of three to six months (Grant, 2019, p. 158). The course’s teaching plan frames the same capacity as sensing an opportunity, seizing it and reconfiguring the firm’s resources around it.

Industry still matters · Ghemawat & Rivkin

Neutralise what is bad, exploit what is good

Firms that build advantage usually design strategies that neutralise their industry’s unattractive features and exploit its attractive ones. Industry conditions also decide whether advantage is possible at all: computer leasing “strait-jackets” firms, prepackaged software lets the best pull far ahead. And a leader such as Alcoa must weigh what new capacity does to the whole industry, not just to its own wedge (1998, p. 4).

Where to look for outside opportunities · Ghemawat & Rivkin (1998), p. 17

Draw your customers’ and suppliers’ value chains. Apparel makers that shipped clothes on the right hangers and in the right containers cut the time and labour department stores spent getting them from dock to floor.

Watch the bleeding edge. Exacting customers foreshadow the market; Yahoo! released test versions to sophisticated users to sense what the wider market would want.

Look for the under- and over-served. Circus Circus saw that Las Vegas offered little to families; Southwest saw travellers paying for frills they did not value.

When a social need is the change

Porter and Kramer argue that managers have looked at the industry and missed the wider environment: society’s unmet needs, the harms that raise costs inside the value chain, and the weaknesses of the places where firms operate (2011, pp. 6–7). Those are external conditions. Shared value is the claim that a firm can turn them into advantage in three ways, which reinforce one another (pp. 7, 16): reconceiving products and markets, redefining productivity in the value chain, and enabling local cluster development.

Patagonia’s external sources, as the course deck framed them

  • Shifting demand. Between 2000 and 2014 the developing world’s growing middle class pushed garment demand up: the average consumer bought 60% more garments a year, and discarded textiles rose 85% a year (case, p. 4).
  • Escalating environmental concern. Greenhouse-gas levels kept rising; the fashion industry made 10% of human emissions and was the second-largest industrial water user (p. 4, Exhibit 3).
  • Industry dynamics. Fast-fashion retailers turning over their whole stock several times a year multiplied (p. 4), while Patagonia held the largest share of US outdoor apparel, nearly 10% (p. 2).
  • Regulation. California’s 2011 law created benefit corporations (p. 5).

Two cautions

A trend helps everyone it touches. Rising concern for the environment is an advantage for Patagonia only if it raises willingness to pay for Patagonia more than for rivals, which takes something rivals lack. That something is an internal source: question 3.

A “moral moat” is a hypothesis. The course’s teaching plan suggests that by shifting buyers towards sustainability Patagonia raised ethical barriers that rivals competing on price alone struggle to cross. That reads Session 3’s entry barriers through shared value; test it before you use it. Good regulation can help: Porter and Kramer argue for rules that set measurable goals and leave the method to firms (2011, p. 14).

Question 03 · Prahalad & Hamel (1990)

What inside the firm creates it, and how would you know it is core?

An internal source is something the firm made: a product, a process, a policy, a way of working, a competence. Prahalad and Hamel put the time scales apart. In the short run, competitiveness comes from the price and performance of today’s products; in the long run, from building, faster and more cheaply than rivals, the core competencies that spawn products nobody has yet imagined (1990, p. 81).

Grant’s internal source is innovation, and he stresses strategic innovation over technology alone: new industry models such as Dell’s direct sales, new revenue models such as Rolls-Royce’s “Power by the Hour” engine contracts, and new enterprise models such as the iPhone’s outsourced manufacture and network of app developers (2019, pp. 158–159). Ghemawat and Rivkin add the warning that matters most for Patagonia: advantage usually comes from the full range of activities acting in harmony, so no single practice explains it (1998, p. 3).

The course deck’s three internal sources

  • Intangible resources: a brand built over decades on quality, durability and activism.
  • Capabilities: designing functional outdoor gear, a supply chain built to cut environmental harm, mission-led marketing, and repair through Worn Wear.
  • Culture: environmental purpose at the centre, and, after the 1991 layoffs, growth capped to keep the workplace stable (case, p. 3).

Prahalad and Hamel give three tests (p. 83). A core competence (1) gives potential access to a wide variety of markets, (2) makes a significant contribution to the benefits customers perceive in the end product, and (3) is difficult for competitors to imitate, because it is a complex harmonisation of technologies and production skills. Small steps that add up

The course’s teaching plan reads Patagonia as a story of continuous innovation: chocks replacing pitons, fleece replacing wool, repair extending product life, giving moving from a share of profit to a share of sales (case, pp. 2–4). Each step was small; together they are a pattern rivals would have to copy in full. That is Ghemawat and Rivkin’s rugged landscape again: one firm’s peak is a bundle of choices, not a feature (1998, pp. 18–19).

Top of the stick · customers

Raising willingness to pay

  • A better product for the same need. The 1977 pile fleece was as warm as wool, lighter and more weather-resistant, and became iconic (p. 3).
  • Durability as a stated value. The best product is useful, versatile, long-lasting, repairable and recyclable (Exhibit 2).
  • Repaired, not replaced. Worn Wear repairs Patagonia clothing free; Reno repaired 100,000 items a year, and returns were resold or recycled (p. 4).
  • Values buyers can see. From 1986 the greater of 1% of sales or 10% of profit went to environmental causes: nearly $200 million by fall 2020 (pp. 3, 6).

Middle of the stick · Patagonia

Choices that raised cost and risk

  • Organic cotton. Conventional cotton, grown on 2.5% of the world’s cultivated land, used 22.5% of chemical insecticides and 10% of agricultural pesticides. Chouinard moved all 166 cotton products to organic within eighteen months or would have stopped selling sportswear, then 30% of the business. Sales were back to their previous level within two years (p. 4).
  • Giving on every sale. The 1986 pledge meant at least 1% of sales went to environmental causes whatever the year’s profit (p. 3).
  • Growth held back. After laying off 20% of its 600 staff in the 1991 recession, Patagonia capped growth at pre-set targets (p. 3).

Bottom of the stick · staff and suppliers

Lowering the price of working with Patagonia

  • A job worth more than its pay. One of the first onsite corporate daycare centres in the US, paid parental leave before it was common, daily surf forecasts, training in Yosemite (p. 3).
  • Stability. The 1991 growth cap was meant to keep the workplace stable (p. 3); a supportive work environment became a charter commitment in 2011 (p. 5).
  • Suppliers: the open question. Most products came from 45 suppliers in 40 factories in 16 countries (p. 2). The case does not say whether they deal with Patagonia on different terms.

Founder’s claim

“I know it sounds crazy, but every time I have made a decision that’s best for the planet, [Patagonia has] made money.” Yvon Chouinard, quoted in Trelstad et al. (2023, p. 3)

On the stick this claims that each move raised willingness to pay, or lowered opportunity cost, by more than it raised cost: shared value in Porter and Kramer’s sense. It is a founder’s summary, not a test. Ask which decisions it leaves out.

The other half of the bargain

“Because if you’re not successful financially, the experiment’s over.” Kris Tompkins, CEO from 1979, quoted in Trelstad et al. (2023, p. 3)

Creating value was never enough. Patagonia had to capture a slice large enough to fund the giving, the repairs and the growth caps, which is why price and margin still matter in a purpose-led firm.

Who captures the value now?

On 14 September 2022 Chouinard told an all-staff meeting in Ventura that he was no longer Patagonia’s owner (case, p. 1). In Ghemawat and Rivkin’s terms the 2022 structure changes who receives the firm’s slice of the stick; in Porter and Kramer’s terms that is distribution, not creation. Follow the votes and the money.

What the case cannot prove

There are no prices, unit costs, rival margins or turnover figures in the case. What it offers is a pattern: choices that looked costly, followed by growth from about $20 million in sales to about $1 billion and the largest share of its US market. A pattern is not proof that the wedge widened. Decide what data would convince a sceptic, and which point on the stick each item would measure.

The question the room usually splits on

Did Patagonia’s purpose create its wedge, or did its wedge pay for its purpose? On the first reading, each environmental choice raised willingness to pay or lowered what it took to attract staff by more than it cost: shared value, as Chouinard claims. On the second, a firm with a strong product and a loyal niche earned a margin wide enough to afford giving, repairs and a growth cap: responsibility funded by advantage. Both readings fit the case’s numbers, and they imply opposite things for a rival that copies the purpose without the product.

Take a side before class, and name the evidence that would change your mind.

    Rumelt, R. P. (2003). What in the world is competitive advantage? (Policy Working Paper 2003-105). The Anderson School at UCLA.

    Five pages and a short test. Take the test before you read the definitions.

  8. Further · framing

    Porter, M. E. (2017, May 24). Strategy: Creating and sustaining competitive advantage [Presentation slides]. Mumbai.

    Industry structure and positioning, the value chain, and zero-sum against positive-sum competition, with IKEA and PACCAR as examples. Porter (1985/2004), Competitive advantage, ch. 1, is the classic statement.
