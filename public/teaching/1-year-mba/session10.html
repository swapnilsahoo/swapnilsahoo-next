Sessions 1-9 provide a chain from a chosen customer promise to a provisional corporate boundary. One continuing classroom story. Nila Foods is a fictional Indian meal-service for working adults. Follow its choices from Session 1; later expansion ideas are proposals to test.

  1. Session 1 · What is strategy?

    Operational effectiveness improves similar activities; strategy requires positioning, trade-offs and fit. State objective, scope and advantage, then compare intended plans with emergent action using evidence.

    Nila Foods: Nila proposes preordered weekday meals on clustered routes, declines instant citywide delivery and studies actual customer behaviour.

  2. Session 2 · Framework for Strategic Analysis

    Purpose and values guide what endures, while a quantified ambition opens a same-date gap from the baseline; closing that gap requires external and internal evidence.

    Nila Foods: Nila separated easing meal burdens and honest food-safety disclosure from its growth target, then compared that target with its projected baseline.

  3. Session 3 · Industry Analysis: The Fundamentals

    Industry boundaries, PESTEL and five forces connect external change to price, cost and investment; complements and possible responses then raise questions about the wider game.

    Nila Foods: Nila traced ingredient-price pressure, supplier bargaining, lunch substitutes and easy entry before asking whether a neighbouring seller could also help it.

  4. Session 4 · Beyond the Five Forces

    Value nets and PARTS show how co-opetition changes added value, while uncertainty shapes commitments; credible moves therefore require examining internal strengths.

    Nila Foods: Nila considered sharing a collection point with a rival and testing the rules through a limited pilot while demand remained uncertain.

  5. Session 5 · Internal Analysis & Resource-Based View

    Resources and capabilities support advantage when they improve buyer benefits or relative costs and pass tests against rivals and substitutes; value-chain analysis then checks their economic contribution.

    Nila Foods: Nila examined kitchen routines, route know-how and trust, asking which linked activities rivals could imitate and whether it could retain their benefits.

  6. Session 6 · Creating advantage and shared value

    Use the value stick to distinguish value created from value captured. Test shared value against both business and social effects, including burdens shifted elsewhere.

    Nila Foods: Nila asks whether demand forecasts and reusable containers reduce waste and cost, including the return and cleaning work.

  7. Session 7 · Business Strategy: Differentiation, Cost Leadership & Blue Ocean

    Porter distinguishes cost leadership, differentiation and focus; Kim and Mauborgne test value innovation; Ghemawat and Pisano examine threats, commitments and capabilities.

    Nila Foods: Nila compares dependable delivery and relative cost within its chosen segment. Its value curve and four-actions proposal must demonstrate new demand and viable economics before supporting a blue ocean claim.

  8. Session 8 · Entrepreneurship, Platforms, Technology & Innovation

    Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne test platform participation and pricing; Bower and Christensen distinguish disruptive trajectories from sustaining improvements.

    Nila Foods: To extend its offer, Nila asks whether customers and nutrition creators could benefit from one another. An experiment tests complement quality, retained use and cross-side effects; success alone would not establish disruption.

  9. Session 9 · Corporate Strategy: Vertical Integration & Diversification

    Porter tests attractiveness, entry cost and the better-off mechanism; Campbell, Goold and Alexander ask whether this parent beats alternative owners.

    Nila Foods: The ecosystem need not be owned. Nila asks whether owning a packaging supplier would improve meal delivery enough to outweigh capital, coordination and harmful misfit; contracts and specialist owners remain alternatives.

Why this leads to Session 10

Nila's packaging question now needs a route: build capability, contract, license, ally, form a joint venture or acquire. Dyer, Kale and Singh direct attention to resource interdependence, uncertainty and collaboration capability. Christensen and colleagues ask whether a deal supplies resources for the existing business or a different business model that may need protection. Compare credible alternatives, allocate responsibility for benefits, and deduct the premium and integration costs before judging buyer value.

Compare your reasoning with a model answer

Nila's weekday promise determines its preorders, clustered routes and refusals. Purpose and a same-date gap set direction; five forces test the economics. Co-opetition and uncertainty shape a bounded office-partner experiment. Internal analysis tests coordination capabilities; activity and social ledgers test their economic and waste-reduction effects. Business strategy asks whether these support a defensible relative value-cost position. Platform analysis treats nutrition creators as a participation hypothesis. Corporate strategy then asks whether packaging ownership passes the three tests and whether Nila is a better parent. None makes acquisition inevitable. If the gap is reliable packaging access and joint design, a contractual alliance might suffice when performance is verifiable and uncertainty favours flexibility. Acquisition needs an additional control or interdependence argument, credible capabilities and value after premium and integration costs. Preserve any target routines the benefit depends on. Repeated contractual failures involving specialised assets could favour ownership; poor unit economics or capability misfit could reverse it.

Carried forward · Session 9

A scope decision still needs a route

Session 9 asked which businesses and value-chain stages belong inside the firm. Today asks how to obtain a missing capability. A promising market does not tell you whether to build, license, partner, form a joint venture or acquire. Each route changes who controls the work, who receives the upside and who bears the risk.

Portfolio logic

Capital can move toward attractive opportunities. But owning a portfolio does not automatically create an operating advantage: investors can often diversify for themselves. What does the parent contribute?

Synergy logic

Two activities may be more valuable together through shared assets, lower coordination costs or better customer access. Identify the specific link. “Cross-selling” is a hypothesis until a buyer actually purchases.

Core-competence logic

A repeatable skill may open a new market. Distinguish a transferable routine from a slogan: which people, systems and know-how must move, and what cannot travel?

Transaction-cost logic

Contracts require search, negotiation, monitoring and enforcement. Ownership may reduce bargaining problems when assets are specialised, but it adds bureaucracy, integration costs and the risk of destroying initiative.

Two required readings

Choose the relationship before you choose the target

What has to be combined?

The reading treats alliances and acquisitions as alternatives. Its comparison considers the desired resources and synergies, market conditions and the firm’s ability to collaborate. A familiar deal type can become a habit that prevents a serious comparison.

Reading task: identify a condition favouring an alliance and one favouring acquisition. Then ask whether Amazon needs ownership of the whole organisation or reliable access to a specific capability.

Publisher’s reading record

Are you buying resources or a different growth engine?

The authors distinguish deals that improve the existing business from deals that change its growth trajectory. Absorbing resources can serve the first purpose; preserving a target’s business model may matter for the second. Target selection, price and integration should follow that purpose.

Reading task: name something that integration would improve and something it could destroy. Explain why the same integration plan should not be applied to every acquisition.

The strategic alternatives

Build, borrow or buy is the start of the argument

RouteWhat you gainWhat you must manage
Build internallyControl over development and ownership of learningTime to competence; hiring and experimentation; whether existing routines can produce the capability
Contract or licenseAccess to a defined input without owning the supplierQuality verification, renewal, intellectual-property boundaries and switching costs
Strategic allianceComplementary assets and shared learning while partners remain separateJoint priorities, knowledge leakage, unequal effort and disputes over who captures value
Equity alliance / joint ventureA stronger commitment; a jointly governed vehicle in a JVBoard rights, additional funding, deadlock, performance measures and exit provisions
AcquireOwnership and authority over the acquired assetsPrice, approvals, cultural disruption, retention and execution of the integration plan
Trade-off: control is valuable only if you can use it. Buying an innovative team and removing its decision rights may damage the capability you paid for. Conversely, an alliance may fail when daily decisions require coordination that the contract cannot specify.

Do not mistake the target for the thesis

“Buy Whole Foods” names an action. A thesis says which customer problem becomes easier to solve, which activities produce the improvement and what rivals cannot reproduce easily.

Write the counterfactual

A good acquisition memo compares buying with licensing, partnering, internal development and doing nothing. Without those alternatives, almost any synergy story can sound compelling.

Hypothetical · original scenario

Nila Delivery: access to a team or ownership of it?

Nila wants a regional delivery team’s route knowledge. Demand is uncertain, service quality can be measured and the team values local decision rights. A limited alliance can test the benefit and the working relationship. Acquisition may become preferable if exclusive control is essential and contracts cannot protect the required investment. Buying first is not a substitute for explaining those conditions or retaining the people who carry the knowledge.

Indian · Tata Motors / 2 June 2008

JLR: acquisition did not eliminate every contract

The completion announcement describes Tata Motors acquiring Jaguar Land Rover, including plants, design centers, sales companies and necessary intellectual-property ownership or licenses.
