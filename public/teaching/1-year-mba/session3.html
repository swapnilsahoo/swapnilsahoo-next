Industry Analysis: The Fundamentals

The External Environment & Porter’s Five (Six) Forces

Why do some industries earn far more than others, year after year? Scan the macro-environment with PESTEL, take an industry apart with Porter’s five forces, settle the argument about a sixth, and turn the analysis into a strategy — then test all of it on the US car industry that Tesla chose to enter.

Session 2 started where Kaplan and Norton start: purpose and values, the core ideology Collins and Porras describe, then a vision precise enough to measure — Tesla’s 500,000 cars a year by 2020 — and the value gap between that ambition and where Tesla stood. Their next step is analysis: the key issues a strategy must address. Session 2’s quick five-forces read said “do not enter”. Today you build the tool properly and decide whether that verdict was wrong, or only the way the tool was used.

Question 01 · Rothaermel (2024), ch. 3

What in the environment actually matters?

The external environment is everything outside the firm that can affect its ability to gain and keep an advantage. Rothaermel arranges it in layers, from the general environment inwards to the industry, the strategic group of close rivals and finally the firm. Leaders have little direct say over the outer layer, such as interest or exchange rates, and some say over the inner ones, such as the structure of their industry (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 83).

The general environment is scanned with PESTEL: political, economic, sociocultural, technological, ecological and legal factors. Each can be an opportunity or a threat. The model has one weakness worth remembering: it is a snapshot, so it cannot tell you which factor is moving fastest (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 90).

Porter supplies the rule that links the two layers. Government, he argues, is not a sixth force, because its involvement is neither good nor bad for profitability in itself; the way to understand a policy is to trace how it changes the five forces (Porter, 2008, p. 86). Apply that to every PESTEL item: a trend matters to strategy only once you can say which force it moves, in which direction, and roughly how much.

Porter’s 1983 note adds the second half of the rule. Forces outside an industry matter mainly in a relative sense: they usually hit every firm in it, so the strategic question is which firms are better able to deal with them (Porter, 1983, p. 1). A trend that hurts everyone equally changes little between rivals; one that hurts incumbents more than an entrant changes a great deal.

P · Political

Pressure from government bodies, NGOs and social movements, usually before any law changes. Firms answer with nonmarket strategy: lobbying, public relations, contributions and litigation (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 83–84).

US autos: politicians at every level were keen to protect nearly 3 million auto jobs, and the dealer lobby fought Tesla’s direct sales (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 3, 14).

E · Economic

Macroeconomic conditions: growth, employment, interest rates, price stability and exchange rates (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 84–86).

US autos: sales rebounded by 2013 on higher incomes and easier credit, then stalled; they were forecast to stay flat until 2021 (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 2). When rates rose sharply in 2022, Tesla’s valuation fell more than 60% (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 15).

S · Sociocultural

A society’s culture, norms and values, including demographic trends (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 86–87).

US autos: 92% of 20–24-year-olds held a driving licence in 1983; by 2014 it was 77% (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 5).

T · Technological

The application of knowledge to create new processes and products (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 87).

US autos: battery prices fell 80% between 2010 and 2017, and an electric car has about a third as many components as a combustion car (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 4, 7).

E · Ecological

The natural environment, climate change and sustainable growth, including the externalities firms impose without paying for them (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 89).

US autos: passenger road vehicles emitted 3.64 gigatons of CO2 in 2020, the largest source in transport (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, Exhibit 1).

L · Legal

The official outcomes of political processes: laws, mandates, regulations and court decisions (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 89).

US autos: in 1990 California required 2% of cars in the state to be zero-emission by 1998, rising to 10% by 2003 (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 4).

Required

Where the pressure comes from: the note behind the article

Porter’s 1983 note, first written in 1975 and the opening chapter of Competitive Strategy, is the longer engine room of the 2008 article. Its job is to find the structural features beneath each force, because the collective strength of the forces may be painfully obvious to every competitor; the strategist has to dig below it (Porter, 1983, p. 1).

  • A floor under returns. Competition pushes return on invested capital towards the floor a perfectly competitive industry would earn; investors will not accept less for long, since they could buy government securities instead. Returns above the floor pull capital in, through entry or expansion. The five forces decide how far that inflow drives returns down (p. 2).
  • The strongest force governs. In ocean tankers it is probably the buyers, the oil majors; in tyres, powerful car-maker buyers plus tough rivals; in steel, foreign competitors and substitute materials (p. 3).
  • Scale is not experience. Scale economies depend on volume per period, experience on cumulative volume. Experience is a barrier only if it stays proprietary, and a new product or process technology can start a fresh experience curve on which entrants leapfrog leaders that are poorly placed to jump. Commitment to scale in an existing technology can cloud a leader’s view of new ones (pp. 6, 8–9).
  • Barriers move, and firms move them. In cars, scale economies rose enormously with post-war automation and vertical integration, virtually stopping successful new entry (p. 7). A price below the entry-deterring price, the level that just balances an entrant’s expected rewards against the cost of barriers and retaliation, keeps entrants out (p. 7).

Reading lens · what structural feature causes the pressure?

Build an evidence ledger: scale requirements, differentiation, switching costs, access to distribution, capital requirements and exit barriers. Separate a structural constraint from one company’s weak execution. Reading task: choose one entry barrier and say what an entrant must spend or change, why incumbents are advantaged, and what could erode the barrier.

Supplementary

Profit = price − cost: where each force bites

“The real point of competition is not to beat your rivals. It’s to earn profits.”Magretta (2012), p. 2

Magretta’s chapter makes Porter’s argument with one equation. Every force works on price, on cost, or on both, and nothing else reaches profit. Strengthen a force below and watch which side of the equation it squeezes. Her three findings: the same forces sit under the skin of every industry; structure, not growth, technology or regulation, decides profitability; and structure is sticky, changing slowly once an industry is past its emerging phase (Magretta, 2012, pp. 2–4).

  • Why only five? Because they cover every relationship in commerce: buyers and sellers, sellers and suppliers, rival sellers, supply and demand (p. 18).
  • Why not SWOT? With no economics underneath it, SWOT yields lists that depend on who is in the room; industry structure is systematic and built on facts (p. 5).
  • One structure, two industries. CEMEX earns more in Mexico, where 85% of cement revenue comes from small buyers, than in the US, where big contractors bargain prices down. Same product, different buyer power, so two industries (p. 8).
  • A shelter in a storm. From 1993 to 2007 the heavy-truck industry averaged a 10.5% return on invested capital; Paccar, serving owner-operators, earned 31.6% (p. 25).

Structure is dynamic

Analysis is a snapshot plus a trend. Walmart was barely visible in 1970; it became the most powerful buyer in industry after industry, and its suppliers watched it happen slowly enough to act. Change that is truly strategic shows up in the five forces (Magretta, 2012, pp. 26–27).

How much does the industry explain?

Rothaermel defines an industry as a group of incumbent firms with more or less the same set of suppliers and buyers, and credits Porter with combining industrial-organisation economics with detailed case studies (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 91). Rothaermel’s sixth force · pp. 109–110

A complement adds value to a product when the two are used together, so it raises demand and the profit potential of the industry. A complementor is a firm whose offering makes customers value yours more. Rivals can be complementors at the same time: Google’s Android makes Samsung’s phones more valuable while Google sells phones of its own.   • The firm matters more than the industry. The research behind the slider is why Session 5 goes inside the firm. The five forces set the baseline a strategy must beat; they do not decide who beats it.

Question 02 · Porter (2008) and Porter (1983)

What sets an industry’s profitability?

Managers tend to define competition as the fight with today’s direct rivals. Porter’s point is that the fight for profit is wider: customers, suppliers, potential entrants and substitutes all compete for the value an industry creates. That extended rivalry is the industry’s structure (Porter, 2008, p. 79). Where the forces are intense, as in airlines, textiles and hotels, almost no company earns attractive returns; where they are benign, as in software, soft drinks and toiletries, many do (p. 80).

“Industry structure drives competition and profitability, not whether an industry produces a product or service, is emerging or mature, high tech or low tech, regulated or unregulated.”Porter (2008), p. 80

Weather and the business cycle move profits in the short run; structure sets them over the medium and long run. And the strongest force decides, which is not always the obvious one. Film makers Kodak and Fuji fought each other, but their low returns came from a substitute, digital photography. In commercial aircraft the strong forces are the Airbus–Boeing rivalry and the airlines that place huge orders; in cinemas they are substitute entertainment and the studios that supply the films (Porter, 2008, p. 80).

The professor’s framing for a 1979 framework, updated in 2008: it does not tell you what to think about an industry; it tells you how to think about one. Treat it as an X-ray of structure, not a crystal ball.

How each force reaches the income statement

Porter ties every force to a line in the accounts and asks the same question of each: how much? (Porter, 2008, p. 87). Three of the forces compete with you sideways, for the same customers’ money: rivals, entrants and substitutes. Two squeeze you along the chain: suppliers above, buyers below.

What makes each force strong

The determinants below are Porter’s, in paraphrase (Porter, 2008, pp. 80–86). These, not the five labels, are what you analyse. A force is a summary of its determinants.

Threat of entry

Entrants add capacity and fight for share. The threat alone caps profits: incumbents hold prices down or invest to deter, as Starbucks must keep modernising stores and menus in a business with low barriers. Seven barriers keep the threat low:

  • supply-side economies of scale (Intel in microprocessors);
  • demand-side benefits of scale, or network effects (eBay);
  • customer switching costs (an installed SAP system);
  • capital requirements, especially unrecoverable ones such as R&D and advertising, though efficient capital markets will fund entry into an attractive industry;
  • incumbency advantages independent of size: technology, raw materials, locations, brands, experience;
  • unequal access to distribution channels;
  • restrictive government policy (which can also ease entry, through subsidies).

Plus expected retaliation: entrants hold back when incumbents have hit back before, have cash and capacity to fight, have high fixed costs, or face slow growth. Barriers decide whether an entrant can get in; expected retaliation decides whether it wants to.

Power of suppliers

Powerful suppliers, including suppliers of labour, take value by charging more, limiting quality or shifting costs. Microsoft did it to PC makers. A supplier group is powerful if it:

  • is more concentrated than the industry it sells to;
  • does not depend heavily on that industry for its revenue;
  • would cost the industry a lot to switch away from;
  • offers differentiated products (patented drugs);
  • has no substitute (a trained pilot in the cockpit);
  • can credibly threaten to integrate forward into the industry.

Power of buyers

Two separate questions. Leverage: few or large buyers, standardised products, low switching costs, a credible threat to integrate backward. Price sensitivity: the purchase is a large share of the buyer’s costs or budget, the buyer earns low profits, and the product barely affects the buyer’s own quality or other costs. Film studios renting cameras care about reliability, not price.

Intermediate customers, such as distribution channels, gain extra power when they influence what end customers buy. Producers fight back with exclusive deals or by selling direct.

Threat of substitutes

A substitute does the same job by a different means: videoconferencing for travel, e-mail for express mail. Doing without, buying used and doing it yourself all count. The threat can also be downstream: lawn-care firms lose when apartments replace suburban houses. Substitutes are easy to overlook because they look nothing like the product. The threat is high when the substitute:

  • offers an attractive price-performance trade-off;
  • costs the buyer little to switch to (branded to generic drugs).

Watch other industries: a change there can turn something into a substitute that was not one before, as better plastics did for steel car parts.

Rivalry among existing competitors

Damage depends on intensity and on the basis of competition. Intensity is greatest when rivals are numerous or equal in size, growth is slow, exit barriers are high, rivals have goals beyond profit, and they misread each other’s signals.

Price wars are likeliest when products are near-identical with low switching costs, fixed costs are high and marginal costs low, capacity comes in big increments, or the product is perishable. Competing on different dimensions for different segments can be positive-sum; everyone chasing the same attributes is zero-sum.

Before you rate anything

Define the arena first

State product, customer, geography and period before you score a force. Porter’s 1983 working definition is the group of firms making close substitutes, and he adds that every definition is a choice of where to draw lines, between rivals and substitutes, incumbents and entrants, the industry and its suppliers and buyers, so it is a matter of degree (1983, pp. 2, 18–19). The 2008 test: if the forces differ markedly, they are separate industries. Car and truck motor oil look alike but are different industries (2008, p. 91), as is cement in Mexico and the US (Magretta, 2012, p. 8).

US passenger-car making is not global battery production or urban mobility: a new boundary can turn the same firm from supplier into rival or substitute. Defining the industry is also not the same as deciding where your firm competes (Porter, 1983, p. 19).

What the 1983 note adds to the determinants

The 2008 article is the short version. The note gives more causes, and more of the auto industry, for the same forces (Porter, 1983).

Rivalry · pp. 9–12

Fixed costs matter relative to value added; costly storage tempts price cuts as surely as fixed costs; diverse rivals, foreign or owner-run, misread each other; high strategic stakes make firms accept poor returns, as Bosch, Sony or Philips might to win the US. Exit barriers have five sources: specialised assets, fixed costs of exit, strategic interrelationships, emotion, and government.

Buyers and suppliers · pp. 14–16

GM and Ford used tapered integration, making some of a part in-house and buying the rest, as a bargaining lever that also taught them suppliers’ costs. Buyer selection is a strategy: the replacement market is usually less price-sensitive than original-equipment makers. Labour’s power rises with its organisation and falls when supply can expand.

Substitutes · p. 13

Watch most closely the substitutes whose price-performance is improving, and those made by highly profitable industries. A collective industry response can work where one firm’s cannot; security-guard firms facing electronic alarms were better off packaging guards with electronics than fighting them.

Government · p. 16

Government is also a buyer and a supplier, and shifts substitutes through subsidies and standards. Analyse it through the five forces rather than as a force of its own, but strategy may still treat government as an actor to be influenced, which is what Tesla’s fight with the dealer lobby was.

Examples in this grid: Starbucks, Intel, eBay, SAP (Porter, 2008, pp. 81–82); Microsoft, patented drugs, pilots (pp. 82–83); film studios (p. 84); videoconferencing, e-mail, lawn care, generic drugs, plastics (pp. 84–85).

Beyond cars · the teaching script, the earlier decks and Magretta (2012)

Five forces in industries you already use

You are here to build the next Nvidia or SpaceX, not to analyse heavy trucks; the forces still apply. Each card asks the question the session script puts to one force and answers it with a company you know. These are illustrations, stated qualitatively: figures in the script that could not be checked against the readings are left out.

The gatekeepers · threat of entry

“What really stops someone copying your business?”

  • SpaceX faced the ultimate capital barrier and then built new ones: reusable rockets that cut its cost per launch, and a record buyers trust. When Boeing’s crew capsule ran into trouble on its first crewed flight, NASA turned to SpaceX to bring the crew home.
  • Threads launched on the back of Instagram’s users instead of building a network from scratch: Porter’s entrant “diversifying from other markets”, which can bypass a network-effect barrier (Porter, 2008, p. 80).
  • Early ride-hailing needed only an app and drivers, so entrants poured in and spent years buying share. Its barriers had to be built, not inherited.

The power players · suppliers

“Who really makes the money in a gold rush?”

  • Nvidia in the AI build-out: one dominant supplier, differentiated chips, and switching costs that sit in software written for its CUDA platform rather than in the hardware.
  • Microsoft and Intel squeezed PC makers, which were also losing ground to buyers as the PC became a commodity; Intel Inside branded what might have become a commodity part (Porter, 2008, p. 82; Magretta, 2012, pp. 9, 11).
  • Neodymium: China produced 95% of the world’s supply; prices quadrupled in 2010 as it restricted supply, and Toyota set out to design a motor without rare earths (Magretta, 2012, p. 11).
  • The response in the script: Tesla making its own battery cells and AI-training hardware, Porter’s “alter technology to avoid a powerful supplier group” (2008, p. 90).

The squeeze · buyers

“One of millions of sellers on one platform: customer or product?”

  • Amazon’s marketplace is one dominant channel facing many fragmented sellers, whose products sit on identical pages competing on price and reviews, and who lose its customers if they leave: an intermediate customer with real clout (Porter, 2008, p. 84).
  • Riders with two cab apps on one phone switch for a few rupees or minutes, so the platforms keep paying to hold them.
  • The reverse: social-network users would lose their followers and history by leaving, so the platform’s power falls on its paying customers, the advertisers.
  • Cement: big US contractors bargain prices down; Mexico’s small buyers cannot (Magretta, 2012, p. 8).

The blind spot · substitutes

“What if your biggest threat doesn’t look like a threat?”

  • Bike taxis in congested Indian cities were cheaper and faster than a cab for a solo rider, one tap away: a better price-performance trade-off with no switching cost. Rapido later launched cabs, turning a substitute into a rival.
  • The job, not the product: airlines compete with videoconferencing (Porter, 2008, p. 84); a short-video app competes with X for the same minutes of downtime; meal kits competed less with each other than with groceries, delivery and frozen pizza.
  • Not always cheaper: the Madrid–Barcelona high-speed train is a dearer, better substitute for flying, as energy drinks are for coffee (Magretta, 2012, p. 13).

The main event · rivalry

“A zero-sum race to the bottom, or a positive-sum game?”

  • The EV shake-out the script describes for 2024–25: early-adopter growth slowing, many rivals (Tesla, BYD, Rivian, GM, Ford), gigafactories that cannot be redeployed, and prices falling. Porter’s warning: as an industry matures, competitors become more alike and rivalry intensifies (2008, p. 88).
  • The opposite: in reusable heavy-lift launch, SpaceX has had almost no direct rival. Near-zero rivalry lets it set terms, for now.
  • Positive-sum is possible: US casinos competed mainly by opening new niches, not by cutting prices (Porter, 2008, p. 88).

One company, five fronts · Amazon

  • Rivals: Walmart, Flipkart, Target.
  • Entrants: Temu and Shein, attacking from a low-cost angle.
  • Buyers: us, demanding fast free delivery, easy returns and low prices, and free to switch.
  • Suppliers: brands such as P&G, Samsung and Sony that would rather sell direct.
  • Substitutes: the shop down the road.

And Amazon the shaper (the script’s closing example): fulfilment logistics raise the bar for entrants, Prime raises switching costs, own brands discipline suppliers, and AWS turned an internal cost into an industry where Amazon is the supplier.

A second industry, done properly · the course’s ride-hailing exercise

Why an industry that created so much value keeps so little of it

Ride-hailing changed urban transport: convenience and transparent prices for riders, flexible income for many drivers. Yet the platforms have long struggled to earn a steady profit. The course’s ride-hailing exercise resolves the paradox the way Porter would: not with the novelty of the technology or the pace of growth, but with structure. It runs Porter’s steps on India.

Boundary. Product scope: four-wheel cabs, three-wheel auto-rickshaws and two-wheel bike taxis, related segments with different dynamics. Geographic scope: local or at most national, because rivals, alternatives and above all regulation differ from one city or state to the next.

Players. For years a two-player fight between Uber and Ola over cabs, paid for with investors’ money. Rapido came in through the overlooked bike-taxi niche on a driver subscription model, then moved into autos and cabs. Smaller players include electric-only fleets and zero-commission community apps, signs of where structure might move next.

Where the value goes

  • Riders keep most of it: low and subsidised fares, convenience, choice.
  • Drivers take a growing share as commissions fall and rules on their pay and welfare tighten.
  • Platforms fight over a small and precarious slice of the value they helped create.

The exercise’s verdicts and mechanisms, restated. Its market-share, commission and cost-per-kilometre figures come from web sources outside the readings, so they are not repeated here.

Threat of entry

Moderate to high

The walls look high: a two-sided network (more drivers, shorter waits, more riders, more drivers) and the capital to subsidise both sides. But there was a gate: a niche entry and a subscription model that built driver supply cheaply. The most potent entrant is not a clone with more money but a firm with a new economic logic.

Licensing cuts both ways: compliance costs deter, patchy state rules open doors.

Power of buyers (riders)

High

Switching costs are close to zero, the ride itself is largely undifferentiated, fares are compared before booking, and surge pricing is a running sore. A regulator’s inquiry into claims that fares varied by the rider’s phone, which the platforms denied, shows buyers’ power amplified by the state.

The platforms’ answer: sticky features such as fixed fares on regular routes and metro ticketing, competing on the app rather than the ride.

Power of suppliers (drivers)

Moderate, growing

One driver has no clout, but drivers run several apps at once and take the best-paid ride, they strike, and without enough of them the whole service collapses. Flat-fee models and national aggregator guidelines on drivers’ share of fares and welfare move power their way.

Autonomy would remove this supplier altogether, at a colossal capital cost.

Threat of substitutes

High

Owning a car (cheaper per kilometre for heavy users), metro and buses (a substitute for the whole trip and a complement for the first and last mile), street taxis and autos, and inside the category bike taxis for solo trips. Competing head-on with subsidised public transport is hard; Uber’s own shuttle-bus service was wound down.

Analyse substitution by use case, not by industry label.

Rivalry

Extreme

Well-funded rivals with leadership ambitions, slowing land-grab growth, a commodity ride, low switching costs and high fixed costs for technology and marketing: a recipe for price and incentive wars, which spilled into court. Exit barriers are high for players to whom India is strategic.

The basis of rivalry is shifting from subsidies to the business model itself.

Factors, traced through the forces

Government shapes entry through licensing, rivalry through fare and surge rules, supplier power through driver-welfare mandates, and substitutes through bike-taxi bans. Technology enables the business but also price transparency and low switching costs; AI routing is operational effectiveness that rivals copy quickly, the distinction from Session 1.

Question 03 · Porter (2008), pp. 86–92

Is there a sixth force, and how does the analysis become a strategy?

The course outline says “five (six)” because the readings disagree. The textbook adds complements as a sixth force; Porter, in the article that defines the five, argues they are not one. Settle that first, because it is the same discipline that turns an analysis into choices: everything that matters must be traced to a force, and every force to a move.

Rothaermel (2024), pp. 109–110

A sixth force: the strategic role of complements

A complement is a product, service or competency that adds value to the original offering when the two are used together. Complements raise demand for the primary product and so raise the profit potential of the industry and the firm. A complementor is a company whose product makes customers value yours more. Rivals can also cooperate as complementors, which Rothaermel calls co-opetition.

Porter (2008), pp. 86–87

Factors, not forces

Complements are products used together with an industry’s product, worth more combined than apart. The value of a car is higher when the driver can reach petrol stations, roadside help and insurance. They matter when they shift demand, but strong complements are not necessarily good or bad for profitability, so they are not a sixth force. They work through the five, and the analyst’s job is to trace their effect on each.

The two positions are closer than they look. Rothaermel stresses that complements can grow demand; Porter accepts that and insists you then ask who captures the extra value. Magretta gives the test that separates a force from a factor: more of a structural force always pushes price or cost one known way, while more growth, technology, government or complements can help or hurt (Magretta, 2012, p. 21). This page uses Porter’s test because it forces the second question.

Four things that look like forces and are not

Porter calls mistaking a visible attribute of an industry for its structure a common pitfall (Porter, 2008, p. 86). Each card gives his reason and an example from the cases.

Industry growth

Growth mutes rivalry, but it can strengthen suppliers and pull in entrants. Fast growth guarantees nothing if buyers are powerful or substitutes attractive; personal computers grew fast and were among the least profitable industries.

US EV sales grew 25% in 2017 to about 200,000 (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 6). The growth drew GM (20 EV models planned by 2023) and Ford ($11 billion for 24 hybrids and 16 EVs by 2022) into the segment (p. 7).

Technology

Advanced technology does not by itself make an industry attractive. Dull industries with high barriers or price-insensitive buyers often out-earn exciting ones.

Connected-car revenue was expected to nearly triple, from $53 billion in 2017 to $156 billion in 2022, yet PwC warned carmakers could become “dumb pipes on wheels” (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 9). Who captures the value?

Government

Trace each policy. Patents raise entry barriers; pro-union rules raise supplier power; bankruptcy rules that let failing firms reorganise rather than exit keep excess capacity in play.

GM and Chrysler went bankrupt in 2009; neither left the market, and none of the Big Three did even when capacity utilisation fell below 33% (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 3). That is rivalry, set by policy.

Complements

They can raise or lower entry barriers, ease or block substitution, and soften rivalry by raising switching costs or sharpen it by neutralising differentiation.

Porter’s own example: needing suitable fuelling stations makes it hard for alternative-fuel cars to substitute for conventional ones (Porter, 2008, p. 87). See EV charging, traced below.

From analysis to strategy: three things to do with the forces

Every company should know its industry’s average profitability and how it is changing; the forces explain why it is what it is. Porter then offers three ways to act, and says the best strategies use more than one (Porter, 2008, pp. 88–89). The 1983 note had named the same three: position the firm where its capabilities best defend against the forces, influence the balance through strategic moves, and anticipate shifts so as to exploit change before rivals see it (Porter, 1983, p. 17). The session script calls it the “so what?”: your actions can build a fortress or dig your own grave.

01 · POSITION

Find the foxhole where the forces are weakest

Heavy trucks are a hard industry: big fleet buyers, near-identical trucks, cyclical price wars, strong unions. Paccar, with about 20% of the North American market, serves owner-operators instead. They have little buying clout and an emotional and economic tie to their trucks, so they are less price-sensitive. Paccar tailors its whole value chain to them, earns a 10% price premium, and had been profitable for 68 years straight (Porter, 2008, p. 89). From 1993 to 2007 it earned a 31.6% return on invested capital against an industry average of 10.5% (Magretta, 2012, p. 25).

The script’s airline version: Ryanair and, historically, Southwest found a defensible position (low cost, point to point, one aircraft type) in an industry where the average firm struggles.

02 · EXPLOIT CHANGE

Claim the position a shift opens up

Analysts expected the internet to flood music with new labels. It did not, because distribution was never the real barrier: the majors pooled the risk of new artists and controlled promotion. The real shift was a substitute, illegal downloading. While the labels hesitated to sell through a rival’s platform, Apple’s iTunes (2003) became the new gatekeeper, and the majors shrank from six in 1997 to four. When structure is in flux, incumbents are often held back by their past strategies, and newcomers fill the gap (pp. 89–90).

03 · SHAPE STRUCTURE

Change the forces themselves

Either redivide profit towards the industry, by neutralising suppliers, countering buyers or powerful channels, tempering price rivalry, raising the fixed cost of entry or out-competing substitutes, or expand the pie for everyone, as soft-drink makers did by rationalising their bottlers. Sysco used private labels against supplier power and added credit, menu planning and inventory services to move competition off price; the industry consolidated (pp. 90–92).

Leaders have a special responsibility here: a better structure is a public good, and the leader usually captures most of it (p. 91).

The dark side of shaping

IBM entered personal computers late and opened its architecture to set the standard. In doing so it handed the operating system and the microprocessor to Microsoft and Intel, encouraged price rivalry and moved power to suppliers. It became the temporary leader of an industry with a lastingly poor structure (Porter, 2008, p. 92), and in 2005 sold its PC business to Lenovo (Magretta, 2012, p. 21).

Eliminating rivals is risky

Mergers meant to stop competition invite new competitors and backlash from customers and suppliers. New York’s banks consolidated through the 1980s and 1990s, yet Manhattan’s retail banking stayed as diverse as ever as new entrants arrived (Porter, 2008, p. 88).

Forecast the industry, not the year · Porter (1983), pp. 17–18

Industry evolution matters only where it changes the structural sources of competition. As minicomputer makers integrated vertically into manufacturing and software, scale economies and capital needs rose, raising barriers to entry. Structural analysis can then forecast an industry’s eventual profitability: examine each force, forecast each cause, and build a composite.

Porter’s example was solar heating: dozens, perhaps hundreds of firms, none with a major position, easy entry, all fighting to establish solar as a substitute for conventional heating. Its potential would depend on future barriers, brand identities, scale or experience in equipment, capital costs and buyer and supplier power. Run the same forecast for electric cars as they looked in 2018.

Eight ways to get the analysis wrong

Porter closes the article with a box of common pitfalls (p. 92). The eighth below comes from earlier in the article, where he calls mistaking a factor for a force a common pitfall (p. 86). The earlier deck listed six of the seven in the box; it left out paying equal attention to every force. Learn all eight, then check you can recognise them in the wild.

01 · Boundary

Defining the industry too broadly or too narrowly

02 · Lists

Making lists instead of analysing rigorously

03 · Equal weight

Giving all forces equal attention instead of digging into the important ones

04 · Effect for cause

Confusing an effect (price sensitivity) with its cause (buyer economics)

05 · Static

Static analysis that ignores industry trends

06 · Cyclical

Confusing cyclical or transient change with structural change

07 · Verdict

Declaring an industry attractive or unattractive instead of guiding choices

08 · Factor as force

Treating growth, technology, government or complements as structure

What good analysis does instead

  • Uses the full business cycle as its time horizon, usually three to five years, and looks at average profitability over it rather than any single year.
  • Quantifies: the share of the buyer’s costs the product represents, the share of industry sales needed to fill an efficient plant, the switching cost an entrant must overcome.
  • Sees the industry as a system: which forces support today’s profits, and how a shift in one triggers the others.

Porter (2008), p. 87, “Industry Analysis in Practice”.

Porter’s sequence

  1. Define the relevant industry: products and geography.
  2. Identify and segment the buyers, suppliers, competitors, substitutes and potential entrants.
  3. Assess the drivers of each force: which are strong, which weak, and why.
  4. Determine the overall structure and test it: is it consistent with actual long-run profitability? Are the more profitable players better positioned?
  5. Analyse recent and likely changes in each force.
  6. Identify what competitors, entrants or you could influence.

Porter (2008), p. 92; Magretta (2012), pp. 22–23. The exhibit workshop below follows these steps.

Supplier power · two ways to build a car

The traditional carmaker

Carmaker → Tier 1 systems (Bosch, Continental, Magna, Denso, ZF) → Tier 2 parts → Tier 3 raw materials. Ford bought 80% of its parts from 100 suppliers (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 4).

Tesla’s Model 3

Up to 80% of components in-house: battery, power electronics, drive-train, cables, displays, fuses and, after quality problems, seats. Battery packs and motors from the Gigafactory, built in partnership with Panasonic (pp. 12–13).

The earlier deck added Tesla’s later in-house cells and AI hardware, the script’s examples of the same logic. Risk and benefit are the question: control and cost against capital and execution risk.

Why incumbents did not simply copy

Dealers made 45–60% of their profits from service and parts, which electric cars need less of, and a 2016 study found dealers steering customers away from EVs (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 5–6). An incumbent selling through franchised dealers could not move to direct sales or push EVs hard without fighting its own channel. That is Porter’s “constrained by past strategies” in a sentence.

The earlier deck’s three takeaways, stated as claims to test: Tesla created a new basis for rivalry (technology, software and performance rather than fuel economy); it neutralised buyer and supplier power through direct sales and vertical integration; and it built a moat of complements with its Superchargers.

What a better analysis concludes

The structure was poor for the average firm, and the margins confirm it. But Porter is explicit that the framework can reveal an industry that is unattractive for the average entrant yet worth entering for a firm that can get past the barriers more cheaply than others, or cope with the forces better (Porter, 2008, p. 89). The right question in 2008 was therefore not “is this industry attractive?” but “is there a part of it where the forces are weaker, and can Tesla reach it at a cost incumbents cannot match?”

Porter’s 1983 note supplies the mechanism. Post-war automation and vertical integration had raised scale economies in cars until they virtually stopped successful entry (p. 7). But a scale or experience barrier can be nullified by a new technology that starts a new experience curve, on which entrants leapfrog leaders poorly placed to jump, and a leader’s commitment to scale in the old technology can cloud its view of the new one (pp. 8–9). Battery-electric drive was that new curve; whether Tesla could ride it down faster than incumbents could jump onto it was the bet.

The question the room usually splits on

Did Tesla improve the structure of the industry it entered, or start a race on a single dimension that no one wins? By 2018 GM, Ford, Toyota and BMW were all committing to electric models (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 7), and Porter warns that as an industry matures, competitors become more alike and rivalry intensifies (Porter, 2008, p. 88). The script reads the 2024–25 EV price war as exactly that zero-sum turn.

Take a side before class. You will be asked which specific evidence would change your mind; a position you cannot imagine losing is not an argument, it is a preference.

  • Suppliers: how powerful are traditional suppliers, and what are the benefits and risks of Tesla’s vertical integration?
  • Substitutes: what replaces owning a car, what does ride-hailing do to the industry, and how does Master Plan, Part Deux answer it?
  • Rivalry: what is rivalry like among traditional carmakers, how did Tesla change the basis of competition, and who are its main rivals now?
  • Bonus, complements: what are Tesla’s key complements, how has it used the Superchargers, and are tax credits a complement or a policy to trace?

Keep two claims apart: what the force predicts for the average firm, and what it predicts for Tesla’s position; a hostile industry can hold an unusually well-placed firm. In the last four minutes, swap ledgers with the next pair and challenge only the link between evidence and mechanism: “five competitors” is a fact until you say when and why they would compete prices down, and a growth forecast is not a profit forecast. Revise your weakest link.

What to hand in

One ledger line, boundary and date → structural driver → force → profit channel, with a case page or exhibit; a rating from 1 to 5; one response for management (position away from the force, anticipate a shift, or change the structure); and one fact that would overturn your diagnosis.

Round 2 · 15 min · fours

Role play: the franchise-law hearing

A state legislature must decide whether a carmaker may sell straight to consumers. Roles: Tesla’s head of retail (company stores, salaried staff, online orders); the state dealers’ association (defends franchise law); a legislator (jobs, tax base, voters’ complaints); a consumer advocate (price, choice, the buying experience). Every speaker must name the force their argument is really about. The legislator may not vote without saying which force the law changes, and for whom.

Evidence to use: dealers partially blocked Tesla’s direct sales in Connecticut, Wisconsin, Texas and Michigan; they made 45–60% of their profits from service and parts; most buyers disliked haggling and 75% would consider buying online (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 5–6, 14); dealers became a lobbying bloc in their own right (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, Exhibit 2).

What to hand in

The vote, the force it moves, and the piece of case evidence that decided it.
