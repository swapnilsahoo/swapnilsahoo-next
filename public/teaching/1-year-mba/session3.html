Industry Analysis: The Fundamentals

Why the industry you compete in sets the ceiling on what you can earn

Session 2 ran a quick five-forces read of the US auto industry before Tesla and reached the verdict a careful analyst would have reached: do not enter. This session takes the framework apart properly. Porter’s claim is that the extended rivalry of five forces, not technology, growth or regulation as such, sets an industry’s profitability over the medium and long run (Porter, 2008).

You will place the industry inside its macro-environment with PESTEL, work through what drives each force, settle the argument about a “sixth force”, learn the mistakes Porter says analysts make, and then rate the US auto industry yourself and test your rating against what carmakers actually earned.

Carried forward · Session 2

What Session 2 left open

Session 2 ended on a puzzle. Its quick five-forces read found four of five forces hostile, yet Tesla entered, survived and by August 2018 was valued above General Motors (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 11). This session does not repeat that read. It builds the tool properly and then asks whether the verdict was wrong, or the way the tool was used.

Kaplan & Norton (2008) · step 3

Analysis comes after the goal

Session 2 set purpose and a quantified goal first; external analysis is the third step, the search for the key issues a strategy must address. Tesla’s goal was to sell 500,000 cars a year by 2020 (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 12). Today asks what the industry would let it keep from those cars.

Session 2 · the quick read

“Do not enter”

Threat of entry very low, supplier power high, buyer power moderate to high, substitutes moderate and rising, rivalry intense. Keep those five verdicts in mind: you will rate the same forces below, determinant by determinant, and compare.

Session 1 · Porter (1996)

Position is a choice inside a structure

Strategy means choosing a different set of activities. Industry analysis tells you where in the industry those choices pay: which buyers, which suppliers, which threats a position has to defend against.

The trap this session sets

Session 2’s read was deliberately a list of verdicts. Porter would call that two of his common mistakes: making lists instead of analysing, and using the framework to declare an industry attractive or unattractive rather than to guide choices (Porter, 2008, p. 92). By the end of today you should be able to say what a better analysis would have concluded about Tesla, and why that conclusion is not “do not enter”.

Two readings · one strategic problem

Read with a question in mind

Who can claim the industry’s value?

Use the five forces to explain pressure on prices, costs or the investment needed to compete. A force rating needs a mechanism and evidence; counting competitors or naming famous brands is only a beginning.

Reading task: take a paragraph from the assigned article and turn it into a conditional statement: “When ___ changes, ___ gains bargaining leverage, which affects ___.” Test that statement against a dated case exhibit.

What structural feature causes the pressure?

Read the assigned structural-analysis note alongside the 2008 article. Build an evidence ledger using scale requirements, differentiation, switching costs, access to distribution, capital requirements and exit barriers. Distinguish a structural constraint from one company’s weak execution.

Reading task: choose one proposed entry barrier. Explain what an entrant must spend or change, why incumbents are advantaged, and what could erode that barrier. Use the course-pack edition for the note’s pagination.

Define the arena before scoring the forces

A conclusion about U.S. passenger-vehicle manufacturing is not automatically a conclusion about global battery production or urban mobility. State product, customer, geography and period first. A different boundary can move the same business from rival to supplier or substitute.

Bring one boundary choice you can defend and one alternative that would materially change your conclusion. Keep complements visible without treating them as an automatic extra force in Porter’s framework.

Analysis of the external environment · Rothaermel (2024), ch. 3

Start outside the industry: the macro layer

The external environment is everything outside the firm that can affect its ability to gain and keep an advantage. Rothaermel arranges it in layers, from the general environment inwards to the industry, the strategic group of close rivals and finally the firm. Leaders have little direct say over the outer layer, such as interest or exchange rates, and some say over the inner ones, such as the structure of their industry (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 83).

The general environment is scanned with PESTEL: political, economic, sociocultural, technological, ecological and legal factors. Each can be an opportunity or a threat. The model has one weakness worth remembering: it is a snapshot, so it cannot tell you which factor is moving fastest (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 90).

Porter adds the rule that links the two layers. Government, he argues, is not a sixth force, because its involvement is neither good nor bad for profitability in itself; the way to understand a policy is to trace how it changes the five forces (Porter, 2008, p. 86). Apply that to every PESTEL item: a trend matters to strategy only once you can say which force it moves, in which direction, and roughly how much.

P · Political

Pressure from government bodies, NGOs and social movements, usually before any law changes. Firms answer with nonmarket strategy: lobbying, public relations, contributions and litigation (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 83–84).

US autos: politicians at every level were keen to protect nearly 3 million auto jobs, and the dealer lobby fought Tesla’s direct sales (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 3, 14).

E · Economic

Macroeconomic conditions: growth, employment, interest rates, price stability and exchange rates (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 84–86).

US autos: sales rebounded by 2013 on higher incomes and easier credit, then stalled; they were forecast to stay flat until 2021 (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 2). When rates rose sharply in 2022, Tesla’s valuation fell more than 60% (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 15).

S · Sociocultural

A society’s culture, norms and values, including demographic trends (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 86–87).

US autos: 92% of 20–24-year-olds held a driving licence in 1983; by 2014 it was 77% (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 5).

T · Technological

The application of knowledge to create new processes and products (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 87).

US autos: battery prices fell 80% between 2010 and 2017, and an electric car has about a third as many components as a combustion car (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 4, 7).

E · Ecological

The natural environment, climate change and sustainable growth, including the externalities firms impose without paying for them (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 89).

US autos: passenger road vehicles emitted 3.64 gigatons of CO2 in 2020, the largest source in transport (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, Exhibit 1).

L · Legal

The official outcomes of political processes: laws, mandates, regulations and court decisions (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 89).

US autos: in 1990 California required 2% of cars in the state to be zero-emission by 1998, rising to 10% by 2003 (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 4).

Porter’s five forces · Porter (2008)

Industry structure, not glamour, sets the ceiling

Managers tend to define competition as the fight with today’s direct rivals. Porter’s point is that the fight for profit is wider: customers, suppliers, potential entrants and substitutes all compete for the value an industry creates. That extended rivalry is the industry’s structure (Porter, 2008, p. 79). Where the forces are intense, as in airlines, textiles and hotels, almost no company earns attractive returns; where they are benign, as in software, soft drinks and toiletries, many do (p. 80).

“Industry structure drives competition and profitability, not whether an industry produces a product or service, is emerging or mature, high tech or low tech, regulated or unregulated.”Porter (2008), p. 80

Weather and the business cycle move profits in the short run; structure sets them over the medium and long run. And the strongest force decides, which is not always the obvious one. Film makers Kodak and Fuji fought each other, but their low returns came from a substitute, digital photography (Porter, 2008, p. 80).

What makes each force strong

The determinants below are Porter’s, in paraphrase (Porter, 2008, pp. 80–86). These, not the five labels, are what you analyse. A force is a summary of its determinants.

Threat of entry

Entrants add capacity and fight for share. The threat alone caps profits: incumbents hold prices down or invest to deter. Seven barriers keep it low:

  • supply-side economies of scale;
  • demand-side benefits of scale (network effects);
  • customer switching costs;
  • capital requirements, especially unrecoverable ones such as R&D and advertising, though efficient capital markets will fund entry into an attractive industry;
  • incumbency advantages independent of size: technology, raw materials, locations, brands, experience;
  • unequal access to distribution channels;
  • restrictive government policy (which can also ease entry, through subsidies).

Plus expected retaliation: entrants hold back when incumbents have hit back before, have cash and capacity to fight, have high fixed costs, or face slow growth.

Power of suppliers

Powerful suppliers, including suppliers of labour, take value by charging more, limiting quality or shifting costs. A supplier group is powerful if it:

  • is more concentrated than the industry it sells to;
  • does not depend heavily on that industry for its revenue;
  • would cost the industry a lot to switch away from;
  • offers differentiated products;
  • has no substitute;
  • can credibly threaten to integrate forward into the industry.

Power of buyers

Two separate questions. Leverage: few or large buyers, standardised products, low switching costs, a credible threat to integrate backward. Price sensitivity: the purchase is a large share of the buyer’s costs or budget, the buyer earns low profits, and the product barely affects the buyer’s own quality or other costs.

Intermediate customers, such as distribution channels, gain extra power when they influence what end customers buy. Producers fight back with exclusive deals or by selling direct.

Threat of substitutes

A substitute does the same job by a different means. Doing without, buying used and doing it yourself all count. Substitutes are easy to overlook because they look nothing like the product. The threat is high when the substitute:

  • offers an attractive price-performance trade-off;
  • costs the buyer little to switch to.

Watch other industries: a change there can turn something into a substitute that was not one before.

Rivalry among existing competitors

Damage depends on intensity and on the basis of competition. Intensity is greatest when rivals are numerous or equal in size, growth is slow, exit barriers are high, rivals have goals beyond profit, and they misread each other’s signals.

Price wars are likeliest when products are near-identical with low switching costs, fixed costs are high and marginal costs low, capacity comes in big increments, or the product is perishable. Competing on different dimensions for different segments can be positive-sum; everyone chasing the same attributes is zero-sum.

Before you rate anything

Draw the boundary

An industry has a product scope and a geographic scope. If two products face the same buyers, suppliers, barriers and so on, they belong to one industry; if the forces differ markedly, they are separate industries needing separate strategies. Car motor oil and truck motor oil look alike but are different industries (Porter, 2008, p. 91). Ask the same of luxury and mass-market cars, or of electric and petrol cars.

Five (six) forces

The sixth force that Porter says is not a force

The course outline says “five (six)” because the two readings disagree. The textbook adds complements as a sixth force. Porter, in the same article that defines the five, argues that they are not one. Read both, then decide which is more useful for the analysis you have to do.

Rothaermel (2024), pp. 109–110

A sixth force: the strategic role of complements

A complement is a product, service or competency that adds value to the original offering when the two are used together. Complements raise demand for the primary product and so raise the profit potential of the industry and the firm. A complementor is a company whose product makes customers value yours more. Rivals can also cooperate as complementors, which Rothaermel calls co-opetition.

Porter (2008), pp. 86–87

Factors, not forces

Complements are products used together with an industry’s product, worth more combined than apart. The value of a car is higher when the driver can reach petrol stations, roadside help and insurance. They matter when they shift demand, but strong complements are not necessarily good or bad for profitability, so they are not a sixth force. They work through the five, and the analyst’s job is to trace their effect on each.

The two positions are closer than they look. Rothaermel stresses that complements can grow demand; Porter accepts that and insists you then ask who captures the extra value. This page uses Porter’s test because it forces that second question.

Four things that look like forces and are not

Porter calls mistaking a visible attribute of an industry for its structure a common pitfall (Porter, 2008, p. 86). Each card gives his reason and an example from the cases.

Industry growth

Growth mutes rivalry, but it can strengthen suppliers and pull in entrants. Fast growth guarantees nothing if buyers are powerful or substitutes attractive.

US EV sales grew 25% in 2017 to about 200,000 (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 6). The growth drew GM (20 EV models planned by 2023) and Ford ($11 billion for 24 hybrids and 16 EVs by 2022) into the segment (p. 7).

Technology

Advanced technology does not by itself make an industry attractive. Dull industries with high barriers or price-insensitive buyers often out-earn exciting ones.

Connected-car revenue was expected to nearly triple, from $53 billion in 2017 to $156 billion in 2022, yet PwC warned carmakers could become “dumb pipes on wheels” (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 9). Who captures the value?

Government

Trace each policy. Patents raise entry barriers; pro-union rules raise supplier power; bankruptcy rules that let failing firms reorganise rather than exit keep excess capacity in play.

GM and Chrysler went bankrupt in 2009; neither left the market, and none of the Big Three did even when capacity utilisation fell below 33% (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 3). That is rivalry, set by policy.

Complements

They can raise or lower entry barriers, ease or block substitution, and soften rivalry by raising switching costs or sharpen it by neutralising differentiation.

Porter’s own example: needing suitable fuelling stations makes it hard for alternative-fuel cars to substitute for conventional ones (Porter, 2008, p. 87). See EV charging, traced below.

Industry analysis in practice · Porter (2008), pp. 86–92

Eight ways to get the analysis wrong

Porter closes the article with a box of common pitfalls (p. 92). The eighth below comes from earlier in the article, where he calls mistaking a factor for a force a common pitfall (p. 86). Learn them before you rate the auto industry, then use the spotter to check you can recognise them in the wild.

01 · Boundary

Defining the industry too broadly or too narrowly

02 · Lists

Making lists instead of analysing rigorously

03 · Equal weight

Giving all forces equal attention instead of digging into the important ones

04 · Effect for cause

Confusing an effect (price sensitivity) with its cause (buyer economics)

05 · Static

Static analysis that ignores industry trends

06 · Cyclical

Confusing cyclical or transient change with structural change

07 · Verdict

Declaring an industry attractive or unattractive instead of guiding choices

08 · Factor as force

Treating growth, technology, government or complements as structure

What good analysis does instead

  • Uses the full business cycle as its time horizon, usually three to five years, and looks at average profitability over it rather than any single year.
  • Quantifies: the share of the buyer’s costs the product represents, the share of industry sales needed to fill an efficient plant, the switching cost an entrant must overcome.
  • Sees the industry as a system: which forces support today’s profits, and how a shift in one triggers the others.

Porter (2008), p. 87, “Industry Analysis in Practice”.

Porter’s sequence

  1. Define the relevant industry: products and geography.
  2. Identify and segment the buyers, suppliers, competitors, substitutes and potential entrants.
  3. Assess the drivers of each force: which are strong, which weak, and why.
  4. Determine the overall structure and test it: is it consistent with actual long-run profitability? Are the more profitable players better positioned?
  5. Analyse recent and likely changes in each force.
  6. Identify what competitors, entrants or you could influence.

Porter (2008), p. 92. The Tesla section below follows these steps.

What a better analysis concludes

The structure was poor for the average firm, and the margins confirm it. But Porter is explicit that the framework can reveal an industry that is unattractive for the average entrant yet worth entering for a firm that can get past the barriers more cheaply than others, or cope with the forces better (Porter, 2008, p. 89). The right question in 2008 was therefore not “is this industry attractive?” but “is there a part of it where the forces are weaker, and can Tesla reach it at a cost incumbents cannot match?” The next section is about that question.

Implications for strategy · Porter (2008), pp. 88–92

From analysis to strategy: three things to do with the forces

Every company should know its industry’s average profitability and how it is changing; the forces explain why it is what it is. Porter then offers three ways to act on that understanding, and says the best strategies use more than one (Porter, 2008, pp. 88–89).

01 · POSITION

Find where the forces are weakest

Heavy trucks are a hard industry: big fleet buyers, near-identical trucks, cyclical price wars, strong unions. Paccar, with about 20% of the North American market, serves owner-operators instead. They have little buying clout and care about their trucks, so they are less price-sensitive. Paccar tailors its whole value chain to them, earns a 10% price premium, and had been profitable for 68 years straight (Porter, 2008, p. 89).

02 · EXPLOIT CHANGE

Claim the position a shift opens up

Analysts expected the internet to flood music with new labels. It did not, because distribution was never the real barrier. The real shift was a substitute, illegal downloading. While the major labels hesitated to sell through a rival’s platform, Apple’s iTunes (2003) became the new gatekeeper. When structure is in flux, incumbents are often held back by their past strategies, and newcomers fill the gap (pp. 89–90).

03 · SHAPE STRUCTURE

Change the forces themselves

Either redivide profit towards the industry, by neutralising suppliers, countering buyers or powerful channels, tempering price rivalry, raising the fixed cost of entry or out-competing substitutes, or expand the pie for everyone. Sysco used private labels against supplier power and added services to move competition off price (pp. 90–91).

The dark side of shaping

IBM entered personal computers late and opened its architecture to set the standard. In doing so it handed the operating system and the microprocessor to Microsoft and Intel, encouraged price rivalry and moved power to suppliers. It became the temporary leader of an industry with a lastingly poor structure (Porter, 2008, p. 92). Moves that win share today can wreck the structure you will compete in tomorrow.

Why incumbents did not simply copy

Dealers made 45–60% of their profits from service and parts, which electric cars need less of, and a 2016 study found dealers steering customers away from EVs (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 5–6). An incumbent selling through franchised dealers could not move to direct sales or push EVs hard without fighting its own channel. That is Porter’s “constrained by past strategies” in a sentence.

The question to hold open

By 2018 GM, Ford, Toyota and BMW were all committing to electric models (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 7). Porter warns that as an industry matures, competitors become more alike and rivalry intensifies (Porter, 2008, p. 88). Did Tesla improve the structure of the industry it entered, or start a race on a single dimension that no one wins? Session 4 gives you the tools to model the rivals’ responses.

Readings for Session 3

Readings and cases are available through the course pack.

Required · the framework

Porter, M. E. (2008). The five competitive forces that shape strategy. Harvard Business Review, 86(1), 78–93.

Read it all, including the boxes on industry analysis in practice, defining the relevant industry, and the typical steps and common pitfalls.

Required · the case

Serafeim, G., & Migdal, A. (2024). Elon Musk at Tesla (HBS No. 9-123-044, rev. June 27, 2024). Harvard Business School Publishing.

Supplementary · industry companion

Sull, D., & Reavis, C. (2019). Tesla’s entry into the U.S. auto industry (No. 18-186). MIT Sloan School of Management.

The assigned Tesla case continues from Session 2. For additional industry context, consult Sull & Reavis pp. 1–10, the industry chapters, with the five forces in hand. Sull & Reavis is licensed CC BY-NC-ND 3.0.

Required · the original note

Porter, M. E. (1983). Note on the structural analysis of industries (HBS No. 376-054). Harvard Business School.

Porter’s earlier, longer treatment of the same framework. This page draws on the 2008 article, his own update of it.

Supplementary · textbook

Rothaermel, F. T. (2024). Strategic management (6th ed.). McGraw Hill. Chapter 3, sections 3.1–3.2.

The source of the PESTEL definitions on this page and of the case for complements as a sixth force.

Carried forward

Kaplan, R. S., & Norton, D. P., with Barrows, E. A., Jr. (2008). Developing the strategy: Vision, value gaps, and analysis. Balanced Scorecard Report, 10(1), 3–7.

Porter, M. E. (1996). What is strategy? Harvard Business Review, 74(6), 61–78.
