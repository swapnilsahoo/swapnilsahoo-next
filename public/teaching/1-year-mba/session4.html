Beyond the Five Forces

Ecosystems, business models and the game your rivals play

Every firm sits in a web of players who create value with it and then fight it for a share: customers, suppliers, rivals, and the firms whose products make yours worth more. This session maps that web, asks how a firm can change the game being played in it, and asks what a strategist should do when no forecast can say how the game will end.

Session 3 rated the US auto industry with Porter’s five forces and ended on a loose thread: complements. Rothaermel counts them as a sixth force; Porter says they work through the five. Then Musk said Tesla was “open” to other carmakers using its Superchargers if they shared the cost (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 14). The moment a rival can also be a customer of your complement, five boxes stop being enough. Today you draw the whole game instead.

Question 01 · Brandenburger & Nalebuff (1995)

Who else shapes the value Tesla can capture?

Brandenburger and Nalebuff begin with a complaint about language. Business borrows its words from war and sport, and both mislead: business is not about winning and losing. Firms can do spectacularly well without anyone else failing, and they can fail however well they play if they are playing the wrong game (1995, p. 57). The game, in their words, “is all about value: creating it and capturing it” (p. 59).

To see who shapes that value, they draw a map they call the value net. On the vertical axis are the firm’s customers and suppliers: resources flow up from suppliers, products flow down to customers, and money flows the other way. On the horizontal axis are players the firm interacts with but does not transact with: substitutors and complementors (p. 59).

The second idea is added value. Take the value created when everyone is in the game, remove one player, and see how much value the rest can still create. The difference is that player’s added value, and in a game without fixed rules no player can take away more than its added value (p. 58). Profit, in other words, follows added value, not value.

CUSTOMERS

Buy the firm’s products. With suppliers they form the vertical axis, where firms cooperate to create the pie and compete to divide it (p. 60).

SUPPLIERS

Provide labour, materials and other resources. Customers and suppliers are symmetric: any strategy towards one has a counterpart towards the other (p. 71).

SUBSTITUTORS

Alternatives from whom customers may buy, or to whom suppliers may sell. Coca-Cola and Pepsi are substitutors for drinkers; Coca-Cola and Tyson Foods are substitutors for carbon dioxide, which one uses to carbonate and the other to freeze chickens (p. 60).

COMPLEMENTORS

Players whose products make customers value yours more, or make suppliers keener to supply you. Faster chips and more powerful software raise each other’s value. American and United are complementors to Boeing: each one’s order helps the other’s plane get built (p. 60).

Why new words

Call a player a “competitor” and you think only about fighting it. “Substitutor” names the relationship without that bias, and complementors, its natural counterpart, are the players traditional analysis most often overlooks (p. 60). One player can hold several roles at once: American and United compete for passengers and complement each other when they buy planes.

The half most managers miss

Managers see substitutors only as enemies and complementors only as friends. Both pictures are half right. A substitutor can be a partner, as GM’s credit card showed Ford, and a complementor can be squeezed, as 3DO squeezed the hardware makers who built its game console (pp. 58, 60–61, 63). Question 2 returns to both stories.

Where Session 3 stopped, and why the value net starts there

Session 3 · five (six) forces

Complements raise value. Who keeps it?

Rothaermel counts complements as a sixth force; Porter says they act through the five. Both agree a complement can grow demand. Neither says how the extra value is split between Tesla and the firms whose products make a Tesla worth more. Added value is the tool for that split.

Session 3 · the hand-off

A rival that could also be a customer

If other carmakers paid to use Tesla’s Superchargers (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 14), they would be substitutors for car buyers and customers of the charging network at the same time. A five-forces diagram can only draw them as rivalry. The value net can draw both roles.

Grant (2018) · the critique

Structure without moves

The central criticism of the five forces, Grant writes, is that they ignore competitive interaction: rivalry becomes a variable linking structure to profit, not a sequence of decisions by firms that anticipate each other (p. 91). And industry factors explain less than 20% of the variation in firms’ return on assets (p. 84). Industry analysis still matters; it is not the whole picture.

Value is not added value: Nintendo and the Game Boy

Cars, televisions and video recorders create far more value in the world than Game Boys. Yet between July 1990 and June 1991 Nintendo’s average stock-market value, 2.4 trillion yen, was higher than Sony’s (2.2 trillion) or Nissan’s (2.0 trillion). Creating value is not enough; profit comes from capturing it, and Nintendo captured a giant slice of a largish pie by keeping its own added value high and every other player’s low (Brandenburger & Nalebuff, 1995, pp. 65–66).

  • Customers. Retailers such as Toys R Us and Wal-Mart were concentrated, so Nintendo did not fill every order: 33 million cartridges in 1988 against demand for 45 million.
  • Complementors. A security chip and a limited number of licences kept outside game developers plentiful and replaceable.
  • Suppliers. Old-generation chips made chip suppliers a commodity; once Mario was a hit, licensed characters mattered less.
  • Substitutors. The largest installed base, plus exclusivity from developers, left rival consoles no added value to offer (p. 65).

The two articles, and the textbook behind them

One question each

Brandenburger and Nalebuff answer who shapes the value and how to change the game. Courtney, Kirkland and Viguerie answer what to do when the game’s end cannot be forecast. Each tab rebuilds the author’s central argument so you can work it rather than read it. The last two tabs connect the readings and show what Grant’s textbook chapter adds.

Added value, played as a card game

The article’s central argument fits in one classroom game. Adam holds 26 black cards; each of his 26 students holds one red card. The dean pays $100 for every red card matched with a black one. Without Adam there is no game, so his added value is the whole $2,600. Without any one student, one match is lost, so each student’s added value is $100. The added values sum to $5,200, but there is only $2,600 to divide, so nobody gets all of theirs; by symmetry each side expects about half, $50 a card (Brandenburger & Nalebuff, 1995, p. 64).

Then Adam publicly burns three of his black cards. The pie shrinks to $2,300, but three students will now go unmatched, so no single student is essential and each one’s added value falls to zero. Adam can offer a 90:10 split and look generous (pp. 64–65). Burn cards below, and then let the students bargain as one.

Two principles sit under the game. In games with rules, look forward and reason backward: play out every reaction to your move, including your own reply to theirs. In games without rules, you cannot take away more than you bring. Both demand allocentrism: thinking from inside the other players’ heads, and asking what you bring to them rather than what they bring to you (p. 58).

Uncertainty is not binary

Courtney, Kirkland and Viguerie see one error behind most strategy made under uncertainty: treating it as binary. Either the world is certain enough for a point forecast, which makes managers bury the uncertainty inside their cash flows, or it is unpredictable, which tempts them to drop analysis and act on gut instinct, or to freeze. All three responses are dangerous (1997, p. 3).

Their alternative starts from what can be known. Some trends are clear; many unknowns are knowable with the right analysis. What is left after the best possible analysis is residual uncertainty, and it falls into one of four levels (p. 3). The level then decides the tools, the posture and the moves.

  1. LEVEL 1A clear-enough future: one forecast points one way.
  2. LEVEL 2Alternate futures: a few discrete outcomes.
  3. LEVEL 3A range of futures: a continuum, no natural scenarios.
  4. LEVEL 4True ambiguity: not even the range can be drawn.

In the authors’ experience at least half of all strategy problems sit at levels 2 or 3 and most of the rest at level 1 (p. 5). Question 3 works through each level.

Their four-step method · p. 12

The article works one example end to end, a regional bank facing electronic commerce, in four steps. Run the same steps on a Tesla decision before you open the answer.

2022: should Tesla commit to Optimus, the humanoid robot Musk said would outgrow the car business, selling millions at under $20,000 each (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 15)?

  1. 1. Identify the residual uncertainties and their level.
  2. 2. Choose a posture: shape, adapt, or reserve the right to play.
  3. 3. Build a portfolio of actions: big bets, options, no-regrets moves.
  4. 4. Manage it actively: name the trigger events, review on a short cycle.

1. Product, customers, rivals and technology are all undefined and they interact: level 4 for the market as a whole, with level 3 pockets (unit cost, say) that can be bounded.

2. Level 4 is where, paradoxically, a shaper may face higher returns and lower risk, because no one yet knows the best strategy (Courtney et al., 1997, p. 13). A shaping posture is defensible if Tesla has the credibility to coordinate suppliers and developers around its vision.

3. Cheap options first (prototypes, pilots in Tesla’s own plants), no-regrets moves (skills in AI and actuators that also serve the cars), and no big bet until the uncertainty moves towards level 3.

4. Re-evaluate the options whenever a major uncertainty clears, at least every six months (p. 14). Triggers: unit cost, a first paying customer, a rival’s prototype.

One reading of the case, not the case’s own analysis.

Reading task. Do not attach a precise probability just because a spreadsheet asks for one. A small investment is not automatically an option: it must buy a meaningful future choice. Write one observable signpost that would make a move expand, stop or change.

A game-changing move is also an uncertain bet

The two articles were written two years apart and point at each other. Courtney’s sidebar on a fuller strategy tool kit names game theory as the way to understand uncertainty that comes from competitors’ conduct, and lists Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s article as the place to start (Courtney et al., 1997, p. 7). Their summary of the four levels puts game theory among the tools for level 2, where a few discrete outcomes depend on what rivals decide (pp. 5–6).

Same move, two lenses

Opening a charging network to rivals changes the players (B&N) and is also a bet on demand that nobody can forecast (Courtney). Write both sides: the PART you change, and the level of uncertainty that governs how much to commit.

Tactics are about uncertainty

Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s tactics either lift the fog or thicken it (1995, p. 69). Courtney’s shapers at levels 3 and 4 need credibility to coordinate others around their preferred outcome more than deep pockets (1997, p. 13). Both describe managing what other players believe.

A persuasive move needs a reply

Before committing, name the most plausible response of the player your move hurts, and the way you will learn whether it is coming. Then carry the logic into the payoff game, the competitor profile and the uncertainty exercises below.

Ecosystems · Grant (2018), ch. 4 · Further

Complements, ecosystems and who captures the value

Grant starts from the asymmetry the value net builds in. A substitute lowers the value of a product; a complement raises it. A printer is useless without ink cartridges, a car without fuel. Once you accept that, the strategic question is no longer whether complements matter but how the value they create is shared between the firms that supply the pieces (Grant, 2018, p. 86). The value net names the players; this section asks which pieces are worth owning.

01 · Complements

The split decides the profit

In 1990s video games, independent developers supplied most of the software and most of the consumer value, yet Nintendo took most of the system’s profit because it controlled the operating system and the making of cartridges. In personal computers the opposite happened: IBM’s open architecture let Windows become the proprietary standard, and the PC drifted towards a commodity (p. 86).

Grant’s rule: keep your own product monopolised, differentiated and in short supply, and encourage competition, commoditisation and excess capacity among your complements (p. 87). Apple’s ownership of iOS lets it choose which apps reach the iPhone and take 30% of their revenue; Nestlé lost control of Nespresso capsules once its patents stopped protecting them.

02 · Ecosystems

Value migrates; look for the bottleneck

A business ecosystem is the community of organisations, institutions and individuals that affect the enterprise, bound by codependence and always evolving. Value moves between its parts with technology, regulation and customer preferences, and firms can push it. The prize is a bottleneck: an activity that creates significant customer value and that the firm can dominate (Grant, 2018, p. 88, drawing on Jacobides).

  • Be the guardian of quality (Intel Inside).
  • Be irreplaceable; keep others replaceable (Apple’s “supply chain atomisation”).
  • Ride shifts in customer needs.
  • Redefine the value chain (IKEA moved assembly to the customer).

03 · Platforms, in brief

When the interface is the business

In digital systems, competition is often between platforms, the interfaces that link the parts. Complementors favour the platform with the most users and users favour the one with the most complements; that loop is the main source of winner-take-all markets (Grant, 2018, pp. 85–87).

The course’s earlier platforms deck put the contrast simply: a pipeline designs, makes and sells (Bajaj Auto, Godrej Appliances); a platform connects producers with consumers (Uber, Airbnb, Flipkart, Zomato). Two-sided platforms are Session 8’s topic, with Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne (2006) and the NVIDIA case.

The value-capture test · Grant (2018), pp. 90–91

How much would the network lose if you left it?

Grant’s value-capture model, which he traces to work by Brandenburger and Stuart, puts bounds on what a firm can take from its network. The ceiling is the value the network would lose if the firm walked away: Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s added value. The floor is what the firm could add to an alternative network. Investments with competitive intent raise the value the firm adds; actions with persuasive intent change how much the others are willing to concede. Ask it of Tesla and of a battery maker: which one does the other need more?

Business models · Grant (2018); Rothaermel (2024) · Further

The business model: how a firm gets paid inside its ecosystem

A model is a simplified description of something real, so a business model is a simplified description of a business: its core logic for creating value. Grant quotes David Teece’s definition, “the manner by which the business enterprise delivers value to customers, entices customers to pay for value, and converts those payments to profit” (Grant, 2018, p. 88). He leaves open whether a business model is more than a strategy under another name, and uses it for one job: designing how a firm exploits the opportunities in its ecosystem (pp. 88–89).

Rothaermel puts it more bluntly: the business model is where strategy turns into action, and it explains how the firm intends to make money. He asks four questions of any model: why it creates value (its revenue and cost models), what activities deliver the offer, who performs them, and how the activities are linked (2024, pp. 476–477). In value-net terms, the who is the question that matters here: which activities the firm keeps, and which it leaves to customers, suppliers and complementors.

The catalogue of business-model types (razor and blades, subscription, freemium, the long tail and the rest) belongs to Session 13. This session keeps one question: where in the ecosystem does the money get captured?

Question 02 · Brandenburger & Nalebuff (1995)

How should a firm play, and change, the game its rivals play?

Brandenburger and Nalebuff open with the US car industry Session 3 analysed. In the early 1990s, year-end rebates and dealer discounts were ruining its profits: when one maker cleared stock with incentives the others had to follow, and buyers learned to wait for them (1995, p. 58). In September 1992 GM and Household Bank launched a credit card that let holders put 5% of their spending towards a new GM car, up to $500 a year and $3,500 in all. There were 1.2 million accounts after a month and 8.7 million after two years.

The card won customers from Ford, a win–lose move. It also replaced GM’s other incentives, which raised the price a non-cardholder paid for a GM car. That gave Ford room to raise its prices, which let GM raise its own: a win–win (p. 58). When Ford and Volkswagen copied the card, they paid for it by cutting their other incentives, so GM’s customers faced higher prices elsewhere. Imitation helped GM (p. 59).

That is coopetition: looking for win–win as well as win–lose moves. Win–lose moves such as price cuts for share often backfire; once matched, they leave everyone where they started at lower prices (p. 59). To change a game, change one or more of its five elements, PARTS (p. 61).

Why win–win is worth looking for · p. 59

  1. It is relatively unexplored, so there are more opportunities to find.
  2. Others give up no ground, so they resist less.
  3. It does not force retaliation, so the new game lasts.
  4. Imitation of a win–win move helps you instead of hurting you.

Did GM plan all this, or get lucky with a loyalty programme? The authors cannot say. Their point is that the stakes are too high to leave to chance (p. 59).

P · PLAYERS

None is fixed, yourself included. Coke and Pepsi encouraged the Holland Sweetener Company to build an aspartame plant, then signed new long-term contracts with Monsanto just before the US NutraSweet patent expired, saving about $200 million a year (pp. 61–62). HSC’s mistake: it should have asked to be paid to play, up front.

A · ADDED VALUES

Raise yours or lower others’. TWA took 5 to 40 seats out of each plane in 1993 and went to first place in long-haul customer satisfaction (p. 64). Softsoap’s maker ordered 100 million pumps, both suppliers’ whole output, buying a 12–18 month head start (p. 66).

R · RULES

From law, custom, practicality or contract. Kiwi International promised to take no more than 10% of any market, so the majors gained more by accommodating it than by fighting it: judo economics (p. 67). Meet-the-competition clauses let carbon-dioxide producers earn more than their added value (pp. 67–68).

T · TACTICS

Moves that shape how others perceive the game: lifting the fog or thickening it. Murdoch announced a 25-cent New York Post and tested it on Staten Island, where sales doubled; the Daily News then raised its own price to 50 cents (p. 69).

S · SCOPE

Games are linked across markets and over time. Sega launched a 16-bit console; Nintendo took two years to answer, because a cheaper 16-bit market would have eaten the value of its 8-bit franchise (p. 70).

Tesla · a published sequence of moves

The 2006 master plan announced Tesla’s sequence in advance: a sports car, then an affordable car, then a cheaper one, plus zero-emission power (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 5). Session 2 read it as purpose. Read it now as a tactic: whose perception of the game was it meant to change, and was it lifting the fog or thickening it?

Five traps · pp. 70–71

Accepting the game you find. Assuming a change must come at others’ expense. Believing you must do something no one else can. Failing to see the whole game, complementors above all. And failing to think methodically, from the other players’ side. There is no end to the game of changing the game: others are changing it too.

Game theory · Grant (2018), pp. 91–95 · Further

The formal tools behind “look forward, reason backward”

A strategic situation is one of interdependence: what each player should do depends on what it expects the others to do. Game theory gives that situation a structure by asking four questions: who the players are, what options each has, what each player earns from every combination of options, and in what sequence the decisions are made (Grant, 2018, p. 91). Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s PARTS are a manager’s version of the same structure; Grant supplies the vocabulary for solving a game once it is drawn.

Sequence · simultaneous games

Choose without seeing the other’s choice

When players commit before they can observe each other, as with capacity booked a year ahead, you reason by best responses: for each move the rival might make, what is my best reply? A pair of moves from which neither player can gain by changing alone is a Nash equilibrium (Grant, 2018, p. 93). It need not be good for either player.

Sequence · sequential games

Move first, and reason backwards

When the second mover sees the first move, the first mover works backwards: for each move I could make, how will the rival reply, and which of those outcomes do I prefer? This is Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s first principle (1995, p. 58). Moving first is worth something only if the move is visible and hard to undo. A price cut that can be reversed next quarter is not a first move; it is an announcement.

The prisoners’ dilemma · p. 93

Grant’s example: Coca-Cola and Pepsi each choose a big or small advertising budget. Both would earn more with small budgets, but each fears the other will exploit its restraint, so both spend big. The equilibrium is worse for both, and any agreement to restrain is unstable because each gains by cheating. Two escapes: turn a one-off game into a repeated one, or change the payoffs through deterrence.

Look for the same structure in the cases. Charging stations were unprofitable to build, and stakeholders argued over who should pay; BMW and Nissan chose to back one shared fast-charging network, EVgo, open to every EV driver (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 7). Which escape is that?

Five ways to change the outcome · pp. 91–94

Cooperation
Co-opetition, Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s term: every relationship mixes the two. Coke and Pepsi still cooperate on policy for soda sales in schools.
Deterrence
Impose costs on moves you do not want. Only credible threats deter: a price war often hurts the incumbent more than the entrant; excess capacity is more convincing.
Commitment
Eliminate your own options so that others believe you. Airbus advertised the A380 heavily before the design was finished.
Changing the game
In June 2014 Tesla offered its patents to competitors, betting that a bigger EV market would outweigh any lost advantage (p. 94).
Signalling
Selective communication meant to shape how rivals read your moves. Threats are credible when backed by reputation; preannounced price changes can also invite cooperation. Compare Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s tactics.

Tesla · commitment and signal

Panasonic agreed to fund 30–40% of the $5 billion Gigafactory 1 (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 9): a visible, sunk, shared investment that is hard to walk back. The case also reports one reading of Musk’s 2018 pay plan as a signal rather than pay: “They were about signaling a credible commitment to Tesla’s purpose: to become a clean energy giant” (p. 10). Does a pay plan remove any options? A factory does.

How far to trust it · pp. 94–95

Game theory is rigorous, but it gives clear predictions only in stylised situations; with realistic detail it often yields no equilibrium or several. Grant judges it better at explaining the past than predicting the future. Its use is to structure the situation and show how to change the game. Commitment returns in Session 7, with Ghemawat and Pisano on sustaining superior performance.

Competitor analysis · Porter’s framework, in Grant (2018), pp. 95–98

Competitor analysis: get inside the rival’s head

Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s demand to be allocentric, to put yourself in the shoes and even the heads of the other players (1995, p. 58), needs a method. In a concentrated industry the behaviour of a few close rivals dominates the environment. Game theory supplies the formal apparatus, but Grant argues that for most decisions a less formal, evidence-based prediction of what rivals will do is enough (2018, p. 95). Competitive intelligence serves three purposes: forecast rivals’ strategies, predict their reactions to your moves, and work out how to influence their behaviour. The boundary with industrial espionage is not always clear (pp. 95–96).

The problem is usually too much information, not too little. Michael Porter’s four-part framework says what to look for (Grant, 2018, p. 96). Each part feeds the same two predictions: what will the competitor do next, and how will it respond to us?

Allocentrism in practice

Kiwi had to see the game from the major airlines’ side to pick a capacity they would accommodate; BellSouth turned a weak hand into a payment because it understood what Lin and McCaw wanted (Brandenburger & Nalebuff, 1995, p. 71). A competitor profile is the same discipline written down.

01

Future goals

Financial or market goals? A rival chasing share fights harder than one chasing profit, and one free of profit discipline, such as a state-owned firm, is hardest of all. The further performance falls short of its goals, the likelier a change of strategy, perhaps of management (p. 97).

02

Current strategy

How is it competing now? Look at what the company says and at what it does, and reconcile the two (p. 96). A forecast of change starts from an accurate picture of the present.

03

Assumptions

What its managers believe about the industry and about themselves. These beliefs are stable and converge across an industry into a shared “recipe” that can blind a whole industry. British and US motorcycle makers dismissed light Japanese bikes in the 1960s; by 1980 BSA and Triumph had stopped production and Harley-Davidson was struggling to survive (p. 97).

04

Resources & capabilities

Strengths and weaknesses decide how serious a challenge or a reply can be. Do not start a price war with a rival sitting on a cash pile; aim at weaknesses it will find hard to answer (p. 98). Session 5 builds the tools for this part.

Question 03 · Courtney, Kirkland & Viguerie (1997)

What should a strategist do when the future cannot be forecast?

The article opens on two firms betting on digital photography in opposite ways: Kodak spending $500 million a year on digital products, Hewlett-Packard $50 million a year on a rival vision built around home photo printers. Most companies, the authors note, lack the position, assets or appetite for risk to make such industry-shaping bets work; others hedge with small investments that may reserve the right to play or only the right to lose; others wait and see, and open a window for competitors (Courtney et al., 1997, p. 2).

The level of residual uncertainty decides which tools work. The standard kit of market research, competitors’ cost and capacity, the value chain, Porter’s five forces and a discounted-cash-flow model suits level 1 (p. 6). It misleads above it. One of history’s great underestimates makes the point: in 1977 Digital Equipment’s president said there was no reason for anyone to have a computer at home, when the personal-computer boom was already within the range experts were discussing (p. 3).

LEVEL 1

A clear-enough future

One forecast is precise enough to point to a single direction; the residual uncertainty does not change the decision. An airline answering a low-cost entrant at its hub can learn segment sizes, the entrant’s costs and capacity, and its objectives (pp. 3–4).

Tools: the standard kit and a DCF (p. 6).

LEVEL 2

Alternate futures

A few discrete outcomes; analysis can suggest probabilities but not pick the winner, and the best strategy depends on which occurs. Pending legislation is one source; another is a rival’s capacity decision in an oligopoly such as pulp and paper (p. 4).

Tools: a valuation model per scenario, decision analysis, game theory, and the trigger variables that signal which future is coming (pp. 5–7).

LEVEL 3

A range of futures

The outcome can fall anywhere on a continuum bounded by a few key variables, with no natural scenarios: a consumer-goods firm entering India might face customer penetration anywhere between 10% and 30%, or the cost of an unproven technology (p. 4).

Tools: no more than four or five scenarios, each distinct, spanning the probable range; test robustness (p. 7).

LEVEL 4

True ambiguity

Several uncertainties interact so that not even the range can be identified. Rare, and transitional: Russia in 1992, consumer multimedia in the 1990s. Such situations drift towards level 3 or 2 (pp. 4–5).

Tools: list what is known and knowable, analogies, indicators to track, and what you would have to believe (pp. 7–8).

Three postures · pp. 8–9

  • Shape. Drive the industry towards a new structure of your own devising, by shaking up a stable industry or by steering a volatile one.
  • Adapt. Take the structure and its evolution as given; win by choosing a position, or by recognising and answering developments fast.
  • Reserve the right to play. A special form of adapting, only at levels 2–4: incremental investments now that give a privileged position in information, cost or relationships, so you can wait until uncertainty falls.

Three kinds of move · pp. 9–10

  • Big bets. Large commitments, such as major capital investments or acquisitions, that pay off big in some scenarios and lose big in others. Shapers usually need them.
  • Options. Modest first investments that secure the upside and limit the downside: pilots, limited joint ventures, licences on an alternative technology.
  • No-regrets moves. Moves that pay off whatever happens: cost cuts, competitive intelligence, skills, and sometimes even capacity or market entry.

A shaper raises uncertainty

FedEx entered a stable, level 1 delivery industry and created level 3 uncertainty for itself and level 2 for UPS: would overnight delivery work, and would UPS have to match it? Its big bets took it to the brink of bankruptcy in its first two years, then reshaped the industry (pp. 10–11).

Shapers can still adapt

Microsoft’s proprietary MSN network was a big bet on one of two futures. When trigger variables showed that open networks would win, it refocused MSN on the internet. Postures are not carved in stone (p. 11).

Credibility can replace cash

At levels 3 and 4 a shaper needs the credibility to coordinate other players around its preferred outcome more than deep pockets. Netscape shaped browser standards on its leaders’ credibility (p. 13).

The right to play, or to lose?

At level 4 it is hard to tell whether small investments reserve the right to play or only the right to lose. Prefer cheap options, and re-evaluate them whenever an important uncertainty clears, at least every six months (pp. 13–14).

The trap this session sets

Tesla’s history is easy to read as a string of brilliant moves, because we know how it turned out. Courtney’s levels and Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s demand to see the game from the other players’ side both force the harder question: what did each move look like before anyone knew how the game would end, and what did the other players believe at the time?

The pattern

Tesla’s portfolio is a shaper’s. The early moves were options and no-regrets moves that kept it alive and taught it the market; the big bets went into the ecosystem pieces that passed the bottleneck test: charging and battery supply. Note when the biggest bets came. Tesla bought SolarCity in November 2016, the year it ended with $1.4 billion of free cash while burning about $500 million a quarter (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 10). FedEx’s big bets took it to the brink in its first two years (Courtney et al., 1997, pp. 10–11).

The incumbents’ portfolio

By 2018 the incumbents were buying positions as well as cars: GM a 9% stake in Lyft, Toyota talks with Panasonic on a battery joint venture, BMW a $1.1 billion contract with CATL for a battery factory in Europe (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 7, 10). Which of these reserve the right to play, and which only the right to lose? What trigger variable would tell you, and how often would you look?

The question the room usually splits on

Was Tesla a game maker or a game taker? One reading: Tesla changed the players (no dealers), raised its added value at the bottlenecks (cells, charging, software) and shaped a level 1 industry into a new one. The other: governments changed the rules for Tesla, with tax credits, emissions limits and combustion-car bans, and much of its early profit was a regulatory credit paid by other carmakers. Both readings fit the evidence, and they imply opposite things about durability. Added value survives a change in the rules; a rule can be rewritten by others.

Take a side before class. The exhibit workshop below gives you the numbers; you will be asked which figure would change your mind.

Finding 1

The colleague is right about 2019

Credits were close to pure profit, and in 2019 they were more than the whole operating profit: 2.4 points against an EBIT margin of 0.3%. In 2020 they were 5.0 of its 6.2 points. Rivals paid for Tesla’s first profits.

Finding 2

Wrong about 2021

Without credits, Tesla’s 9.4% EBIT margin still ranks second of seven, and its 23.2% gross margin first. By 2021 most of the profit was earned on cars. A P/E of 216 against 8.7 is a bet on far more than either reading.

Finding 3

The complements are costs

Service lost money every year and energy slid below zero. Tesla captured value on the car and the credit, not on the complements around it, which it ran as costs of selling the car.

What the numbers do and do not settle

Read the credits through the value net. Tesla sells them to other firms regulated under the same vehicle-emissions rules (Tesla, 2022), which makes the carmakers that buy them Tesla’s substitutors in the car market and its customers in the credit market. Brandenburger and Nalebuff would ask what Tesla’s added value is in that second game. It is high while rivals sell few EVs of their own, and it falls as their own EV sales let them meet the rules themselves. A rule, they warn, can be rewritten by others.

What the exhibits cannot settle: whether credits really carry no operating cost (the EBIT figures above assume so), whether the losses on service were a deliberate subsidy to sell cars or simple inefficiency, and how long the credit market lasts. Those are Courtney questions: name the level of uncertainty before you value the stock.
