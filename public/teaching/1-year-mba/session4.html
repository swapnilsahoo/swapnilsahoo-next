Beyond the Five Forces

Ecosystems, business models, and rivals who move back

Session 3 used the five forces to ask how much of the value an industry creates its members get to keep. The framework holds the rest of the world still: suppliers supply, buyers buy, and rivals are a level of intensity. Tesla does not sit still in those boxes. Its battery supplier was also an investor, it built its own charging network in an industry where building and running stations was unprofitable, and the incumbents it attacked sold through dealers who earned much of their profit servicing the kind of car Tesla meant to replace.

This session adds two lenses. Ecosystems and business models show where value is created around the product and who captures it. Game theory and competitor analysis treat rivals as players who anticipate and respond. Courtney, Kirkland and Viguerie (1997) then ask the question that decides how far to trust either lens: how uncertain is the future you are planning for?

Carried forward · Sessions 2 and 3

What the five forces leave out

Session 3 built the five forces properly and rated the US auto industry with them. It ended on two loose threads, and a third comes from the textbook’s critique of the framework. Nothing below re-teaches the forces; it starts where they stop.

Session 3 · five (six) forces

Complements raise value. Who keeps it?

Rothaermel counts complements as a sixth force; Porter says they act through the five. Both agree a complement can grow demand. Neither tells you how the extra value is split between Tesla and the firms whose products make a Tesla worth more. That split is where this session begins.

Session 3 · the hand-off

A rival that could also be a customer

Musk said Tesla was “open” to other carmakers using its Supercharger network if they shared the cost of usage (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 14). On a five-forces diagram a rival can only appear as rivalry. Here it could also pay for, and depend on, a complement Tesla owns.

Grant (2018) · the critique

Structure without moves

The central criticism of the five forces, Grant writes, is that they ignore competitive interaction: rivalry becomes a variable linking structure to profit, not decisions by firms that anticipate each other (p. 91). And industry explains less than 20% of the variation in firms’ return on assets (p. 84). Industry analysis still matters; it is not the whole picture.

The trap this session sets

Tesla’s history is easy to read as a string of brilliant moves, because we know how it turned out. Game theory and Courtney’s levels of uncertainty both force the harder question: what did each move look like before anyone knew how the game would end, and what did the other players believe at the time?

Session 2 · still in play

The 2006 master plan published Tesla’s sequence of moves in advance: sports car, then an affordable car, then a cheaper one, plus zero-emission power (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 5). Session 2 read it as purpose. Today, read it as a signal to rivals, suppliers and investors.

Beyond the five forces · Grant (2018), ch. 4

Complements, ecosystems and who captures the value

Grant starts from a simple asymmetry. A substitute lowers the value of a product; a complement raises it. A printer is useless without ink cartridges, a car without fuel. Once you accept that, the strategic question is no longer whether complements matter but how the value they create is shared between the firms that supply the pieces (Grant, 2018, p. 86).

01 · Complements

The split decides the profit

In 1990s video games, independent developers supplied most of the software and most of the consumer value, yet Nintendo took most of the system’s profit because it controlled the operating system and the making of cartridges. In personal computers the opposite happened: IBM’s open architecture let Windows become the proprietary standard, and the PC drifted towards a commodity (p. 86).

Grant’s rule: keep your own product monopolised, differentiated and in short supply, and encourage competition, commoditisation and excess capacity among your complements (p. 87). Apple’s ownership of iOS lets it choose which apps reach the iPhone and take 30% of their revenue; Nestlé lost control of Nespresso capsules once its patents stopped protecting them.

02 · Ecosystems

Value migrates; look for the bottleneck

A business ecosystem is the community of organisations, institutions and individuals that affect the enterprise, bound by codependence and always evolving. Value moves between its parts with technology, regulation and customer preferences, and firms can push it. The prize is a bottleneck: an activity that creates significant customer value and that the firm can dominate (Grant, 2018, p. 88, drawing on Jacobides).

  • Be the guardian of quality (Intel Inside).
  • Be irreplaceable; keep others replaceable (Apple’s “supply chain atomisation”).
  • Ride shifts in customer needs.
  • Redefine the value chain (IKEA moved assembly to the customer).

03 · Platforms

Network effects and winner-take-all

In digital systems, competition is often between platforms, the interfaces that link the parts. Complementors favour the platform with the most users and users favour the one with the most complements. That loop is the main source of winner-take-all markets, where the leader can take almost the whole profit pool (Grant, 2018, pp. 85–87).

Rothaermel defines a platform as a business that enables value-creating interactions between external producers and consumers, and names four roles in its ecosystem: owner, providers, producers and consumers (2023, pp. 282–283). Session 9 takes platforms further.

The value-capture test · Grant (2018), pp. 90–91

How much would the network lose if you left it?

The value capture model sets bounds on what a firm can take from its network of transacting parties. The ceiling is the value the network would lose if the firm walked away; the floor is what the firm could add to an alternative network. Investments with competitive intent raise the value the firm adds; actions with persuasive intent change how much the others are willing to concede. Ask it of Tesla and of a battery maker: which one does the other need more?

Business models · Grant (2018); Rothaermel (2024)

Business models: the logic of getting paid

A model is a simplified description of something real, so a business model is a simplified description of a business: its core logic for creating value. Grant quotes David Teece’s definition, “the manner by which the business enterprise delivers value to customers, entices customers to pay for value, and converts those payments to profit” (Grant, 2018, p. 88). Grant leaves open whether a business model is anything more than a strategy under another name. He uses it for one job: designing how a firm exploits the opportunities in its ecosystem (pp. 88–89).

Rothaermel puts it more bluntly: the business model is where strategy turns into action, and it explains how the firm intends to make money. He asks four questions of any model: why it creates value (revenue and cost models), what activities deliver the offer, who performs them, and how the activities are linked (2023, pp. 476–477).

The Business Model Canvas treats the firm as four blocks. A business model is one integrated set of choices across them; mapping it lets you try alternative configurations.

Eight common revenue logics

Paraphrased from Rothaermel (2024, pp. 478–480). Real firms combine them.

Razor–razor blades

Sell the base product cheaply, even at a loss, and earn on the complement it needs (Gillette, HP ink).

Subscription

Pay for access for a period, whether or not you use it (Office 365, Netflix).

Pay-as-you-go

Pay only for what you consume (utilities, cloud computing).

Freemium

Basic version free or cheap; charge for premium features or add-ons.

Ultra-low cost

A bare basic service at a low price, with extras sold à la carte (Ryanair).

Wholesale

Sell to retailers at a fixed price; they set the final price and keep the margin.

Agency

An agent sells on the producer’s behalf for a set percentage commission (app stores).

Bundling

Sell together, at a discount, products whose demand is negatively correlated (Office suite).

Competitive interaction · Grant (2018), pp. 91–95

Game theory: rivals who move back

A strategic situation is one of interdependence: what each player should do depends on what it expects the others to do. Game theory gives that situation a structure by asking four questions: who the players are, what options each has, what each player earns from every combination of options, and in what sequence the decisions are made (Grant, 2018, p. 91).

Sequence · simultaneous games

Choose without seeing the other’s choice

When players commit before they can observe each other, as with capacity booked a year ahead, you reason by best responses: for each move the rival might make, what is my best reply? A pair of moves from which neither player can gain by changing alone is a Nash equilibrium (Grant, 2018, p. 93). It need not be good for either player.

Sequence · sequential games

Move first, and reason backwards

When the second mover sees the first move, the first mover works backwards: for each move I could make, how will the rival reply, and which of those outcomes do I prefer? Moving first is worth something only if the move is visible and hard to undo. A price cut that can be reversed next quarter is not a first move; it is an announcement.

The prisoners’ dilemma · p. 93

Grant’s example: Coca-Cola and Pepsi each choose a big or small advertising budget. Both would earn more with small budgets, but each fears the other will exploit its restraint, so both spend big. The equilibrium is worse for both, and any agreement to restrain is unstable because each gains by cheating. Two escapes: turn a one-off game into a repeated one, or change the payoffs through deterrence.

Look for the same structure in the cases. Charging stations were unprofitable to build, and different stakeholders argued over who should pay; BMW and Nissan chose to back one shared fast-charging network, EVgo, open to every EV driver (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 7). Which escape from the dilemma is that?

Five ways to change the outcome · pp. 91–94

Cooperation
Co-opetition: every relationship mixes the two. Coke and Pepsi still cooperate on policy for soda sales in schools.
Deterrence
Impose costs on moves you do not want. Only credible threats deter: a price war often hurts the incumbent more than the entrant; excess capacity is more convincing.
Commitment
Eliminate your own options so that others believe you. Airbus advertised the A380 heavily before the design was finished.
Changing the game
In June 2014 Tesla offered its patents to competitors, betting that a bigger EV market would outweigh any lost advantage (p. 94).
Signalling
Selective communication meant to shape how rivals read your moves. Threats are credible when backed by reputation; preannounced price changes can also invite cooperation.

Tesla · commitment and signal

Panasonic agreed to fund 30–40% of the $5 billion Gigafactory 1 (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 9): a visible, sunk, shared investment that is hard to walk back. The case also reports one reading of Musk’s 2018 pay plan as a signal rather than pay: “They were about signaling a credible commitment to Tesla’s purpose: to become a clean energy giant” (p. 10). Does a pay plan remove any options? A factory does.

How far to trust it · pp. 94–95

Game theory is rigorous, but it gives clear predictions only in stylised situations; with realistic detail it often yields no equilibrium or several. Grant judges it better at explaining the past than predicting the future. Its use is to structure the situation and show how to change the game. Deterrence and preemption get a full session in Session 7.

Competitor analysis · Grant (2018), pp. 95–98

Competitor analysis: get inside the rival’s head

In a concentrated industry the behaviour of a few close rivals dominates the environment. Game theory supplies the formal apparatus, but Grant argues that for most decisions a less formal, evidence-based prediction of what rivals will do is enough (2018, p. 95). Competitive intelligence serves three purposes: forecast rivals’ strategies, predict their reactions to your moves, and work out how to influence their behaviour. The boundary with industrial espionage is not always clear (pp. 95–96).

The problem is usually too much information, not too little. Porter’s four-part framework says what to look for. Each part below feeds the same two predictions: what will the competitor do next, and how will it respond to us?

01

Future goals

Financial or market goals? A rival chasing share fights harder than one chasing profit, and one free of profit discipline, such as a state-owned firm, is hardest of all. The further performance falls short of its goals, the likelier a change of strategy, perhaps of management (p. 97).

02

Current strategy

How is it competing now? Look at what the company says and at what it does, and reconcile the two (p. 96). A forecast of change starts from an accurate picture of the present.

03

Assumptions

What its managers believe about the industry and about themselves. These beliefs are stable and converge across an industry into a shared “recipe” that can blind a whole industry. British and US motorcycle makers dismissed light Japanese bikes in the 1960s; by 1980 BSA and Triumph had stopped production and Harley-Davidson was struggling to survive (p. 97).

04

Resources & capabilities

Strengths and weaknesses decide how serious a challenge or a reply can be. Do not start a price war with a rival sitting on a cash pile; aim at weaknesses it will find hard to answer (p. 98). Session 5 builds the tools for this part.

Strategy under uncertainty · Courtney, Kirkland & Viguerie (1997)

How uncertain is the future you are planning for?

Courtney, Kirkland and Viguerie see one error behind most strategy made under uncertainty: treating it as binary. Either the future is certain enough for a point forecast, which pushes managers to bury uncertainty inside their cash flows, or it is unknowable, which tempts them to drop analysis and go on instinct. Both are dangerous (pp. 2–3). Even in uncertain markets, some trends are clear and many unknowns are knowable with the right analysis. What is left after the best possible analysis is residual uncertainty, and it comes in four levels (p. 4).

The level matters because it decides which tools work. The standard kit of market research, competitor cost and capacity analysis, the value chain, the five forces and discounted cash flow suits level 1 (p. 10). It misleads above it.

LEVEL 1

A clear-enough future

One forecast is precise enough to point to a single direction; the residual uncertainty does not change the decision. An airline answering a low-cost entrant at its hub can know segment sizes, the entrant’s costs and capacity (pp. 4–5).

Tools: the standard kit and a DCF.

LEVEL 2

Alternate futures

A few discrete outcomes; analysis can suggest probabilities but not pick the winner, and the best strategy depends on which occurs. Pending legislation is one source; another is a rival’s capacity decision in an oligopoly (pp. 5–6).

Tools: discrete scenarios, a valuation per scenario, decision analysis, trigger variables to watch (pp. 10–11).

LEVEL 3

A range of futures

The outcome can fall anywhere on a continuum bounded by a few key variables, with no natural scenarios: market penetration somewhere between 10% and 30%, say, or the cost of an unproven technology (pp. 7–8).

Tools: a few distinct scenarios (no more than four or five) spanning the probable range; test robustness (pp. 11–12).

LEVEL 4

True ambiguity

Several uncertainties interact so that not even the range can be identified. Rare, and transitional: Russia in 1992, consumer multimedia in the 1990s. Such situations drift towards level 3 or 2 (pp. 8–9).

Tools: list what is known and knowable, analogies, indicators, and what you would have to believe (pp. 12–13).

Three postures · pp. 13–15

  • Shape. Drive the industry towards a new structure of your own devising.
  • Adapt. Take the structure and its evolution as given; win by recognising and responding to developments fast.
  • Reserve the right to play. A special form of adapting, only at levels 2–4: incremental investments now that give a privileged position in information, cost or relationships, so you can wait until uncertainty falls.

Three kinds of move · pp. 15–16

  • Big bets. Large commitments, such as major capital investments or acquisitions, that pay off big in some scenarios and lose big in others. Shapers usually need them.
  • Options. Modest first investments that secure the upside and limit the downside: pilots, limited joint ventures, licences on an alternative technology.
  • No-regrets moves. Moves that pay off whatever happens: cost cuts, competitive intelligence, skills, and sometimes even capacity or market entry.

A shaper raises uncertainty

FedEx entered a stable, level 1 delivery industry and created level 3 uncertainty for itself and level 2 for UPS: would overnight delivery work, and would UPS have to match it? Its big bets took it to the brink of bankruptcy in its first two years, then reshaped the industry (pp. 17–19).

Credibility can replace cash

At levels 3 and 4 a shaper needs the credibility to coordinate other players around its preferred outcome more than deep pockets. Netscape shaped browser standards on its leaders’ credibility (p. 24).

The right to play, or to lose?

At level 4 it is hard to tell whether small investments reserve the right to play or only the right to lose. Prefer cheap options, and re-evaluate them whenever an important uncertainty clears, at least every six months (p. 25).

The pattern

Tesla’s portfolio is a shaper’s. The early moves were options and no-regrets moves that kept it alive and taught it the market; the big bets went into the ecosystem pieces that passed the bottleneck test: charging and battery supply. Note when the biggest bets came. Tesla bought SolarCity in November 2016, the year it ended with $1.4 billion of free cash while burning about $500 million a quarter (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 10). FedEx’s big bets took it to the brink in its first two years (Courtney et al., 1997, pp. 18–19).

The incumbents’ portfolio

By 2018 the incumbents were buying positions as well as cars: GM a 9% stake in Lyft, Toyota talks with Panasonic on a battery joint venture, BMW a $1.1 billion contract with CATL for a battery factory in Europe (Sull & Reavis, 2019, pp. 7, 10). Which of these reserve the right to play, and which only the right to lose? What trigger variable would tell you, and how often would you look?
