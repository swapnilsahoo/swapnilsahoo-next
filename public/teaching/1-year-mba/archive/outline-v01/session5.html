Internal Analysis & Resource-Based View

What Patagonia has that a rival cannot buy

Sessions 3 and 4 looked outward, at industries and the players in them. That view explains why some industries earn more than others. It cannot explain why firms facing the same industry earn different returns. For that you have to open the firm and take stock of what it owns, what it can do, and which of those things a rival could not simply buy or copy.

The case is Patagonia, the privately held outdoor-apparel company that used a Black Friday newspaper ad in 2011 to tell customers not to buy its fleece jacket. This session reads that ad as evidence: of an intangible resource built over decades, deployed through a particular value chain, and tested with VRIO and SWOT.

Carried forward · Sessions 2 to 4

The outside view is done. Now open the firm.

Session 2 put analysis third in Kaplan, Norton and Barrows’s sequence, under one question: what are the key issues our strategy must address? That step had three parts: external analysis, internal analysis and SWOT. Session 3 and Session 4 did the external part. This session does the internal part and then joins the two in a SWOT.

Session 3 · five forces

Industry structure sets the average

The forces explain why some industries are more profitable than others on average. They are silent on why two firms facing the same five forces earn different returns. Hold that gap open; it is today’s subject.

Session 4 · beyond the forces

Rivals are players, not averages

Ecosystems, business models and game theory treated competitors as actors who make moves. A move only works if the firm has something to move with. That something is a bundle of resources and capabilities.

Session 1 · Porter (1996)

Advantage lives in a system of activities

Fit among activities made a position hard to copy. Today gives you the tool that lists those activities one by one, the value chain, and a test for the resources behind them, VRIO.

The number that sets up the session

Rothaermel reports that firm-specific effects explain up to 55% of the differences in performance between firms (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 131). If the industry decided everything, firms in it would earn the same. They do not, so some of the explanation has to sit inside the firm. Internal analysis is the search for it.

Two readings · one strategic problem

Read with a question in mind

Which resource survives a serious challenge?

Five tests: inimitability, durability, appropriability, substitutability and competitive superiority. Compare a resource with alternatives, not just the firm’s own history.

Your application: call a Patagonia resource “valuable” only after naming a customer benefit or cost effect, a comparison and evidence. Then challenge who keeps the returns and what could make the resource obsolete.

Competing on Resources · assigned 1995 article ↗

What capability travels beyond one product?

Core competence connects collective learning across businesses. Test access to multiple markets, customer benefit and difficulty of imitation.

Your application: start with a repairable garment. Work backwards to the material, design, service and knowledge-sharing routines needed to keep it useful. Now name a second product where those routines might help—and one where they might not.

The Core Competence of the Corporation · assigned reading ↗

Resource inventory → competence → strategic choice

A trademark is an asset; a repeatable ability to coordinate design and repair is a capability hypothesis. A strong product line does not itself prove that the underlying learning transfers elsewhere. Use the resource tests to challenge the capability, then use the competence tests to challenge the proposed new market.

Bring one claim that fails. The learning value of internal analysis often lies in discovering where an apparent strength stops being useful.

Internal analysis of the company · Rothaermel (2024), ch. 4

Resources, capabilities, and what they build

Internal analysis uses four nested terms. Get them straight first, because most weak SWOTs come from mixing them up: a building is listed next to a reputation and a skill as if they were the same kind of thing.

1 · Resources

What the firm can draw on

Any asset the firm can use to form and carry out a strategy. Tangible resources have physical form: labour, capital, land, buildings, plant, equipment. Intangible ones do not: culture, knowledge, brand equity, reputation, intellectual property.

2 · Capabilities

What the firm can do with them

The organisational and managerial skills needed to orchestrate resources and deploy them. Capabilities are intangible by nature and show up in the firm’s structure, routines and culture.

3 · Core competencies

The strengths that matter

Unique strengths embedded deep in the firm that let it offer more value than rivals, or comparable value at lower cost. They come from the interplay of resources and capabilities.

4 · Activities

Where they become visible

Distinct, fine-grained processes such as taking orders, delivering products or invoicing. Competencies are deployed through activities, which is why the value chain comes later in this session.

The two assumptions behind the RBV

Heterogeneity: resource bundles differ across firms, even within one strategic group. Immobility: resources are “sticky” and do not move easily between firms, so the differences last. Rothaermel’s example: Continental and Delta tried to copy Southwest with Continental Lite and Song, and neither reproduced the bundle. Under perfect competition both assumptions fail, and so does any lasting advantage.

Why intangibles carry more weight

Anyone with cash can buy buildings or servers on the open market. A brand or a reputation has to be built, usually over a long period. Rothaermel concludes that competitive advantage is more likely to come from intangible resources than tangible ones (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 139). Keep that in mind when you score Patagonia: its most important assets do not appear on a balance sheet.

Stocks and flows: the bathtub

An intangible resource is a stock, like water in a bathtub. Investments are the flows from the taps, and leakage through turnover or forgetting drains it. You cannot fill the tub faster by opening every tap at once on the last day; it fills over time (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 152–153, after Dierickx & Cool, 1989). The Patagonia case is a record of flows.

“Core competencies that are not continuously nourished will lose their ability to yield a competitive advantage.” Rothaermel (2024, p. 138)

Resource-based view of the firm · VRIO

Four questions, asked in order

A list of resources is not yet an argument. The VRIO framework, which Rothaermel takes from Jay Barney, asks whether a resource, capability or competency is Valuable, Rare and costly to Imitate, and whether the firm is Organised to capture its value. It is a decision tree, not a scorecard: the first “no” ends the test, and only four “yes” answers support a sustained advantage (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 141–145).

NOT VALUABLE

Competitive disadvantage

It neither exploits an opportunity nor offsets a threat.

V, NOT RARE

Competitive parity

Needed to play; rivals have it too.

V + R, EASY TO COPY

Temporary advantage

A head start that imitation or substitution erodes.

V + R + I, NOT ORGANISED

Temporary at best

Xerox PARC invented the GUI and the mouse; Apple and Microsoft captured the value.

V + R + I + O

Sustained advantage

For as long as the environment and substitutes allow.

Valuable

Tied to the outside world

A resource is valuable if it helps the firm exploit an external opportunity or offset an external threat. That is the hinge to Sessions 3 and 4: value is judged against the environment, never in isolation. Session 6 takes the economics of value creation further.

Costly to imitate

Why copying is hard

Rivals attack an advantage by direct imitation or by substitution. Copying is costly when the resource depends on history (path dependence), when nobody can say exactly what causes the result (causal ambiguity), or when it lives in many interacting systems (social complexity). The sustaining-advantage extension develops these as isolating mechanisms; Session 7 applies commitments and capabilities to positioning.

Organised

Structure that captures the value

The firm needs a structure, processes and systems that let it use the resource. Xerox’s headquarters was focused on copiers, on the other side of the country from PARC, and did not organise to exploit what PARC built. A resource the firm cannot use earns nothing.

A second set of tests: Grant’s appraisal

Grant (2019, pp. 120–122) asks three questions of a resource: can it establish an advantage, can it sustain one, and can the firm appropriate the returns? His criteria line up with VRIO, with one addition.

    Value chain analysis · Porter (1987)

    Where the difference is made

    VRIO tests what the firm has. The value chain shows where it is used. Porter treats every business unit as a collection of discrete value activities, from sales to accounting, and locates advantage there: “It is at this level, not in the company as a whole, that the unit achieves competitive advantage” (Porter, 1987). He groups the activities into nine categories.

    Five primary activities

    Make it, move it, sell it, support it

    Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. They create the product, deliver and market it, and provide after-sale support.

    Four support activities

    The inputs and infrastructure

    Firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development and procurement. Rothaermel’s textbook relabels some of them (supply chain management, distribution, R&D, information systems, accounting and finance), but the logic is Porter’s.

    The question to ask

    Difference, or cost?

    Every activity adds some value and some cost. Value chain analysis asks which activities generate the firm’s differentiation and which drive its costs (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 154). Which of the two Patagonia should compete on is Session 7’s question.

    Why the value-chain extension helps here

    Porter (1987) introduces the value chain to judge diversified firms: a corporate parent can add value through mechanisms such as transferring skills between similar value chains or by sharing activities, such as a common sales force or logistics network. Session 9 returns to that corporate-strategy use. Today the tool is applied to a single business, which is how it starts in the article: advantage is built activity by activity, inside one unit.

    SWOT analysis · joining the inside to the outside

    A list is not a test

    SWOT is where internal and external analysis meet. Strengths and weaknesses are internal: they concern resources, capabilities and competencies, and VRIO decides which is which. A resource that is not valuable is a weakness; one that is valuable, rare, costly to imitate and organised is a strength and a core competency. Opportunities and threats are external and come from PESTEL and the five forces (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 160). Ask of every strength on a list: if a rival’s SWOT shows the same item, was it ever a strength?

    The SWOT matrix as four questions · after Rothaermel (2024), Exhibit 4.11

    Strengths × Opportunities

    How can internal strengths exploit external opportunities? The “offensive” options; start here.

    Strengths × Threats

    How can strengths reduce the likelihood and impact of external threats?

    Weaknesses × Opportunities

    Which weaknesses stop the firm taking an opportunity, and how can it shore them up?

    Weaknesses × Threats

    Which weaknesses would turn a threat into reality? The “defensive” options.

    The caveat that matters most

    One fact, two boxes

    A strength can also be a weakness, and an opportunity can also be a threat. Rothaermel’s examples: Alphabet’s Silicon Valley location brings talent and venture capital, and also high costs and earthquake risk; climate change threatens carmakers through fuel taxes and opens demand for low-emission cars. SWOT is only as good as the external and internal analysis done before it (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 161–162).

            Case · Patagonia: “Earth Is Now Our Only Shareholder” (HBS 9-323-057)

            “Don’t Buy This Jacket”, read as an asset on no balance sheet

            In November 2011 Patagonia ran a full-page Black Friday ad in The New York Times: a picture of its fleece jacket above the words “DON’T BUY THIS JACKET”. Black Friday traditionally opens the US holiday shopping season. The ad launched the Common Threads Initiative, which asked customers to reduce, repair, reuse, recycle and reimagine Patagonia products (Trelstad et al., 2023, p. 5). By 2022 Patagonia held the largest share of the US outdoor apparel industry, nearly 10% (p. 2).

            The puzzle

            Telling customers not to buy

            For almost any retailer, an ad against its own product on the busiest shopping day of the year is incoherent. It is coherent only if the firm is selling something other than the jacket: a reason to believe the jacket is worth keeping, repairing and passing on.

            The RBV reading

            The ad spends a stock

            The ad is one deployment of an intangible resource, environmental credibility, built by decades of flows. A rival could run the same words tomorrow. Without the history, customers would read it as a stunt. That is the difference between an activity anyone can copy and the resource that makes it work.

            What the case does not tell you

            There is no sales figure

            Patagonia is private and does not publish financial data; the roughly $1 billion of revenue and $100 million of net income for 2022 are media estimates (p. 2). The case gives no figure for the ad’s effect on sales. Before calling it a success, say what evidence you would want.

            Profit is part of the resource, not its opposite

            Kris Tompkins, recalling the 1980s: “Because if you’re not successful financially, the experiment’s over” (Trelstad et al., 2023, p. 3). Chouinard goes further and claims that every decision best for the planet has made the company money (p. 3). Treat that as a hypothesis. The RBV can explain why credibility earns returns; it cannot show that every green decision paid.

            What comes next

            In September 2022 the Chouinard family moved all of Patagonia’s voting stock into a purpose trust and all of its non-voting stock into a nonprofit, the Holdfast Collective (p. 11). Session 6 distinguishes shared value from the distribution of profits. Session 7 asks which competitive position these activities support. The sustaining-advantage extension examines imitation.

            For class

            Six questions to argue about

            01

            If you were CEO Ryan Gellert, which single Patagonia resource would you protect first, and which of the company’s own activities is most likely to erode it?

            02

            The case gives no sales figure for “Don’t Buy This Jacket”. What evidence would convince you that it created value rather than attention, and who would you need to hear from?

            03

            Chouinard says every decision best for the planet has made the company money. Is that a causal claim, or survivorship? What would a counter-example look like, and would the case report it?

            04

            In the cotton switch, was the advantage the organic cotton, or the organisation that could move 166 products in 18 months? Which of the two could a rival buy?

            05

            By 2020, 1% for the Planet had more than 5,000 business members, and B Lab’s co-founder says thousands of companies followed Patagonia in making themselves legally accountable to stakeholders. When a practice you pioneered becomes common, what happens to its VRIO score, and where must your advantage move?

            06

            A customer post the professor shared (r/BuyItForLife) describes free repairs and, when a jacket could not be fixed, store credit for its full original value. Is that a cost, a marketing expense, or an investment in a resource? How would you show which?
