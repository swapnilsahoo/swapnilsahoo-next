Differentiation, Cost Leadership & Blue Ocean

Two ways to win, one way to lose, and a company that keeps saying no

In a world of rivals, why can’t a business just be good at everything? Porter’s answer is that there are only two basic types of competitive advantage, lower cost and differentiation, and that each can be pursued across a whole industry or in a narrow part of it. A firm that tries for every kind of advantage usually ends up with none: it is stuck in the middle.

Patagonia sells about 1,400 products, from casual T-shirts to wetsuits, and holds the largest share of US outdoor apparel. It also caps its own growth and once ran a full-page advertisement telling customers not to buy its jacket. Today you decide which generic strategy the case evidence supports, and what the company has had to refuse to keep it. Then design a candidate blue-ocean move and identify the commitments needed to deliver it.

Read the argument · compare the lenses

Two readings. One difficult choice.

Can a different offering change the terms of competition?

The 2004 article shifts attention from beating rivals on their established measures to creating a different combination of buyer benefits and costs. Value innovation requires both sides: a meaningful leap in buyer value and a cost structure that makes the offering viable. A premium feature by itself is differentiation; a technological breakthrough by itself is invention.

Their Cirque du Soleil example combines theatre-like elements with selected circus attractions while removing costly conventions. Ask what the new buyer values, what the firm stops paying for, and what new demand follows. A blue ocean is a strategic move to examine, not a permanent label to award a company.

Reading lens: Kim & Mauborgne (2004), Blue Ocean Strategy; course-pack reprint pp. 14–21. Applications and model assumptions below are learning exercises.

Reading question: If a repair service attracts existing Patagonia buyers but leaves occasional users unable to afford outdoor clothing, has it created new demand or strengthened an existing position? What evidence distinguishes those explanations?

What can a rival copy, and what can the firm no longer undo?

Commitments change future choices because they are difficult to reverse. Capabilities develop through performing and improving activities over time. The two reinforce one another: commitments shape what a firm learns, while existing capabilities constrain the commitments it can make credibly. Spending more money is not, by itself, evidence of either.

Their four threats sharpen the diagnosis: imitation erodes distinctiveness; substitution changes the solution buyers prefer; hold-up allows other parties to capture value; slack dissipates value internally. Match the response to the threat. A promise of lifelong repair may build trust and learning, yet also create future service obligations.

Reading lens: Ghemawat & Pisano (1997), Sustaining Superior Performance: Commitments and Capabilities, especially pp. 13–17. Applications and model assumptions below are learning exercises.

Reading question: Which Patagonia choice creates an irreversible constraint, which creates a repeatable capability, and which does both? Defend the distinction using a specific case page.

Core question · create value differently

Must better value always mean higher cost?

Start with the generic strategies below, then challenge the industry’s familiar bundle. A different value curve should explain both the activities removed and the buyer problem solved. Treat Patagonia as an open case: its environmental commitments and repair activities do not automatically prove a blue-ocean strategy.

Demand test

Name a noncustomer and the reason they stay out: price, inconvenience, unfamiliarity, or a poor fit with their needs. A larger share of existing buyers is not the same as new demand.

Economic test

Specify what disappears from the activity system as new benefits are added. If every factor is raised, the proposal may become expensive differentiation. If every factor is reduced, it may simply be a low-cost offering.

Use the four actions to make that reasoning explicit: eliminate an unneeded convention, reduce an overprovided feature, raise an important benefit, and create a missing source of value. The studio translates those questions into a deliberately simplified decision.

Reading lens: Kim & Mauborgne, Four Actions Framework (companion tool to the assigned article). Applications and model assumptions below are learning exercises.

Session 6 · creating advantage

Two ways to widen the wedge

Raise willingness to pay by more than you raise cost, or lower cost by more than you lower willingness to pay. Session 6 showed both as moves on the same value stick. Porter turns them into the two basic types of advantage and asks you to pick one.

Session 5 · the value chain

Advantage is made activity by activity

In Session 5 you tagged Patagonia’s activities and left open which of the two it should compete on. Porter answers with drivers: the structural reasons an activity is cheap, or unique, or both. You will sort Patagonia’s activities by driver below.

Sustaining advantage extension · Ghemawat & Pisano (1997)

A position is a bundle of commitments

If positions were easy to reverse, a firm could try one and switch for free. They are not: each involves many hard-to-reverse commitments to doing particular activities in particular ways (Ghemawat & Pisano, 1997, p. 14). Sustaining advantage extension used that to explain why advantage lasts; here it explains why choosing is hard.

The trap this session sets

Different is not the same as differentiated. Porter warns that firms are often different without being differentiated, because they are unique at things buyers do not value (1985, p. 119). Equally, a high price is not a strategy and a low price is not a cost advantage. For every claim on this page, ask which driver makes it true and what it costs.

Generic strategies · Porter (1985), chapter 1

Two types of advantage, two kinds of scope

A firm may have many strengths and weaknesses against its rivals, but Porter argues that each one matters only through its effect on relative cost or on differentiation. Combine those two types of advantage with the scope of the target, the whole industry or a narrow segment of it, and you get three generic strategies, the third of which has two variants (1985, pp. 11–12). The word “generic” is the point: the specific actions differ by industry, but these are the routes to advantage that must be probed in any industry.

Cost leadership · pp. 12–14

Be the low-cost producer

Broad scope, often across many segments, because breadth itself feeds the cost advantage. Sources include scale, proprietary technology and preferential access to raw materials. The product is usually standard or no-frills. It is more than going down the learning curve: the leader must find and exploit all sources of cost advantage.

Differentiation · p. 14

Be unique where buyers care

Choose one or more attributes that many buyers see as important and position uniquely to meet them; the reward is a premium price. Caterpillar built its position in construction equipment on durability, service, spare-parts availability and its dealer network. The means differ in every industry.

Focus · pp. 15–16

Serve a segment to the exclusion of others

The focuser optimises for its target and wins there even without an advantage overall. It works only if the segment is genuinely different: buyers with unusual needs, or a production and delivery system unlike the rest of the industry’s. Narrowness alone is not enough.

The confusion Porter flags · p. 15, n. 8

Broad differentiation or differentiation focus?

These are the two strategies most often confused in practice. The broad differentiator builds on attributes valued widely across the industry (his example: IBM in computers); the focuser finds segments with special needs and meets them better (Cray Research). Hold this distinction: it is the Patagonia question.

What each strategy must also get right

Every generic strategy has a second condition, on the dimension it does not compete on. Most failures in Porter’s examples come from that second condition, not the first.

Cost leader

Parity or proximity in differentiation

If buyers do not see the product as comparable, the leader must discount below rivals and the cost advantage drains away. Texas Instruments in watches never closed the gap and left the industry; Northwest Airlines caught the problem and invested in marketing and service. There is also room for only one cost leader: several aspirants fight over every point of share (pp. 13–14).

Differentiator

Parity or proximity in cost

The premium must exceed the extra cost of being unique, and a markedly inferior cost position will cancel the premium. So the differentiator cuts cost in every area that does not affect its uniqueness. Unlike cost leadership, several differentiators can prosper in one industry if buyers value several different attributes (p. 14).

Focuser

A segment that is different, and attractive

Royal Crown focused on cola, but Coca-Cola and Pepsi could serve cola drinkers while serving everyone else, and their broader lines gave them the advantage. The segment must also be structurally attractive: some are much less profitable than others (p. 16).

Low cost and uniqueness · Porter (1985), chapters 3 and 4

Where cost and uniqueness come from

“We have economies of scale” and “we have a great brand” are conclusions, not explanations. Porter pushes the analysis down to the value activity and asks what structural factor makes that activity cheap or unique. He calls these factors drivers. The same list appears on both sides, which is why cost and differentiation are so hard to pull apart.

Cost drivers · p. 70

Ten structural causes of cost

Economies of scaleLearningCapacity utilisation LinkagesInterrelationshipsIntegration TimingDiscretionary policiesLocation Institutional factors

Drivers interact, and their weight differs from one activity to the next, so no single driver, whether scale or the learning curve, is ever the whole explanation of a firm’s cost position (p. 70). Policies are the driver managers forget they control: a no-frills airline lowers cost by dropping free meals and baggage allowances, flying from secondary airports with spartan terminals and ticketing on board (pp. 80–81).

Uniqueness drivers · pp. 124–127

Nine reasons an activity is unique

Policy choicesLinkagesTiming LocationInterrelationshipsLearning and spillovers IntegrationScaleInstitutional factors

Listed roughly in order of prominence. Policy choices come first: what features to offer, what service, how intensely to advertise, what quality of inputs to buy. Porter concludes that much uniqueness is therefore discretionary (p. 124), which cuts both ways, because a policy is also easy to copy.

Some drivers last longer

Policy choices may be easier for rivals to imitate than uniqueness that stems from interrelationships or from exploiting linkages (p. 127). Learning helps only while it stays proprietary; once it spills over to competitors it stops differentiating (p. 126).

Uniqueness usually costs money

Greater durability may need more or dearer material: Rockwell’s water meters outlast rivals’ because they use more bronze (pp. 127–128). Some uniqueness is cheap, such as better coordination of linked activities. Having different but more desired features usually costs less than having more features (p. 128).

A driver can switch sides

Scale lets Hertz offer more pick-up points and faster field service, a source of uniqueness. Yet Porter notes that scale can also reduce how flexibly fashion-related firms respond to buyers (p. 127). Keep that in mind when you compare Patagonia with firms built for volume.

Stuck in the middle · Porter (1985), pp. 16–20

Stuck in the middle, and the case for doing both

A firm that engages in each generic strategy but achieves none of them has no competitive advantage. In any segment, a cost leader, a differentiator or a focuser is better placed, and if the stuck firm stumbles on a profitable product or buyer, rivals with a real advantage soon take it away. Porter adds that in most industries quite a few competitors are in this position (p. 16).

How firms get stuck · p. 17

An unwillingness to choose

The firm seeks advantage by every means and gets none, because the different types require inconsistent actions. Successful firms get stuck too, when they compromise their strategy for growth or prestige. Laker Airways began as a no-frills cost focuser on the North Atlantic, then added frills, services and routes, blurred its image and went bankrupt.

The focuser’s temptation · p. 17

Success invites blurring

Focus means deliberately limiting potential sales volume. Once a focuser dominates its segment, it is most tempted to trade the focus for growth. Porter’s advice: grow by taking the same generic strategy into new industries, not by diluting it in the old one. Keep this card in mind for Patagonia.

When being stuck pays · pp. 17–18

Only with luck, or separation

A stuck firm earns attractive profits only if its industry is highly favourable or its rivals are stuck too, and industry maturity widens the gap. A firm can run two generic strategies only in strictly separate units, as Trusthouse Forte did with five hotel chains aimed at different segments.

Can a firm be low-cost and differentiated at once?

Porter does not say never. Differentiation is usually costly, so the two usually conflict, but if a firm achieves both the rewards add up: a premium price on a lower cost base. Crown Cork & Seal did it in the “hard to hold” segments for steel cans, with service and technical help on one side and dedicated, modern plants on the other (pp. 18–19). He names three conditions, and each one is temporary.

CONDITION 1 · p. 19

Rivals are stuck in the middle

No one pushes the firm to the point where cost and uniqueness conflict. It lasts until a competitor chooses one strategy and executes it well.

CONDITION 2 · p. 19

Cost depends on share or interrelationships

A big share lead lowers cost enough to pay for extra uniqueness. It lasts until a capable rival matches the share or the interrelationship.

CONDITION 3 · p. 20

The firm pioneers a major innovation

A new technology or practice lowers cost and lifts quality together, but only while the firm is the only one with it.

“A firm should always aggressively pursue all cost reduction opportunities that do not sacrifice differentiation.” Porter (1985, p. 20). He adds the mirror rule, to pursue every differentiation that is not costly, and then insists the firm must choose.

The debate: integration, or “best cost”

Porter’s position

Cost reduction is not cost advantage

Porter grants that many firms cut cost while raising differentiation, and that a firm which has never tried can find large savings with no loss of uniqueness. But a firm facing capable rivals who are also chasing cost will eventually reach the point where the next saving costs some differentiation. That is where the strategies become inconsistent and the firm must choose (p. 18).

Ghemawat & Pisano’s challenge

Not every firm faces the same frontier

Honda’s Accord and Toyota’s Camry sat squarely in the mass market of GM, Ford and Chrysler, at similar prices: “No creative positioning here” (1997, p. 16). Yet they were developed in about half the time with half the engineering resources, cost about $2,000 less per comparable car and had higher quality (pp. 16–17). Capabilities built over years meant rivals did not share one efficiency frontier (p. 13).

Where they agree

Both readings tie a cost position to commitment. Porter calls cost leadership a strategy particularly dependent on preemption (p. 14). Ghemawat and Pisano’s Du Pont example shows how: a low-cost process gave it a 20% cost advantage in titanium dioxide, and a large new plant crowded out rivals’ big expansions. Twenty years later it was still the only firm running that process (1997, p. 14).

Stuck in the middle, in commitment language

Ghemawat and Pisano warn that trying to stay totally flexible rarely pays: it risks lock-out and yields, at best, mediocre performance (1997, p. 15). A firm that will not commit to one set of activities is Porter’s stuck firm seen from the other side. The question for class: is a capability lead like Toyota’s a lasting position, or Porter’s third condition, which ends when rivals catch up?

Kim and Mauborgne’s claim that value innovation can break the trade-off altogether is explored in the Blue Ocean lens and the decision studio.

    The case · Trelstad, Hsieh, Norris & Pinckney (2023)

    Patagonia’s generic strategy: what it chose, and what it refused

    The case never names a generic strategy. It does supply the facts Porter’s tests need: how broad the target is, which attributes customers pay for, and which activities the company declined to perform. Nobody serious argues that Patagonia is a cost leader. The live question is the one Porter says practitioners most often get wrong.

    CaseReading 1 · broad differentiation

    A wide line, sold widely, on widely valued attributes

    • About 1,400 products, from fleece jackets and casual T-shirts to high-performance athletic and outdoor wear, camping gear, backpacks and wetsuits (p. 2).
    • 36 stores in the US and 55 abroad, a website shipping to 38 countries, and a large wholesale business through Dick’s Sporting Goods, REI and independent specialty retailers (p. 2).
    • About half of sales outside the US, and the largest share of US outdoor apparel, at nearly 10% (p. 2).
    • Its first stated value is quality: products that are useful, versatile, long-lasting, repairable and recyclable (Exhibit 2, p. 14). Durability is something most buyers of clothing can value.
    CaseReading 2 · differentiation focus

    A segment with special needs, and a cap on volume

    • The apparel began as gear for climbers: rugby shirts imported because they stood up to abrasion from rock, then a pile fleece meant to beat a wool sweater that grew heavy and slow to dry on climbs (pp. 2–3).
    • Since 1991, growth has been limited to pre-set targets (p. 3). Porter: focus means deliberately limiting potential sales volume (1985, p. 17).
    • The 2022 philosophy says growth must never outpace the ability to make products with intention and quality, and that Patagonia should not encourage consumption (Exhibit 9, p. 19).
    AnalysisIt depends where you draw the industry

    Porter says breadth of target is a matter of degree; what makes a focus strategy is that the target differs from the rest of the industry, so that broadly targeted rivals serve it badly (1985, p. 15). Inside US outdoor apparel, where the case measures its share, Patagonia looks like a broad differentiator. Inside apparel as a whole, the fashion industry the case describes on page 4, it serves the buyers who want clothes that last, get repaired and come from a firm they trust on the environment.

    Your answer commits you. Call it broad differentiation and you must show that its attributes are valued across the industry and that it keeps cost close to rivals’ outside them. Call it differentiation focus and you must show that its buyers’ needs really differ, and then face Porter’s warning that a dominant focuser is the firm most tempted to blur its strategy for growth (p. 17).

    What Patagonia refused

    A position is defined as much by what a firm will not do. Each card states a refusal from the case, with its page; the grey line under it is this page’s reading of what was kept and what it cost.

    Refused · growth for its own sake

    In the 1991 recession a lender cut its credit line and it laid off 20% of its 600 staff. Chouinard concluded the company had been growing for the sake of growing; from then on growth was held to pre-set targets (p. 3).

    Kept: a stable workplace. Cost: scale it chose not to have.

    Refused · conventional cotton

    After a 1994 impact study, Chouinard gave the company eighteen months to move all 166 cotton products to organic cotton or stop selling sportswear, then 30% of the business. Sales were back to their previous level within two years (pp. 3–4).

    Kept: product integrity. Cost: a bet with 30% of the business on it, and up to two years to win back sales.

    Refused · selling more units

    On Black Friday 2011 a full-page New York Times ad showed its fleece with the words “DON’T BUY THIS JACKET” and launched Common Threads: reduce, repair, reuse, recycle, reimagine (p. 5).

    Kept: credibility on its claims. Cost: some sales it asked customers not to make.

    Refused · the disposable garment

    Worn Wear repairs Patagonia clothing free of charge, and a buyback scheme resells or recycles used items for store credit. The Reno facility repaired 100,000 items a year (p. 4).

    Kept: the durability promise, made usable. Cost: free repairs.

    Refused · wholesale on any terms

    It has a large wholesale business (p. 2), but the 2022 philosophy says partners, wholesale included, should mirror Patagonia’s values and complement its direct business rather than compete with it (Exhibit 9, p. 19).

    Kept: control of how the brand reaches buyers. Cost: reach it could buy elsewhere.

    Refused · profit as the scorecard

    Since 1986 it has given the greater of 1% of sales or 10% of profit to environmental causes (p. 3). The 2022 philosophy adds that financial success should enable good but does not equal success, and that marketing should not be judged on ROI (Exhibit 9, p. 19).

    Kept: a purpose buyers can check. Cost: a slice of every year’s sales.

    Analysis

    The proximity test the case cannot settle

    Porter’s differentiator must keep cost close to rivals’ in everything that does not create its uniqueness (1985, p. 14). The case shows activities that add cost on purpose: organic cotton, free repairs, giving away 1% of sales. It shows none that hold cost down outside them, because Patagonia does not publish its accounts. The media estimates imply a net margin of roughly 10%, but two estimates divided give only a rough figure.

    Analysis

    Commitments behind the position

    Ghemawat and Pisano say positions are commitment-intensive (1997, p. 14). Patagonia’s are unusually hard to undo: since 2011 its environmental and stakeholder commitments sit in its articles and bylaws and could not be changed without a unanimous shareholder vote (case, p. 5). Sustaining advantage extension showed how the 2022 ownership change made them harder still. A rival copying the products would not be copying these.

    Analysis

    Where the position could crack

    Porter lists “too big a price premium” among the pitfalls: buyers abandon a differentiator whose premium outruns its value, and the premium drifts up when cost is not kept close to rivals’ (1985, pp. 160–161). Patagonia adds cost on purpose: organic cotton, free repairs, 1% of sales given away (case, pp. 3–4). Each one widens the gap its prices must cover, so the test is whether buyers go on valuing what the extra cost buys. That is the risk to watch.

    Decision workshop · 8 minutes

    The repair promise: copyable slogan or durable advantage?

    Teaching scenario, not a reported Patagonia decision: a rival announces free repairs. Your team has a limited budget. Decide how to respond before reading the consequences.

    Consider the consequences

    A visible promise is easy to match; delivery may be much harder. Test repair capacity, parts availability, unit cost and response time first. A promise without the operational capability can destroy willingness to pay. Your evidence request: repair turnaround and repeat failure rates.

    Consider the consequences

    Training, product repairability and feedback to design could accumulate capability over time. This is a plausible response to imitation, but it adds commitments and does not solve every substitution threat. Your evidence request: whether learning reduces repair cost and improves product life.

    Consider the consequences

    A lower price can improve access, but it also reduces the value captured per sale. Unless the activity system changes, the decision is a price cut rather than cost leadership. Your evidence request: which avoidable costs fund it and whether a new buyer group enters.
