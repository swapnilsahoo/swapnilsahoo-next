Internal Analysis & Resource-Based View

What Patagonia has that a rival cannot buy

Industry analysis explains why some industries earn more than others. It cannot explain why two firms facing the same industry earn different returns. This session opens the firm: what it has, what it has learned to do, which of those things a rival could not simply buy or copy, where in its activities the difference is made, and how the inside meets the outside in a SWOT.

Session 4 drew the game around a firm: Brandenburger and Nalebuff’s value net, in which no player can take away more than its added value, and Courtney, Kirkland and Viguerie’s levels of uncertainty. It left a question open: why does one player bring more added value to the game than another? The answer sits inside the firm, in resources and competences built over years. Patagonia’s 2011 Black Friday ad, “Don’t Buy This Jacket”, is today’s test. A rival could print the same words. It could not print the forty years behind them.

Question 01 · Prahalad & Hamel (1990)

What does a firm have, and what can it do?

Prahalad and Hamel open with two companies that began the 1980s with comparable portfolios. In 1980 GTE had sales of $9.98 billion and NEC $3.8 billion. By 1988 NEC’s sales, $21.89 billion, had passed GTE’s $16.46 billion. Their explanation is that NEC conceived of itself in terms of core competencies and GTE did not: one was a portfolio of competencies, the other a portfolio of businesses (Prahalad & Hamel, 1990, pp. 79–80).

So internal analysis asks two things. What does the firm have: its resources, tangible and intangible? And what has it learned to do with them: its capabilities, and the few core competencies that differ from a rival’s? The second question is the harder one, because the answer is not on the balance sheet and is often invisible to the firm itself.

Why look inside at all · Rothaermel (2024), Exhibit 3.2

The industry explains a fifth; the firm explains up to half

In the studies Rothaermel reports, industry effects explain roughly 20% of overall firm performance and firm effects up to 55%; business cycles and unexplained variance account for the remaining 25% or so (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 90–91, 131). Session 3’s five forces explain the first slice: why some industries are more profitable on average. They are silent on the second. If the industry decided everything, firms in it would earn the same. They do not.

Four nested terms

Get them straight first, because most weak internal analyses come from mixing them up: a building is listed next to a reputation and a skill as if they were the same kind of thing.

1 · Resources

What the firm can draw on

Any asset the firm can use to form and carry out a strategy. Tangible resources have physical form: labour, capital, land, buildings, plant, equipment. Intangible ones do not: culture, knowledge, brand equity, reputation, intellectual property (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 137–139).

2 · Capabilities

What it can do with them

The organisational and managerial skills needed to orchestrate resources and deploy them. Capabilities are intangible by nature and show up in the firm’s structure, routines and culture (p. 137). The test: is it something the firm does, rather than something it has?

3 · Core competencies

The few that really differ

Unique strengths embedded deep in the firm that let it offer more value than rivals, or comparable value at lower cost (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 132). For Prahalad and Hamel they are collective learning, and few firms build world leadership in more than five or six (1990, p. 83).

4 · Activities

Where they become visible

Distinct, fine-grained processes such as taking orders, delivering products or invoicing (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 137). Competencies are deployed through activities, which is why the value chain is question 3.

The two assumptions behind the RBV

Heterogeneity: resource bundles differ across firms, even within one strategic group. Immobility: resources are “sticky” and do not move easily between firms, so the differences last. Rothaermel’s example: Continental and Delta tried to copy Southwest with Continental Lite and Song, and neither reproduced the bundle. Under perfect competition both assumptions fail, and so does any lasting advantage (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 140–141).

Why intangibles carry more weight

Anyone with cash can buy buildings or servers on the open market. A brand or a reputation has to be built, usually over a long period. Rothaermel concludes that competitive advantage is more likely to come from intangible resources than tangible ones (2024, p. 139). Collis and Montgomery agree: the best resources “are often intangible, not physical” (1995, p. 124). Patagonia’s most important assets do not appear on a balance sheet.

Stocks and flows: the bathtub

An intangible resource is a stock, like water in a bathtub. Investments are the flows from the taps, and leakage through turnover or forgetting drains it. You cannot fill the tub faster by opening every tap on the last day; it fills over time (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 152–153, after Dierickx & Cool, 1989). The Patagonia case is a record of flows.

“Core competencies that are not continuously nourished will lose their ability to yield a competitive advantage.” Rothaermel (2024, p. 138)

Each tab rebuilds the article’s central diagram so you can work it rather than read it. The third tab sets the two against each other.

The firm as a tree

Prahalad and Hamel picture the diversified firm as a tree. The end products are the leaves, flowers and fruit; the business units are the smaller branches; the trunk and major limbs are core products; the root system is the core competence. Look only at the fruit and you miss the strength of the tree (1990, p. 81).

Core products are the components that carry a competence into many end products. Canon was reputed to hold an 84% world manufacturing share in desktop laser-printer engines while its brand share of laser printers was minuscule (p. 85).

Three tests for a core competence (p. 83)

  1. 1. Access: it opens potential access to a wide variety of markets.
  2. 2. Customer benefit: it contributes significantly to the benefits customers perceive in the end product.
  3. 3. Hard to imitate: it is a complex harmonisation of technologies and skills a rival cannot assemble piece by piece.

What it is not

Not outspending rivals on R&D: Canon passed Xerox in copier unit share in 1983 on a fraction of its reprographics budget. Not shared costs, and not vertical integration (p. 83).

How it is lost

By outsourcing what looks like a cost centre. Chrysler’s outsourced engines rose from 252,000 to 382,000 between 1985 and 1987, while Honda kept engines in-house. Such skills cannot be “rented in” (p. 84).

Value is set in the market, not in the firm

Collis and Montgomery present the resource-based view as the bridge between two swings of the pendulum: industry analysis, which looked outside, and core competence, which looked inside. A resource cannot be valued in isolation; its value is set where three market forces meet: demand, scarcity and appropriability (1995, pp. 119–121). Nobody has managed to brand lobsters, and a brand that once mattered in personal computers no longer did, as IBM found at great cost (p. 120).

  1. 1. Inimitability. Is it hard to copy? IBP’s automated beef plants earned it returns of 1.3% in the 1970s; once ConAgra and Cargill replicated them, returns fell to 0.4% (pp. 120–121).
  2. 2. Durability. How fast does it depreciate? Disney’s brand survived two decades of neglect; know-how in semiconductor memories wastes fast (p. 122).
  3. 3. Appropriability. Who captures the value? The banking contacts of leveraged-buyout firms sat with the individuals, who left and took the profits (pp. 122–123).
  4. 4. Substitutability. Can a different resource trump it? The majors could not copy People Express’s no-frills system, so they matched its fares with reservation systems and yield management (p. 123).
  5. 5. Competitive superiority. Whose resource is really better? Judge it against rivals, not against the firm’s other activities (p. 123).
“Core competence has too often become a ‘feel good’ exercise that no one fails.” Collis & Montgomery (1995), p. 123

Disaggregate, or the test is empty

“Consumer marketing skills” is too broad to test. Break it into brand management, then into product-line extensions or cost-effective couponing, and you can measure who is better. A maker of diagnostic equipment found that its claimed competence, “instrumentation”, was really the design of the interface between machine and user; it hired ergonomists and moved into doctors’ offices (pp. 123–124).

Resource inventory → competence → strategic choice

A trademark is an asset. A repeatable ability to coordinate design, materials and repair is a capability, and calling it a core competence is a hypothesis. A strong product line does not prove that the learning behind it transfers anywhere else. Use Collis and Montgomery’s tests to challenge the capability, then Prahalad and Hamel’s tests to challenge the market you propose to take it into.

Where they agree

Both put the valuable thing inside the firm and both expect it to need investment. Collis and Montgomery credit core competence with making the corporate office the guardian of the firm’s “crown jewels” (1995, p. 125); Prahalad and Hamel warn that competencies fade if not used (1990, p. 81).

Where they part

Prahalad and Hamel judge a competence by what it lets the firm do next. Collis and Montgomery insist on the market: a competence nobody fails is no test, and investing in one without asking how attractive the industry is can waste it. Masco built real skill in metalworking and took it into industries with price-sensitive buyers, low entry barriers and powerful suppliers; returns disappointed (1995, pp. 123, 126).

A shared discipline: keep the list short

A firm that lists 20 to 30 capabilities has probably not produced a list of core competencies (Prahalad & Hamel, 1990, p. 83). Disaggregate until each item can be compared with a rival’s (Collis & Montgomery, 1995, p. 123).

Bring one claim that fails. The learning value of internal analysis often lies in finding where an apparent strength stops being useful. The tree above tests “our best-selling jacket” as a competence; the market-forces diagram tests a designer who could leave with the customers.

From the earlier decks · core competencies in the wild

What a competence looks like from outside

A competence is invisible; its traces are not. Two textbook cases show the traces: a list of things the firm does, and a longer list of things it refuses to do. “Strategy is as much about deciding to do things differently from competitors as it is about choosing not to do certain things at all” (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 132).

Five Guys · Rothaermel (2024), ChapterCase 4

“Make the best burger. Don’t worry about cost.”

Jerry Murrell opened the first store in 1986. The family ran only five stores for 17 years while it perfected the recipe; the sons blind-tasted 16 mayonnaises and chose the most expensive, and when a hurricane nearly tripled tomato prices the company did not compromise on quality (pp. 129–130). By some estimates it took half the “better burger” segment (p. 130).

Does

Fresh, never-frozen beef; Idaho potatoes cut by hand; burgers made to order with free toppings (pp. 132–133).

Refuses

Menu bloat, drive-throughs, delivery, WiFi, a place to linger, and marketing. Even former President Obama waited in line (p. 133).

Yeti · Rothaermel (2024), Strategy Highlight 4.1

Making quality cool

Founded in 2006 by two brothers frustrated with coolers that caved in, Yeti used rotational moulding to make a near-indestructible cooler that sold for $250 to $1,300, more than ten times an ordinary one. Drinkware took it from $150 million of revenue in 2014 to over $1 billion in 2020; in 2022 sales were $1.5 billion and net income $215 million (pp. 134–135).

Rothaermel names two competencies: superior quality and performance, and a “coolness factor” built through about 130 brand ambassadors rather than paid influencers. Success then drew cheaper imitators (p. 135). Which of the two competencies did they copy, and which could they not?

Question 02 · Collis & Montgomery (1995)

Which of its resources can sustain an advantage?

A list of resources is not yet an argument. Collis and Montgomery’s harsh truth is that most firms’ resources do not pass an objective application of the market tests (1995, p. 124). The course’s working tool for applying them is VRIO, which Rothaermel takes from Jay Barney: is the resource Valuable, Rare and costly to Imitate, and is the firm Organised to capture its value? It is a decision tree, not a scorecard: the first “no” ends the test, and only four “yes” answers support a sustained advantage (Rothaermel, 2024, pp. 141–145).

The earlier decks call the fourth box an “unexploited competitive advantage”. This page follows Rothaermel: without organisation the best case is a temporary advantage, and at Xerox not even that (2024, p. 145).

Valuable

Tied to the outside world

A resource is valuable if it helps the firm exploit an external opportunity or offset an external threat (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 141). That is the hinge to Sessions 3 and 4: value is judged against the environment, never in isolation, which is exactly Collis and Montgomery’s point about demand.

Costly to imitate

Copying, or going round

Rivals attack an advantage by direct imitation or by substitution (pp. 143–144). Copying is costly when the resource depends on history, when nobody can say exactly what causes the result, or when it lives in many interacting systems. The next section takes these isolating mechanisms one by one.

Organised

Structure that captures the value

The firm needs structure, processes and systems that let it use the resource. Xerox’s headquarters, focused on copiers and on the other side of the country from PARC, did not organise to exploit what PARC built (p. 145). A resource the firm cannot use earns nothing.

Three vocabularies for the same job

Collis and Montgomery’s five tests, Barney’s VRIO and Grant’s appraisal (2019, pp. 120–122) line up closely. The professor’s scripts use VRIN, whose “N” (non-substitutable) is Collis and Montgomery’s fourth test.

VRIO applied · Rothaermel (2024), Strategy Highlight 4.2

The rise and fall of Groupon

Founded in 2008, Groupon turned down a $6 billion offer from Google in early 2011 and went public that November valued at more than $16 billion. A year later its share price had fallen 90%, to a market value under $1.8 billion. Its local deal-making was valuable and, at first, rare. It was not costly to imitate: it was more a sales operation than a technology, entry barriers were negligible, and hundreds of clones and the internet giants moved in (pp. 145–146). Note the link to the five forces: hard-to-imitate resources and high entry barriers are the same fact seen from two sides.

    Moats leak: rigidities, and the capability to renew

    Core rigidity

    A former core competency that became a liability because the firm relied on it too long without honing and upgrading it. Circuit City’s logistics and service were outflanked by Best Buy and Amazon, and it went bankrupt (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 151). Kodak, from Session 1’s fallen giants, is the course’s other example.

    Dynamic capabilities

    The ability to create, deploy, modify, reconfigure, upgrade or leverage resources over time (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 152). Nvidia’s path is the earlier deck’s illustration: a gaming GPU firm that bet on deep learning in 2013, met an unexpected market in crypto mining with a dedicated line in 2017, and found AI demand arrive at scale with ChatGPT (Yoffie & von Bargen, 2024, p. 5).

    Invest and upgrade

    Because all resources depreciate, Collis and Montgomery prescribe continual investment: Disney’s $50 million Roger Rabbit revived its animation. Upgrading adds new resources, as Intel added a brand; Sharp built from a 1964 calculator to LCDs and a Viewcam that took 20% of the Japanese market within six months of its 1992 launch. Xerox, which thought its copier capability inimitable, slept while Canon took leadership (1995, pp. 124–127).

    Question 03 · Value chain analysis · Porter (1987)

    Where in its activities is the difference made?

    VRIO tests what the firm has. The value chain shows where it is used. Porter treats every business unit as a collection of discrete value activities, from sales to accounting, and locates advantage there: “It is at this level, not in the company as a whole, that the unit achieves competitive advantage” (Porter, 1987). He groups the activities into nine categories. Session 1 made the complementary point with Porter (1996): advantage lives in the fit among activities, which this section measures.

    Five primary activities

    Make it, move it, sell it, support it

    Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. They create the product, deliver and market it, and provide after-sale support.

    Four support activities

    The inputs and infrastructure

    Firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development and procurement. Rothaermel’s textbook relabels some of them (supply chain management, distribution, R&D, information systems, accounting and finance), but the logic is Porter’s (2024, p. 156).

    The question to ask

    Difference, or cost?

    Every activity adds some value and some cost. Value chain analysis asks which activities generate the firm’s differentiation and which drive its costs (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 154). The professor’s 2025 deck read Patagonia’s chain as differentiation, not cost leadership; Session 7 tests that claim.

    Why a corporate-strategy article supplies the tool

    Porter (1987) introduces the value chain to judge diversified firms: a corporate parent can add value by transferring skills between similar value chains or by sharing activities, such as a common sales force or logistics network. Session 9 returns to that use. Today the tool is applied to a single business, which is how it starts in the article: advantage is built activity by activity, inside one unit.

    Question 04 · SWOT analysis · Andrews (1971), via Collis & Montgomery (1995)

    Where do the inside and the outside meet?

    SWOT is older than any tool on this page. Collis and Montgomery trace strategy as a field to Kenneth Andrews’s The Concept of Corporate Strategy (1971), which defined strategy as the match between what a company can do, its strengths and weaknesses, and what it might do, its opportunities and threats. Managers got few ways to assess either side until Porter (1980) gave them the outside; core competence and capabilities then swung the pendulum inside; the resource-based view joins the two (1995, p. 121).

    Session 2 put analysis third in Kaplan, Norton and Barrows’s sequence, under one question: what are the key issues our strategy must address? That step had three parts: external analysis, internal analysis and SWOT. Sessions 3 and 4 did the first; questions 1 to 3 did the second. This is the third.

    The rule that makes a SWOT honest

    Strengths and weaknesses are internal and VRIO decides which is which: a resource that is not valuable is a weakness, and one that is valuable, rare, costly to imitate and organised is a strength and a core competency. Opportunities and threats are external and come from PESTEL and the five forces (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 160). Ask of every strength: if a rival’s SWOT shows the same item, was it ever a strength?

    The SWOT matrix as four questions · after Rothaermel (2024), Exhibit 4.11

    Strengths × Opportunities

    How can internal strengths exploit external opportunities? The “offensive” options; start here.

    Strengths × Threats

    How can strengths reduce the likelihood and impact of external threats?

    Weaknesses × Opportunities

    Which weaknesses stop the firm taking an opportunity, and how can it shore them up?

    Weaknesses × Threats

    Which weaknesses would turn a threat into reality? The “defensive” options.

    Rothaermel, 2024, p. 161.

    The caveat that matters most

    One fact, two boxes

    A strength can also be a weakness, and an opportunity a threat. Rothaermel’s examples: Alphabet’s Silicon Valley location brings talent and venture capital, and also high costs and earthquake risk; climate change threatens carmakers through fuel taxes and opens demand for low-emission cars. A SWOT is only as good as the external and internal analysis done before it (2024, pp. 161–162). It is a snapshot, the professor’s plan adds: it does not say how to prioritise.

            The same tool on another firm · from the earlier deck

            Nvidia’s SWOT ends in three questions, not a list

            Strengths: the CUDA ecosystem and a data-centre share analysts put at 98% in 2023. Weaknesses: one supplier, TSMC, and four customers providing nearly half of revenue. Threat: those customers designing their own chips, and an antitrust investigation opened in 2024 (Yoffie & von Bargen, 2024, pp. 1, 6). The deck turned the matrix into questions, which is the point of the exercise:

            • S–T. How can the CUDA strength blunt the threat of customers’ in-house chips?
            • W–T. How can Nvidia reduce its dependence on TSMC, given the geopolitical risk?
            • W–O. Should it open CUDA to reach more of the market, or would that destroy its key strength?

            The professor’s teaching plan runs the same drill on firms from other sessions, qualitatively: Tesla’s strengths in brand, battery technology, direct sales and the Supercharger network against its past production-scaling problems and reliance on one leader; Twitter’s history of inconsistent profitability; Amazon’s run of opportunities taken, from books to everything, AWS, Whole Foods and Prime Video. For practice, try a firm you know well, or the coffee shop nearest the campus: name one strength, one weakness, its primary and support activities, and one resource you think passes VRIO.

            The case · Patagonia: “Earth Is Now Our Only Shareholder” (2023)

            Arguing all four questions on one company

            In November 2011 Patagonia ran a full-page Black Friday ad in The New York Times: a picture of its fleece jacket above the words “DON’T BUY THIS JACKET”. Black Friday traditionally opens the US holiday shopping season. The ad launched the Common Threads Initiative, which asked customers to reduce, repair, reuse, recycle and reimagine Patagonia products (Trelstad et al., 2023, p. 5). By 2022 Patagonia held the largest share of the US outdoor apparel industry, nearly 10% (p. 2). Bring the case having formed a view, not a summary.

            Note on the case. “Patagonia: ‘Earth Is Now Our Only Shareholder’” (Trelstad, Hsieh, Norris & Pinckney, 2023) is a Harvard Business School case and is not reproduced here; obtain it through the course pack. For this session read pages 1–6 and Exhibits 2, 4, 5 and 9: the history, not the ownership decision. The prompts and readings below are original to this session; full references are at the end.

            Against Q1

            List what Patagonia has and what it has learned to do. Then draw its tree: which one or two competencies feed fleece, T-shirts, wetsuits and repairs alike? If your list has more than six, Prahalad and Hamel would say it is not a list of core competencies.

            Against Q2

            Run environmental credibility through Collis and Montgomery’s five tests. It passes inimitability easily. Who captures its value: Patagonia, its customers, or the 5,000 firms in 1% for the Planet? And what would substitute for it in a buyer’s mind?

            Against Q3

            Find the activities that differentiate. Is the difference made in one box, such as marketing, or across the chain? If a rival copied the ad, the repairs and the pledge, which activities would it still be missing?

            Against Q4

            Write Patagonia’s SWOT in 2011, the year of the ad, using only case facts dated before 2012. Then name the one fact that sits in two boxes and say which box the ad moved it towards.

            The puzzle

            Telling customers not to buy

            For almost any retailer, an ad against its own product on the busiest shopping day of the year is incoherent. It is coherent only if the firm is selling something other than the jacket: a reason to believe the jacket is worth keeping, repairing and passing on.

            The RBV reading

            The ad spends a stock

            The ad is one deployment of an intangible resource, environmental credibility, built by decades of flows. A rival could run the same words tomorrow; without the history, customers would read it as a stunt. That is the difference between an activity anyone can copy and the resource that makes it work.

            What the case does not tell you

            There is no sales figure

            Patagonia is private and does not publish financial data; the roughly $1 billion of revenue and $100 million of net income for 2022 are media estimates (p. 2). The case gives no figure for the ad’s effect on sales. Before calling it a success, say what evidence you would want.

            Profit is part of the resource, not its opposite

            Kris Tompkins, recalling the 1980s: “Because if you’re not successful financially, the experiment’s over” (Trelstad et al., 2023, p. 3). Chouinard goes further and claims that every decision best for the planet has made the company money (p. 3). Treat that as a hypothesis. The RBV can explain why credibility earns returns; it cannot show that every green decision paid.

            What comes next

            In September 2022 the Chouinard family moved all of Patagonia’s voting stock into a purpose trust and all of its non-voting stock into a nonprofit, the Holdfast Collective (p. 11). Session 6, Shared Value and Competitive Advantage, reads Prahalad and Hamel again with Ghemawat and Rivkin to ask how these resources become an advantage, and how sharing value through operations differs from giving profits away. Session 7 asks which competitive position the activities support.

            The question the room usually splits on

            Is Patagonia’s core competence environmental credibility or making things that last? The first is a reputation: an intangible resource, path-dependent in Collis and Montgomery’s sense, which travels to anything that can carry the message. The second is a capability in Prahalad and Hamel’s sense, collective learning in materials, design and repair, which travels only where durability and repair matter. Both readings fit the case, and they imply opposite things about growth. A reputation is spent by every product that does not live up to it; a capability is strengthened by every product that uses it.

            Take a side before class. The exhibit workshop below gives you the numbers; you will be asked which piece of evidence would change your mind.

            Finding 1

            The floor is the signal

            The proposal saves money only in a bad year, $5m at a 5% margin. A commitment that costs nothing in good years and something in bad ones is what makes it credible; dropping it the first time it bites tells everyone it was conditional.

            Finding 2

            The pledge is common; the stock is not

            Five thousand firms have signed the pledge. None of them has paid it since 1986, a score 1.9 times the threshold and sixteen awards. A rival can copy the flow from today; it cannot buy the stock (path dependence).

            Finding 3

            The stock was paid for

            In pledges that bite in bad years, in a best seller’s costs printed in public, and in fifteen years of slow growth after the 1991 caps. That bill is the cost a rival would have to pay to imitate, which is the “I” in VRIO.

            What the numbers do and do not settle

            They settle that the giving rule bites when profits fall, that the credibility stock is old and verified by outsiders, and that the pledge on its own is no longer rare. They do not settle whether the pledge or the ad earned money: the case has no sales figure for either, and the faster growth of the 2010s cannot be credited to the stock without one. Nor do they settle which of the case’s two growth figures for the 1980s is right.

            Round 2 · 15 min · fours

            Role play: the board tests the competence claim

            Roles: Head of Product (states Patagonia’s core competence in one disaggregated sentence); CFO (asks who keeps the returns, and what happens if the people who carry the competence leave); rival strategist (may copy the visible product, but must explain the cost, time and coordination needed to copy the routines, then asks whether customers could get the benefit another way); Director (asks: better than whom? An improvement on the firm’s own past is not superiority). The Head of Product revises the claim once.

            What to hand in

            The revised claim, and the test that forced the revision: imitation, substitution, appropriability or superiority.
