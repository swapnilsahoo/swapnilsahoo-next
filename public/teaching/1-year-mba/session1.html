Introduction to Strategy

Almost everyone in the room already uses the word “strategy” at work. This session makes the word precise enough to be useful — and precise enough to be wrong, which is what makes it a decision tool rather than a compliment.

Question 01 · Porter (1996)

What is strategy?

Porter’s argument (Porter, 1996) begins with a negative, and the negative is the useful half: being better at what everyone is already doing is not a strategy. Running the same activities faster, cheaper or with fewer defects is operational effectiveness. It is necessary, it is often urgent, and it is almost always copied.

Operational effectiveness

Doing the same activities better than rivals do them.

  • • Competes on execution quality
  • • Diffuses quickly — consultants, suppliers and staff carry it
  • • Converges the industry: everyone ends up similar
  • • Rivalry shifts to price, and margins compress
Strategic positioning

Deliberately performing different activities, or the same activities differently.

  • • Competes on the choice of what to do
  • • Requires saying no — a real trade-off
  • • Preserves difference rather than erasing it
  • • Defensible because copying one piece does not work

The three tests a position has to pass

1. A distinct position

Serving different customers, different needs, or the same needs through a different configuration of activities.

2. Genuine trade-offs

Choosing this position must make some other position harder to occupy. If a rival can add your move without giving anything up, it is not protecting you.

3. Fit across activities

The choices reinforce one another, so advantage rests on the whole system rather than any single part. Rivals must copy everything, or copying gains them little.

This is why Porter (1996) treats fit as the real source of durability. A competitor can hire your supplier, match your software or poach your head of operations. Reproducing a system of dozens of mutually reinforcing choices is a different problem entirely.

    Question 02 · Collis & Rukstad (2008)

    Why do firms need one?

    The blunt answer: without a strategy, every function optimises locally and the firm competes on whatever the loudest customer asked for most recently. Collis and Rukstad (2008) make the point diagnostically — most executives cannot state their firm’s strategy in a single clear sentence, and a strategy nobody can state cannot guide anybody’s choices.

    Element 1

    Objective

    A single measurable end, with a deadline. Not “be the leading provider” — leading on what, by when?

    Element 2

    Scope

    Where you will and will not compete: customer, geography, product, stage of the value chain. Scope is where the word no appears.

    Element 3

    Advantage

    Why the chosen customer picks you and keeps picking you — the customer-facing promise and the activities that make it deliverable.

    What a strategy statement is not

    • Mission — why the organisation exists.
    • Vision — what it wants to become.
    • Values — how people are expected to behave.

    All three are useful and none of them is a strategy. They do not tell a product manager which segment to drop. The test is simple: if the sentence cannot be used to refuse an opportunity, it is not doing strategic work.

    Draft one, in 35 words

    Pick any firm you know well. Fill all three and read it back.

    Question 03 · Mintzberg (1987)

    How is strategy actually made?

    Mintzberg’s objection (Mintzberg, 1987) is not that planning is wrong but that it is an incomplete account of where strategies come from. He describes the work as crafting — closer to a potter at the wheel, hands on the material, adjusting as the shape reveals what it will and will not do, than to an analyst producing a plan for others to execute.

    Deliberate

    Intended, articulated in advance, and realised roughly as intended. Requires a predictable enough environment for the intention to survive contact with it.

    Emergent

    A pattern that forms through a stream of decisions without anyone having intended it up front. Recognised in hindsight — then, if it is working, adopted on purpose.

    Realised

    What the firm actually did. Almost always part deliberate and part emergent; the intended strategy that never happened is simply unrealised.

    Why this matters for a manager, not just a theorist

    If strategies can emerge, then a central managerial skill is detecting a pattern already forming in your own organisation — usually in a corner of the business that nobody is presenting at the review. The manager’s job is less to invent the future in a planning cycle than to notice which experiments are quietly working and decide which of them to make deliberate. That skill is diagnostic, and it is the one a purely planning-led view trains out of you.

      The case · Elon Musk at Tesla (2024)

      Arguing all three questions on one company

      Bring the case having formed a view, not a summary. These prompts are written for this session and map onto the three questions above — the case supplies the evidence, and you supply the argument.

      Note on the case. “Elon Musk at Tesla” (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024) is a Harvard Business School case and is not reproduced here — obtain it through the course pack. The prompts below are original to this session; full references are listed at the end.

      Against Q1

      Name the trade-offs Tesla has actually made — the things it has chosen not to do that a conventional carmaker does. For each, say what it would cost a legacy manufacturer to copy it. If a rival could adopt it on Monday without giving anything up, it is operational effectiveness, not position.

      Against Q2

      Write Tesla’s strategy statement in the Collis and Rukstad (2008) form — objective, scope, advantage — in under 35 words. Then write the same statement for a legacy carmaker’s EV division. Which one tells a manager what to refuse?

      Against Q3

      Tesla’s 2006 plan — sports car, then cheaper car, then cheaper still, plus power generation — is publicly documented. How much of what Tesla became was in that plan, and how much emerged? Energy storage, the charging network, direct sales, software margin: deliberate or emergent?

      The question the room usually splits on

      Is Tesla’s advantage the activity system — batteries, software, charging, direct distribution, manufacturing — or is it the founder? Both readings of the evidence are defensible, and they imply opposite things about durability. An activity system survives its architect. A founder-dependent advantage has a succession problem disguised as a strategy.

      Take a side before class. You will be asked which specific evidence would change your mind — a position you cannot imagine losing is not an argument, it is a preference.

      In the room

      Studio: two rounds, one decision each

      Round 1 · 12 min · pairs

      The imitation test

      One partner names a firm and its supposed advantage. The other plays a well-funded rival and has three minutes to describe exactly how they would copy it. If the copy is straightforward, the “advantage” was operational effectiveness. Swap and repeat.

      What to hand in

      One sentence: the single choice that made the copy expensive.

      Round 2 · 15 min · fours

      Role play: the board asks for the sentence

      Roles: CEO (states the strategy in one sentence), CFO (asks what we stop funding), Head of Sales (brings a large customer who falls outside scope), Director (asks what would have to be true for this to fail). The CEO may not answer any question with “both”.

      What to hand in

      The opportunity the CEO refused, and the reason given.

      Last eight minutes — retrieval, no screens

      On one side of A4, by hand: define strategy without using the words best, leading or world-class; name one trade-off your own employer has made; and name one strategy there that emerged rather than being planned. Ungraded. The point is to find out which of the three you cannot yet do.
