Session 2 · Strategy of Disruption: Tesla

Tesla nearly died in 2008.
It nearly died again in 2018.

A company that survives one near-bankruptcy might be lucky. One that survives two, a decade apart, on the same founder's watch, is running a different kind of experiment entirely. Was Tesla's chaos a necessary cost of disruption, or a preventable failure of execution that got lucky twice?

The Case

Tesla's 2008–2012 entry into the U.S. auto industry: the Roadster shipped in 2008 with the company weeks from bankruptcy; the Model S launch in 2012 required a last-minute $465M DOE loan and a Daimler equity investment just to survive production ramp-up. The pattern repeated in 2017–2018's "production hell" for the Model 3, where Elon Musk slept on the factory floor and the company burned through cash at a rate that had short-sellers openly betting on collapse.

First Principle Question

If Tesla had been run by a professional, risk-averse CEO instead of a founder willing to bet the company twice, would Tesla exist today — or would it have quietly failed, the "safe" way, before ever reaching Model 3 volume?

Two Decades, Two Near-Death Experiences

The Master Plan, Under Fire

First Principle Question

Musk's 2006 "Secret Master Plan" (sports car → funds an affordable car → funds an even more affordable car → solar) took 16 years to execute. Would you have kept funding it in year 10, with no proof it worked yet?

2008: The Roadster Crisis

The 2008 financial crisis hit exactly as Tesla needed a fresh funding round to finish Roadster deliveries. Musk personally put in his last liquid capital; the round closed on Christmas Eve 2008 — Tesla later confirmed it had days of cash left. This wasn't a hypothetical near-miss; it was reported as literally days from payroll failure.

2017–18: Production Hell

The Model 3 ramp fell dramatically short of targets (Musk had promised 5,000/week; actual output stalled in the hundreds for months). Musk moved onto the factory floor. Short interest in Tesla stock hit historic highs on public bets the company would run out of cash before the Model 3 became profitable.

First Principle Question

Operational effectiveness (doing things well) is not strategy (doing things differently) — Porter's core distinction from Session 1. Which of Tesla's near-death moments was actually a strategy failure, and which was just poor operational execution of a sound strategy? Does the distinction even matter to an investor who lost money either way?

Classroom Debate: The Founder Premium

Divide into two groups. Group A argues Musk's willingness to bet the company twice was the source of Tesla's advantage — a professional CEO would have played it safe and lost to incumbents with more capital. Group B argues Tesla survived despite Musk's risk-taking, not because of it, and got lucky on financing timing both times.

Bring evidence: Tesla's stock performance, the fate of other EV startups from the same era (Fisker, Coda) that also bet big and failed, and what "founder mode" governance research (Hambrick & Mason's Upper Echelons Theory) predicts about high-risk-tolerance leaders.
