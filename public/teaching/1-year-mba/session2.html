Framework for Strategic Analysis

Purpose, the Value Gap & the Analysis Before the Choice

Strategy does not start with analysis. The firms Kaplan and Norton studied start with what they are for and how far they mean to go, and only then ask what stands in the way. This session builds that front end — purpose and values, a goal precise enough to measure, the gap it opens — and argues it on Tesla.

Session 1 asked what strategy is and tested Tesla against Porter, Mintzberg and Collis & Rukstad. It left a question those tools cannot answer alone: why would anyone choose the hardest entry in the car industry and keep going through losses in all but two quarters from its 2010 listing to mid-2018? The answer sits upstream of the strategy, in a purpose and in a goal big enough to force the strategy.

Question 01 · Collins & Porras (1996)

What is the firm for?

Collins and Porras studied companies that stayed great across generations and found one pattern underneath: core values and a core purpose that stay fixed while business strategies and practices keep adapting (Collins & Porras, 1996, p. 65). Their name for the discipline is preserving the core while stimulating progress, and a vision is the tool that says which is which.

A well-conceived vision has two parts (p. 66). Core ideology says what the organisation stands for and why it exists; it is discovered by looking inside, and it should outlast products, markets and leaders. The envisioned future says what the organisation aims to become; it is created, it is meant to be reached, and then it is replaced (pp. 71, 75).

Core ideology · preserve it

What we stand for and why we exist. The yin of the scheme.

  • • Core values: a few enduring tenets, usually three to five, that need no external justification (p. 67)
  • • Core purpose: the reason for being; never fulfilled, like a guiding star on the horizon (p. 69)
  • • Discovered by looking inside, never set by looking at the market (p. 71)
  • • Its job is to guide and inspire, not to differentiate (p. 71)
Envisioned future · stimulate progress

What we aspire to become, achieve or create. The yang.

  • • A vision-level BHAG: a Big, Hairy, Audacious Goal of 10 to 30 years with a clear finish line (p. 73)
  • • A vivid description: the goal translated from words into pictures (p. 74)
  • • Created, not discovered; there is no right answer to analyse your way to (p. 75)
  • • Once reached it must be replaced, or the We’ve Arrived Syndrome sets in (p. 76)

Tesla’s version · the plan Musk published in 2006

One sentence of purpose, four lines of future

The case opens with Musk’s 2006 purpose for the company: to help speed the move “from a mine-and-burn hydrocarbon economy towards a solar electric economy” (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 1). The same blog post set out the sequence below (p. 5).

Read with Collins and Porras, the sentence is a core purpose: it can never be finished, and it has already led Tesla beyond cars. The four steps are an envisioned future with a finish line, an affordable car, much as Ford’s “democratize the automobile” was a qualitative target BHAG (Collins & Porras, 1996, p. 72). The case never lists Tesla’s values; the evidence points to reasoning from first principles (“two false premises”, p. 4) and to intensity (the “Ultra Hardcore” email, p. 7).

The wording has moved. Rothaermel (2023, p. 50) records Tesla’s vision changing from accelerating the transition to sustainable transport to sustainable energy after SolarCity, and the 2017 change of name from Tesla Motors to Tesla, Inc. Hold that for the vision-drift workshop.

STEP 1

Build a sports car

Roadster, released 2008 at $109,000; 500 sold that year (p. 6).

STEP 2

Model S, launched 2012; Consumer Reports’ best car tested in 2013 (p. 8).

STEP 3

Use that money to build an even more affordable car

Model 3 at $35,000, unveiled 2016 with more than 232,000 pre-orders in its first week (p. 9).

STEP 4

Also provide zero-emission power generation

Solar and storage: SolarCity bought for $2.6 billion in 2016 (p. 10).

Why publishing it was itself strategic

Collins and Porras’s mechanism: a clearly stated ideology attracts people whose values fit it and repels those whose values do not (1996, p. 71) — engineers, early buyers, investors willing to wait. Having said what came next, Tesla also could not quietly drop it. Mintzberg would add that the plan fixed a direction without fixing the route.

The question to hold open

Step 4 arrived in November 2016 as the SolarCity purchase, with Musk the largest shareholder of both firms and his cousins running SolarCity (p. 10), while Tesla prepared Model 3 production. Was that the purpose being honoured, or capital and attention leaving the one thing that had to work? The cash figures are in the exhibit workshop.

Three confusions Collins and Porras warn against

1. Purpose versus BHAG

“Think of the core purpose as the star on the horizon to be chased forever; the BHAG is the mountain to be climbed.”

Collins & Porras (1996, p. 74)

2. Values you wish you had

Aspirations belong in the envisioned future or the strategy. Listing a value the organisation does not actually hold breeds cynicism among the people who know better (p. 71).

3. Ideology versus competence

Sony’s miniaturisation is a core competence that might leave its strategy; its purpose would not (p. 73). Competences change over the decades, ideology does not. The rule that follows: if it is not core, it is up for change (p. 73).

    Two discovery tools

    The Mars Group: name the five to seven people you would send to recreate the organisation’s best on another planet; they articulate the values (pp. 67–68).     The We’ve Arrived Syndrome

    The complacency that follows a reached BHAG with no new one: NASA after the moon landings, Ford after democratising the car, start-ups after going public (p. 76). Tesla delivered 500,000 vehicles in 2020 (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 13). What was the next mountain?

    Read against the case. A core value is one that still guides conduct when following it is costly (p. 67); a product, channel or operating practice can change without abandoning it. Name one Tesla choice that could be revised without touching its purpose. Then name a compromise that would make you doubt a value Tesla claims.

    One vision, two useful tests

    The two readings use “vision” at different levels. Collins and Porras separate an enduring identity, the core ideology, from a future worth pursuing. Kaplan and Norton make the ambition concrete enough to guide analysis: a measure, a niche and a date (Kaplan et al., 2008, p. 3). Each test catches what the other misses.

    Working vocabulary

    Five words, one job each

    Purpose, values, vision, mission and BHAG are used loosely in boardrooms and inconsistently in the literature. The useful test is not what a sentence is called but which job it does. The glossary below is the earlier deck’s, kept and sourced; the table under it shows where the readings disagree.

    The one-line test. Ask what the sentence lets someone decide: who we are (values), why we exist (purpose), where we are going (vision and BHAG), how we will get there (mission, in the textbook’s sense, and strategy).

    The foundation · yin

    Core values · who we are
    The few beliefs that guide behaviour and decisions at every level, held even when they cost something (Collins & Porras, 1996, p. 67).
    Core purpose · why we exist
    The reason for existing beyond making money; pursued forever, never completed (pp. 68–69).

    The future · yang

    Vision · where we are going
    What the organisation wants to become. Kaplan and Norton add the discipline: to guide strategy it must name a measure, a niche and a date (Kaplan et al., 2008, p. 3).
    Mission · how we will get there
    In the textbook’s sense, what the organisation does, for which markets, and how it will achieve the vision, often written with “by” (Rothaermel, 2023, p. 51).

    Question 02 · Kaplan, Norton & Barrows (2008)

    How large is the value gap, and how will it be closed?

    Kaplan and Norton treat strategy as a quest for value with a measured destination. Step 1 reaffirms the mission, values and vision. Step 2 turns the vision into a stretch target and measures it against “what could be achieved by maintaining the status quo with the existing strategy” (Kaplan et al., 2008, p. 4). The difference is the value gap, and closing it is the new strategy’s job.

    This is where Question 1 turns into a number. Kaplan and Norton cite Collins and Porras on BHAGs (p. 4): a leader’s stretch target is what stops an organisation settling for incremental improvement. The BHAG supplies the ambition; the value gap says how far it is, and the strategic themes say who will carry which part of the distance.

    Mission · stable

    Why the organisation exists

    A brief, usually one-sentence statement of fundamental purpose: what the organisation provides and the goal its people have come together to pursue. Google’s: “To organize the world’s information and make it universally accessible and useful” (p. 2).

    Values · stable

    How it will behave

    Values prescribe the behaviour, character and culture the company wants. Whole Foods calls its core values “the soul of our company” and says they must hold however large it grows (p. 2).

    Vision · three to ten years

    Where it intends to be

    A concise, external, market-facing statement of mid- to long-term goals. To guide strategy it must be measurable as well as inspiring. “To be our customers’ preferred provider” is laudable and gives no guidance (p. 3).

    Why the gap comes before the five forces

    A hostile industry is hostile relative to a goal. Only once the gap is sized can the analysis ask what stands in the way of closing it, and which theme it threatens. That is Question 3.

    Question 03 · Kaplan, Norton & Barrows (2008), step 3

    What does a disciplined analysis add before the choice?

    With the gap sized, step 3 asks what stands in the way of closing it. Kaplan and Norton’s analysis has two halves and a summary (Kaplan et al., 2008, p. 5). The external half scans the macro environment (PESTEL), the industry (Porter’s five forces), the firm’s financial ratios against its competitors, and the competitors themselves on a two-by-two map. The internal half uses Porter’s value chain to find the activities the firm will perform “differently or better” than rivals (p. 5). A SWOT then compresses it all into a short list of key issues the strategy must address (p. 5).

    What the discipline adds is direction. Analysis without a goal describes an industry. Analysis after a goal looks for the constraints on closing this gap by this date, and those are what the strategy in step 4 has to answer.

    One constraint, found by analysis

    In 2012 Tesla’s chief technical officer realised that the plan to sell 500,000 cars a year by 2020 would need the world’s entire 2012 output of lithium-ion batteries (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 12). That one line of analysis became the Gigafactory.

        Losses in all but two quarters from listing to mid-2018 (p. 11); the runway is computed in the exhibit workshop.

      Step 4 is the strategy that answers them. Read Tesla’s moves against the list: the Gigafactory, a factory designed “like a product” (p. 13), company-owned stores, the Supercharger network, and money from a public listing and a government loan (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, pp. 6–7). Which issue did the strategy answer worst?

      The case · Elon Musk at Tesla (2024)

      Arguing all three questions on one company

      Bring the case having formed a view, not a summary. The prompts below map onto the three questions; the case supplies the evidence and you supply the argument. The case ends in January 2024 with Musk saying he is uncomfortable growing Tesla into a leader in AI and robotics without about 25% of the votes (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 15). Every question on this page eventually leads there.

      From Session 1 · Porter (1996)

      Strategy is not operational effectiveness

      Doing the same activities better gets copied; doing different ones, and refusing what they preclude, does not. Sort Tesla’s troubles into failures of execution and choices of position.

      From Session 1 · fit

      Advantage lives in the system

      Ask what a legacy carmaker would have to give up to match Tesla, not what it would have to add.

      From Session 1 · Mintzberg (1987)

      Strategy also emerges

      The 2006 plan was deliberate. Much of what Tesla became was not in it. Today’s question is what held the pattern together.

      The trap this case sets

      It is easy to explain Tesla backwards: it succeeded, so the strategy was brilliant. Hold yourself to the harder standard. Say what you would have concluded from the 2018 data alone, and what evidence would have changed your mind.

      Against Q1

      Apply Collins and Porras’s tests to Tesla’s purpose. Is the 2006 sentence (p. 1) a purpose or a goal? Run the serial-killer test: what would be lost if Tesla stopped? Then set the 2022 remark that the mission “does somewhat broaden” with the Optimus robot (p. 15) beside it. Whose purpose is it, Tesla’s or Musk’s?

      Against Q2

      Write Tesla’s vision with all three of Kaplan and Norton’s components and size the gap as the board saw it in 2016: 500,000 a year against about 100,000. Which theme carried most of it? Tesla delivered 245,506 cars in 2018 and 499,647 in 2020 (Exhibit 11a). Against which deadline did it close the gap?

      Against Q3

      Run step 3 as a planner in 2012. Which key issue would you have ranked first, and did Tesla’s strategy answer it or override it? Name one finding of the five-forces read that the purpose made irrelevant, and one it could not.

      The question the room usually splits on

      Is Tesla’s purpose the company’s, or the founder’s? Collins and Porras say the most lasting contribution of company builders is the core ideology (1996, p. 66), and that a good envisioned future keeps people going even if the leaders who set it disappear (p. 75). Tesla is a live test of that claim. If the purpose lives in the organisation — the engineers it attracted, the factory, the stores — the 25% request is a governance question. If it lives in Musk, it is a succession problem disguised as a strategy.

      Take a side before class. You will be asked which specific evidence would change your mind — a position you cannot imagine losing is not an argument, it is a preference.

      Discussion questions

      1. Was Tesla’s chaos a necessary, calculated cost of its strategy, or a preventable failure of execution?
      2. How did Tesla turn the industry’s barriers — suppliers, dealers, charging — into parts of its position, and what did each refusal cost?
      3. Why might investors in mid-2018 value Tesla above Ford and GM? As a GM executive, would you have treated it as a serious threat?
      4. Do SolarCity and the 25% request complete the purpose, or threaten the fit that sustains the strategy?
      5. Tesla’s stated purpose moved from sustainable transport to sustainable energy (Rothaermel, 2023, p. 50), and in 2022 the mission “does somewhat broaden” (p. 15). Core preserved, or core moving?
      6. As an investor or a director, would you vote to give Musk about 25% of the votes (p. 15)?

      The exhibit workshop · 25 minutes

      Raise capital now, or trust the ramp?

      It is May 2018. You sit on Tesla’s board. Analysts estimate the company must build at least 5,000 Model 3s a week to turn a profit this year, and some wonder whether it will run out of cash by December (Sull & Reavis, 2019, p. 1). On this month’s analyst call a question about capital requirements is waved away as “boring” (p. 1). The quarter just closed is in front of you.       What the numbers do and do not settle

      They settle that the bet was a race. In May 2018 the cash covered two to three quarters at the burn rates of the time; the turn came inside that window, as Model 3 deliveries rose from 8,180 in the first quarter to 56,065 in the third and free cash flow turned positive. The position was real too: buyers paid close to list price for the Model S.

      They do not settle that the bet was wise. One more slipped quarter would have forced capital on worse terms, and 2018 still ended at less than half the 500,000 target (Serafeim & Migdal, 2024, p. 12). A board that pressed for a raise in May was not wrong; it was buying insurance against the ramp failing.

      Round 1 · 12 min · pairs

      The four-line case brief

      From the 2024 case, write four separate lines: Tesla’s core purpose, one candidate core value, a dated ambition, and one operating choice. Cite a page or exhibit for each, and label your own wording as a proposal. Then your partner runs Collins and Porras’s tests on it: one more “why is that important?”; the Random Corporate Serial Killer (what would be lost if Tesla closed?); and, for the value, would Tesla keep it if it were penalised for holding it? Swap and repeat.

      What to hand in

      The four lines after the tests, each with its evidence, and the line your partner made you rewrite.

      Round 2 · 15 min · fours

      Role play: the board sets the target

      Roles: CEO (proposes a vision with a measure, a niche and a date), CFO (sizes the value gap against what the existing strategy would deliver by that date, and asks which theme closes how much), Head of Operations (names the binding constraint the analysis found; for Tesla in 2016, battery supply), Independent director (asks what can change and what cannot, and which stakeholder bears the cost if the gap is closed badly). Use Tesla in 2016 or a firm of your own. The CEO may not state a target without a date.

      What to hand in

      The vision sentence, the value gap as two numbers and a date, the theme carrying most of it, and one measurable signal that would make the board revise the strategy.
