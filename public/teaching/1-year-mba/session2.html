Elon Musk at Tesla

Putting Session 1’s tools under load

Session 1 gave you two lenses and a test. This session points them at one company and asks whether they hold. Tesla entered the most hostile industry a new firm could pick, lost money for a decade, and was still valued above General Motors. Either the frameworks explain that, or they do not.

Carried forward · Session 1

The three things you already have

Nothing in this session is new theory. Every claim below is a tool from Session 1 being loaded until it either holds or breaks.

Porter (1996)

Strategy is not operational effectiveness

Doing the same activities better gets copied. Doing different activities, and refusing what they preclude, does not. Watch for which of Tesla’s troubles are OE failures and which are strategic choices.

Porter · fit

Advantage lives in the system

A rival can copy one activity. Copying dozens of mutually reinforcing ones is a different problem. Ask what a legacy carmaker would have to give up to match Tesla, not what it would have to add.

Mintzberg (1987)

Strategy is also a pattern that emerges

The 2006 master plan was deliberate. Energy storage, the charging network, direct sales and software margin were not all in it. Decide how much of Tesla was planned and how much was realised along the way.

The trap this session sets

It is very easy to explain Tesla backwards. It succeeded, therefore the strategy was brilliant. Hold yourself to the harder standard: state, from the 2017 data alone, what you would have concluded — and what evidence would have changed your mind.

Strategic intent

The plan Musk published in 2006

Musk posted the plan publicly under the title “The Secret Tesla Motors Master Plan (just between you and me).” Its stated purpose was “to help expedite the move from a mine-and-burn hydrocarbon economy towards a solar electric economy.” Four lines, in order.

STEP 1

Build a sports car

Roadster, 2008. Low volume, high price, proves the technology.

STEP 2

Use that money to build an affordable car

Model S, 2012. The step this session tests hardest.

STEP 3

Use that money to build an even more affordable car

Model 3, 2017. Where production hell happened.

STEP 4

Provide zero-emission power generation

Solar and storage. The step critics call a distraction.

Why publishing it was itself strategic

A plan announced in advance recruits people who want that outcome — engineers, early buyers, investors willing to hold through losses. It also removes the option of quietly abandoning it. Porter would call the published refusal a commitment device; Mintzberg would note that it set a direction without specifying the route.

The question to hold open

Step 4 arrived as a $2.6 billion acquisition of SolarCity in 2016 — during the Model 3 ramp, and of a company chaired by Musk himself. Was that the master plan being completed, or attention and capital leaving the one thing that had to work? You will have the cash figures in hand before you answer.

Framework for strategic analysis · Kaplan & Norton (2008)

Strategy as a quest for value

Session 1 asked what strategy is. Kaplan and Norton ask where it comes from. In their reading, successful companies do not start with analysis. They start with purpose and an ambition precise enough to measure, and the distance between that ambition and where the current strategy would take them is the job the new strategy has to do. They call that distance the value gap (Kaplan & Norton, 2008).

“What business are we in and why?”

Mission, vision and values.

STEP 4 · LATER

“How can we best compete?”

Strategy formulation: where and how to compete.

Mission · stable

Why the organisation exists

A brief statement, usually one sentence, of the organisation’s fundamental purpose: what it provides and the goal its people have come together to pursue. Google’s: “To organize the world’s information and make it universally accessible and useful.”

Values · stable

How it will behave

Core values prescribe the behaviour, character and culture the company wants. Whole Foods calls its values “the soul of our company” and argues they must survive however large the company grows.

Vision · three to ten years

Where it intends to be

A concise, external, market-facing statement of mid- to long-term goals. To guide strategy it must be measurable as well as inspiring. “To be our customers’ preferred provider” is laudable and, in Kaplan and Norton’s view, no guidance at all.

Now do it for Tesla

Tesla’s 2006 purpose is a mission: it says why, not how much or by when. Its plan “to sell 500,000 cars a year by 2020” (Sull & Reavis, 2019) is closer to a quantified vision. Write Tesla’s vision with all three components. Then state its value gap as it stood at the end of 2018: what annual run rate had Tesla reached, and how far was that from 500,000 cars a year? The quarterly deliveries further down this page give you the numbers.

Why this sits before the five forces

Kaplan and Norton put analysis third, after purpose and goals. You cannot judge whether an industry is hostile until you know what you are trying to achieve in it. The five forces below are their Step 3: the test of whether Tesla’s gap could be closed in the industry it chose.

The conclusion the framework actually produces

Four of five forces are hostile and the fifth is no help. The correct Porterian recommendation in 2003 was do not enter. Tesla entered anyway and, by 2017, was worth more than General Motors. That is not a footnote to the framework — it is the problem this session exists to work on. Either the analysis was wrong, or it was right and something outside it decided the outcome.

Operational effectiveness, not strategy

Production hell, classified

Session 1’s first distinction earns its keep here. Tesla’s worst years were not a failure of position. The position was working — the Model S outsold every established large luxury sedan and barely discounted. What failed was the ability to build the thing at rate.

An OE failure

Excessive automation

Tesla skipped the industry’s standard pilot production stage and over-automated final assembly. Musk’s own verdict: “Excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake… Humans are underrated.” The fix was manual rework and a tent-built production line — expensive, and copied from no one.

A strategic choice

Owning the battery

Building the Gigafactory was not an efficiency move. Tesla’s 2020 volume goal would have required roughly the world’s entire 2012 output of lithium-ion cells. Making the scarcest, costliest component in-house answers supplier power directly — a different activity, not the same one done better.

Sort these yourself

For each, decide whether Tesla was failing at execution or making a strategic choice. There is a defensible case on both sides of two of them.
