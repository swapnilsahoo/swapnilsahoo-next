Official Session 06 · Creating Competitive Advantage

Two companies sell bottled water.
One charges $1, one charges $4. Both are profitable.

Competitive advantage is created two ways, and only two: raise the value a customer perceives (V), or lower what it costs you to deliver it (C). The gap between the two — the "economic wedge" — is what advantage actually is, once you strip away the buzzwords.

The Assigned Reading

Ghemawat, P., & Rivkin, J. W. (1998). "Creating Competitive Advantage," Harvard Business School Note. Their framing: a firm has an advantage when the gap between the value it creates (V − C) exceeds a rival's — and that gap can come from external sources (an industry-wide shift the firm exploited first) or internal sources (superior activities the firm alone can execute). Most strategy narratives overclaim brilliance and underclaim the boring, activity-level execution that actually produces the wedge.

Case · Patagonia: "Earth Is Now Our Only Shareholder"

Advantage From an Unlikely Source

Patagonia's 2022 ownership transfer to the Holdfast Collective looks like a values statement. Read through the V−C lens, it's also a competitive-advantage move: it raises perceived value for a specific, high-willingness-to-pay customer segment (environmentally-committed outdoor consumers) in a way a shareholder-owned rival structurally cannot credibly copy — the "internal source" here is an ownership structure, not a factory process.
