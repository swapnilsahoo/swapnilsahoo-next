Global Strategy & MNC

Which advantages cross a border, and how much a multinational should standardise or adapt

Corporate strategy has three dimensions: which stages of the value chain to own, which products and services to offer, and where in the world to compete (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 298). Sessions 9 and 10 examined scope and ownership routes. Today takes geography. A global strategy is the part of corporate strategy that seeks advantage against foreign and domestic rivals around the world (p. 378), in a world the textbook’s indicators put at no more than 10 to 25% globalised (p. 381).

The case calls Amazon a dominant global player with more than 200 million Prime members worldwide (case, pp. 1–2), yet almost every business it describes is American. The textbook adds some of the rest: Amazon opened its first sites outside the US, in Britain and Germany, in 1998, closed its domestic marketplace in China in 2019, and was losing money on retail abroad while AWS paid for the expansion (pp. 295–296, 333). Today you ask which of its advantages travel, which must be rebuilt market by market, and what it should standardise or adapt.

Differences can be managed in three ways

Publisher’s reading record

Competing globally · Rothaermel (2024), §10.1–10.2, pp. 377–391

Why go abroad, and what it costs

The textbook treats going global as a value decision like any other. A firm should expand abroad if doing so raises the value it creates or lowers its costs by more than the extra costs of operating across borders, and the move should reinforce whatever its advantage already rests on: low cost, differentiation or value innovation (pp. 384, 387). Four terms first.

Globalisation · p. 377

Closer integration and exchange between countries, businesses and peoples, made possible by falling trade and investment barriers, better telecommunications and cheaper transport.

Multinational enterprise · p. 378

A company that deploys resources and capabilities to procure, produce and distribute goods and services in at least two countries. The textbook calls it the engine of globalisation.

Foreign direct investment · p. 378

Investment in value-chain activities abroad. Airbus spent more than $1 billion on a plant in Alabama to build jetliners close to its US customers and outside the reach of tariffs.

Global strategy · p. 378

The part of corporate strategy that seeks to gain and sustain competitive advantage against foreign and domestic rivals around the world.

Three reasons to go · pp. 384–387

  • A larger market. Firms whose advantage rests on economies of scale or scope gain most, because a bigger market deepens the advantage. For firms from small home economies, such as IKEA, Nestlé or LEGO, going abroad may be the only way to reach efficient scale.
  • Low-cost inputs. Cost leaders go abroad for cheaper raw materials and labour: China for manufacturing, India for IT and business-process services, where English-speaking graduates were as important as wages.
  • New competencies. Differentiators invest abroad to sit inside communities of learning and reap location economies, such as AstraZeneca’s move into the Boston biotech cluster. GE’s Bangalore team built an $800 handheld ECG against $2,000 for a conventional machine, and it is now entering Western markets.

Three costs of going · pp. 387–391

  • Liability of foreignness. The extra cost of doing business in an unfamiliar culture and economy, and of coordinating across distance. Walmart’s exits from Germany and South Korea in 2006 are the textbook’s example (below).
  • Loss of reputation. A global supply chain exposes the brand to what suppliers do: worker suicides at Foxconn, Apple’s main supplier, in 2010, and the Rana Plaza factory collapse in Bangladesh in 2013.
  • Loss of intellectual property. Kawasaki, Siemens and Alstom formed joint ventures to build China’s high-speed rail network. They allege their partners built on their technology and now compete against them abroad; the Chinese side says it “re-innovated”.

Three stages · pp. 379–381

  1. 1.0 (1900–1941): everything at home except sales and distribution; knowledge flows one way, out from headquarters.
  2. 2.0 (1945–2000): smaller self-contained copies of the firm in a few key countries, more locally responsive but costly to duplicate.
  3. 3.0 (from 2001): global collaboration networks that put each function wherever cost, capability and PESTEL factors are best.

Retrenchment? · pp. 382–384

The textbook asks whether a “Globalization 3.1” is reversing the trend: the financial and euro crises, Brexit, rising nationalism and a US–China “techno cold war” that may split trade into two blocs and make supply chains deliberately redundant.

Liability of foreignness in practice · Strategy Highlight 10.1, p. 388

Walmart in Germany: the home recipe did not travel

Entry. Facing a saturated US market, Walmart bought the 21-store Wertkauf chain and 74 hypermarkets from Spar in 1998 and ran the US playbook: greeters, cheer, bagging at the checkout.

Friction. Employees resisted the transplanted values; the first boss, sent from Arkansas, spoke no German and made English the in-house language. Without its US scale and distribution, and under stricter labour laws, Walmart’s prices landed in the middle of the market.

Exit. Discounters Aldi and Lidl were already there, and Metro started a price war. Walmart left in 2006 and sold its stores to Metro. The advantage was real at home and did not survive the distance.

Where to compete · Ghemawat (2001); Rothaermel (2024), pp. 391–395

Distance still matters: the CAGE framework

Ghemawat’s argument is that companies routinely overrate foreign markets because their tools count only the size of the prize. Country portfolio analysis ranks markets by GDP, consumer wealth and propensity to consume, and so ignores the costs and risks of getting there, most of which come from distance (p. 138). Distance has four dimensions, and each hurts different industries.

The cautionary tale · pp. 137–144

Star TV: a big market, misread twice

Star TV launched in 1991 to beam television by satellite to the richest 5% of Asia. English was most target viewers’ second language, so it planned to use cheap English-language programming, and satellites would let it sidestep geography. News Corporation bought out the founders for $825 million between 1993 and 1995 (p. 137). Losses from 1996 to 1999 came to about $500 million (p. 138).

Cultural mistake: evidence from Europe showed mass audiences in large countries prefer local programming, so China and India would need heavy investment in localisation (p. 142). Administrative mistake: soon after Rupert Murdoch called satellite TV a threat to totalitarian regimes, China banned reception of foreign satellite services (p. 144).

How much distance weighs on trade

Ghemawat reports a gravity-model study by economists Jeffrey Frankel and Andrew Rose. A 1% rise in a country’s wealth or size raises its trade by only about 0.7 to 0.8%; the distance variables move it far more (p. 138; restated in Rothaermel, pp. 391–392).

The four dimensions, and what each one hits

Summarised in this page’s words from Ghemawat (pp. 140–147) and the textbook’s adaptation (Exhibit 10.6, p. 392). Read the right-hand line of each card against Amazon’s businesses.

C · Cultural · pp. 140–142

Grows with different languages, ethnicities, religions and social norms, and the absence of ties between peoples. Trade between countries that share a language is about three times that between countries that do not.

Hits hardest products with heavy linguistic content (television), products tied to national or religious identity (food: Hindus do not eat beef), and features that vary with taste, as with the small cars and appliances Japanese buyers prefer.

A · Administrative · pp. 140, 142–144

Grows with no shared currency, trading bloc or colonial history; political hostility; and weak legal and financial institutions. Most often the barrier is the target government itself: tariffs, quotas, limits on FDI, favours for local firms.

Hits hardest industries a government protects: large employers, national champions, anything vital to national security, staples, “entitlements” such as health care, natural resources, and businesses with large sunk local investments.

G · Geographic · pp. 140, 144–145

Grows with physical remoteness, but also a country’s size, access to the sea, topography, climate and time zones, and the quality of its roads, power and communications.

Hits hardest goods with a low value-to-weight ratio (cement), fragile or perishable goods (glass, fruit), and services that need close connectivity or local supervision. Even capital flows thin out with distance.

E · Economic · pp. 140, 145–147

Grows with differences in consumer income and in the cost and quality of natural, financial and human resources, infrastructure and information.

Hits hardest firms that win by replicating one model at scale: they should go where economies look like home. Wal-Mart in Canada was virtually a carbon copy of the US business; in India it would be a very different one. Firms that win by arbitrage go looking for difference.

Distance changes the ranking · Ghemawat, p. 147

Tricon Restaurants International, which ran Pizza Hut, Taco Bell and KFC outside the US, had to choose where to invest scarce cash. On country portfolio analysis, Mexico ranked 16th of 20 fast-food markets. Adjusting for geographic distance from Dallas lifted it to sixth; adding the common border and the trade agreement put it second, after Canada. Tricon’s president called Mexico one of its top two or three priorities.

The textbook adds · pp. 393–395

Cultural distance can be scored with Hofstede’s dimensions: measured from the US, Australia scores 0.02, the UK 0.09 and Canada 0.12, against Russia’s 4.42. Language and culture are why internet companies such as Amazon and Google run country-specific sites, and why they are often outflanked by native providers: Alibaba and Baidu in China, Ozon and Yandex in Russia. And distance is partly firm-specific: a cadre of cosmopolitan managers shrinks it.

Once you know where: how to enter · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 395–396

Entry modes along the investment–control continuum

Least investment, least control

Exporting

Make at home, sell abroad. One of the oldest forms, often a test of whether a market is ready.

Contract-based

Licensing & franchising

Long-term contracts that rent out a product, trademark or service format.

Shared ownership

Equity alliances & joint ventures

Shared risk and local knowledge; some hosts require a joint venture before a foreign firm may operate.

Most investment, most control

Subsidiary: acquisition or greenfield

Full ownership, as with Tesla’s Shanghai factory. Best protection against loss of reputation and of intellectual property.

Analysis · where Amazon sits

The textbook reads the continuum as a stage model of growing commitment, and says it does not fit firms born global, such as internet companies (p. 395). A website reaches every country on day one. But the textbook also says Amazon’s global presence rests on country-specific sites and local logistics services (p. 298), and warehouses and delivery fleets cannot be exported. So which of Amazon’s businesses can enter from the left of the continuum, and which must start on the far right?

How to compete · Rothaermel (2024), §10.4, pp. 396–403

Global integration versus national differentiation

Every multinational feels two opposing pressures. Integrating and standardising across countries cuts cost and reinforces a cost-leadership strategy; responding to each country raises differentiation and usually cost as well. The two trade off, just as low cost and differentiation do inside one business (p. 396). The course outline calls the tension global integration versus national differentiation; the academic literature calls the tool built on it the integration–responsiveness framework (Prahalad & Doz, 1987); the textbook renames it the cost-responsiveness framework for ease (p. 397 and note 68). All three describe the same grid.

The pull to integrate · p. 396

The globalisation hypothesis

Many executives assume consumer needs are converging, an idea the textbook traces to Theodore Levitt. If they are, one product made at global scale wins on cost. Toyota sells the Prius in more than 90 countries, and most vehicles are now built on global platforms, modified, sometimes only cosmetically, for local tastes and standards. But Ford learned by lack of sales that buyers would not accept one near-identical car, the Mondeo, everywhere.

The pull to adapt · p. 396

Local responsiveness

National differences persist because institutions and cultures differ. Walmart sells live snakes, eels and toads for cooking in China; IKEA sells kimchi refrigerators and metal chopsticks in South Korea; McDonald’s uses chicken and fish instead of beef in India and offers a teriyaki burger in Japan, while its fast-food model stays the same. Responsiveness costs money, sometimes more than the scale economies it gives up.

Two streaming strategies · Strategy Highlight 10.2, pp. 400–401

Netflix runs local experiments; Disney exports blockbusters

Netflix, transnational. It spent over $1 billion on Korean films from 2016, $500 million of it in 2021, and introduced 80 Korean films and series in five years, treating each as an experiment. Squid Game was made for Korea, dubbed into 13 languages, and more than 95% of its viewers were outside Korea. The textbook links the fit to Netflix’s presence in 190 countries.

Disney, international. It spends heavily on a few productions and exports them; Avengers: Endgame cost about $400 million, more than 20 times a season of Squid Game. Each hit becomes a franchise of sequels, parks and merchandise. Even Jeff Bezos publicly praised Netflix’s internationalisation strategy as hard to do.

Case In 2023 Amazon spent over $19 billion on TV and music content, including licensing, against $13 billion by Netflix (case, p. 2). The case does not say how that money splits between global and local titles.

Positions move · pp. 401–403

Google launched YouTube Red in the US and then only in culturally close Australia and New Zealand (international); relaunched it as YouTube Premium in 17 countries, including culturally distant ones (multidomestic); and aims to use its recommendation engine to go transnational.

The hypothesis to test

The earlier global-strategy notes argue that Amazon is transnational: it standardises its technology backbone and brand promise and localises what customers touch. Test that business by business in the next activity. One company can sit in more than one quadrant.

      Advantage in an international context · Rothaermel (2024), §10.5, pp. 404–407 · Porter (1990)

      Where national advantage comes from: Porter’s diamond

      If firms can source anything from anywhere, location should stop mattering. The textbook calls that the death-of-distance hypothesis and finds it more hype than reality: leading firms in an industry cluster in particular countries, such as software and biotech in the US, chips in Taiwan, cars in Germany and business-process outsourcing in India (p. 404). Firms from a country that leads an industry tend to be its strongest global competitors. Porter’s answer to why is the diamond. Magretta’s summary of it: comparative advantage credits cheap labour or natural resources, while Porter credits productivity and innovation, which arise where the local environment is most forward-looking, dynamic and challenging (Magretta, 2012, pp. 214–215).

      Factor conditions · pp. 404–405

      Natural, human and other resources, plus capital markets, institutions, research universities and infrastructure. Natural resources matter less than you might think: several resource-rich countries host no world-leading firms, while resource-poor ones such as Denmark, Israel, Japan, Singapore and Switzerland build world-class human capital instead.

      Demand conditions · p. 405

      Sophisticated home customers who hold firms to a high standard. Dense cities, humid summers and costly energy made Japanese buyers demand small, quiet, efficient air conditioners; a sparse, remote population without landlines made Finns demand tough handsets, and Nokia became an early leader.

      Competitive intensity · pp. 405–406

      Firms that fight hard at home outperform rivals that do not. German carmakers face fierce domestic rivals, demanding drivers and a no-speed-limit autobahn, and most sit within easy driving distance of one another. Tesla chose Germany for its first European factory to be part of that crucible.

      Related & supporting industries · p. 406

      World-class suppliers and complementors nearby. Switzerland carried an early lead in industrial chemicals into pharmaceuticals; Toyota’s success rested largely on a tight network of Japanese suppliers, an advantage that faded when rapid growth forced it to add suppliers abroad that could not match the quality.

      Clusters · p. 407

      The diamond explains why world-class firms gather in regional clusters: computing in Silicon Valley, medical devices around Chicago, biotech around Boston, carmakers in southern Germany, fashion in northern Italy, IT in Bangalore. Porter: “Paradoxically, the enduring competitive advantages in a global economy lie increasingly in local things—knowledge, relationships, and motivation that distant rivals cannot match” (as quoted in Rothaermel, 2024, p. 407).

      CaseTextbookAnalysis

      What advantage is Amazon carrying abroad?

      Start from what the sources say Amazon does well. Bezos built a virtual store offering low prices, vast selection and convenience, with unlimited inventory and low fixed costs (case, p. 1). Amazon pioneered recommendations drawn from similar customers’ purchases and built an ad network on its customer data (p. 2). Its fulfilment centres and delivery vans have made it a logistics company that competes with UPS and FedEx (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 297), and it knows what each customer views and buys (p. 334).

      Now apply the chapter. Software and know-how are codified and travel cheaply. Warehouses, delivery fleets and supplier relationships are tied to a place and have to be built again, which is why the textbook ties Amazon’s presence abroad to local logistics. Customer data and trust are earned customer by customer, so a new country starts near zero. And a marketplace connects buyers and sellers who can trade with each other: how many of those connections cross a border?

      Contrast · the company the earlier deck used

      IKEA standardised the product; Amazon standardises the platform

      In 1989 furniture retailing was still a fragmented national industry, with preferences that varied by region (Bartlett & Nanda, 1990, p. 1). IKEA went the other way: 12,000 of its 20,000-plus products formed a core common to stores worldwide (p. 6), store layouts were standardised to the centimetre (p. 7), and design, purchasing and distribution stayed central (p. 8). Yet a manager asked whether to adapt for the US, where customers wanted shelves with space for a TV set while European shelves were built for books (p. 11).

      Analysis IKEA’s product is the same in every country and the store is a copy. Amazon’s “product” in each country is a local assortment, delivered by a local network, on a shared platform. Which is easier to take across a border?

      Open questions for class · what none of the sources tells you

      How big is Amazon abroad? Neither the case nor its exhibits split revenue or profit by country. What would you need to know before calling it a global firm rather than a US firm with foreign branches?

      Why China and India differ. It closed its domestic marketplace in one market led by native platforms and doubled down on another with a strong native rival. Which CAGE dimensions, or which entry choices, would explain the difference?

      What Prime promises where. The case gives Prime’s prices only for the US. Should Prime cost the same everywhere? And if a benefit such as two-hour delivery runs only in some US cities, what is a member elsewhere paying for?

      Who pays for distance? If AWS profits fund money-losing retail abroad, is that the internal capital market doing its job, or a subsidy for expansion whose costs exceed its value? Revisit Session 9’s diversification logic.

      For class

      Six questions to argue

      Come with a position on each and the page of the case or reading that supports it. Where the sources are silent, say what you would need to find out.

      01

      The case calls Amazon a dominant global player, yet nearly every business it describes is American, and the textbook says Amazon lost money on retail abroad. Is Amazon a global company or a US company with foreign branches? What evidence would settle it?

      02

      Amazon closed its domestic marketplace in China in 2019, where Alibaba and JD.com dominate, but invests billions in India against Flipkart. Use CAGE and the textbook’s point that natives often outflank global internet firms to argue why the two might turn out differently.

      03

      Prime is one brand with more than 200 million members worldwide, yet the case reports only US prices and US-only benefits such as two-hour delivery in some cities. Is Prime an act of global integration, of national differentiation, or both? Say which parts you would standardise and which you would let each country set.

      04

      Amazon outspent Netflix on content in 2023. Should Prime Video copy Netflix’s transnational bets on local productions or Disney’s international export of blockbusters? What does “TV is hardly cement” imply for the choice?

      05

      AWS looks like Amazon’s most standardised business. Which administrative or geographic pressures could still force it to localise, and would they change its strategy on the grid or only its entry mode?

      06

      Build Amazon’s US diamond. Which corner explains its advantage best, and does that advantage travel? If Porter is right that lasting advantage is local, what does that predict for Amazon against local champions?
