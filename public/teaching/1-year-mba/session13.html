Recall → connect → carry forward · Sessions 1–12

The strategy story so far: Sessions 1–12

Bring Sessions 1-12 together through fictional Nila Foods. A focused weekday-meal promise has led to questions about purpose, industry economics, capabilities, shared value, competitive positioning, complements, packaging access, borders and execution. The final session asks who sets the objectives, who can challenge decisions and who receives benefits or bears risk. A coherent and efficiently executed strategy still needs credible governance and ethical scrutiny.

One continuing classroom story. Nila Foods is a fictional Indian meal-service for working adults. Follow its choices from Session 1; later expansion ideas are proposals to test.

  1. Session 1 · What is strategy?

    Operational effectiveness improves similar activities; strategy requires positioning, trade-offs and fit. State objective, scope and advantage, then compare intended plans with emergent action using evidence.

    Nila Foods: Nila proposes preordered weekday meals on clustered routes, declines instant citywide delivery and studies actual customer behaviour.

  2. Session 2 · Framework for Strategic Analysis

    Purpose and values guide what endures, while a quantified ambition opens a same-date gap from the baseline; closing that gap requires external and internal evidence.

    Nila Foods: Nila separated easing meal burdens and honest food-safety disclosure from its growth target, then compared that target with its projected baseline.

  3. Session 3 · Industry Analysis: The Fundamentals

    Industry boundaries, PESTEL and five forces connect external change to price, cost and investment; complements and possible responses then raise questions about the wider game.

    Nila Foods: Nila traced ingredient-price pressure, supplier bargaining, lunch substitutes and easy entry before asking whether a neighbouring seller could also help it.

  4. Session 4 · Beyond the Five Forces

    Value nets and PARTS show how co-opetition changes added value, while uncertainty shapes commitments; credible moves therefore require examining internal strengths.

    Nila Foods: Nila considered sharing a collection point with a rival and testing the rules through a limited pilot while demand remained uncertain.

  5. Session 5 · Internal Analysis & Resource-Based View

    Resources and capabilities support advantage when they improve buyer benefits or relative costs and pass tests against rivals and substitutes; value-chain analysis then checks their economic contribution.

    Nila Foods: Nila examined kitchen routines, route know-how and trust, asking which linked activities rivals could imitate and whether it could retain their benefits.

  6. Session 6 · Creating advantage and shared value

    Use the value stick to distinguish value created from value captured. Test shared value against both business and social effects, including burdens shifted elsewhere.

    Nila Foods: Nila asks whether demand forecasts and reusable containers reduce waste and cost, including the return and cleaning work.

  7. Session 7 · Business Strategy: Differentiation, Cost Leadership & Blue Ocean

    Porter distinguishes cost leadership, differentiation and focus; Kim and Mauborgne test value innovation; Ghemawat and Pisano examine threats, commitments and capabilities.

    Nila Foods: Nila compares dependable delivery and relative cost within its chosen segment. Its value curve and four-actions proposal must demonstrate new demand and viable economics before supporting a blue ocean claim.

  8. Session 8 · Entrepreneurship, Platforms, Technology & Innovation

    Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne test platform participation and pricing; Bower and Christensen distinguish disruptive trajectories from sustaining improvements.

    Nila Foods: To extend its offer, Nila asks whether customers and nutrition creators could benefit from one another. An experiment tests complement quality, retained use and cross-side effects; success alone would not establish disruption.

  9. Session 9 · Corporate Strategy: Vertical Integration & Diversification

    Porter tests attractiveness, entry cost and the better-off mechanism; Campbell, Goold and Alexander ask whether this parent beats alternative owners.

    Nila Foods: The ecosystem need not be owned. Nila asks whether owning a packaging supplier would improve meal delivery enough to outweigh capital, coordination and harmful misfit; contracts and specialist owners remain alternatives.

  10. Session 10 · Alliances, mergers and acquisitions

    Choose the route that supplies the needed capability after governance, uncertainty, price and integration costs. Ownership and synergy require an evidence-based mechanism.

    Nila Foods: Nila compares a packaging contract, an alliance and acquiring a supplier; it tests control needs and integration risks before committing.

  11. Session 11 · Global Strategy & MNC

    CAGE identifies relevant cross-border frictions; adaptation, aggregation and arbitrage specify different responses with different organisational demands.

    Nila Foods: For an overseas pilot, Nila would test menus and delivery windows locally while considering shared forecasting tools. The proposal needs evidence that its domestic advantage can travel and a suitable entry route.

  12. Session 12 · Organisational Design

    Execution depends on decision rights, usable information, incentives and culture; structure should address the coordination problem in the chosen strategy.

    Nila Foods: Nila could delegate bounded order exceptions to cluster managers while retaining clear quality escalation. The team must test whether its authority, information and rewards support reliable meals as operations become more complex.

Why this leads to Session 13

Nila's original promises and value-creation hypotheses now become tests of accountability. Governance asks who oversees management and whether the chosen measures support enterprise health. Ethics asks what targets, incentives or outsourcing could make people fail to notice. The business model traces how customers, creators, suppliers, staff, delivery partners and owners receive value or bear risk. Use the earlier competitive and organisational analysis to specify an oversight mechanism rather than rely on good intentions.

Carried forward · Session 12

Good execution still needs a defensible purpose

A team can execute an ill-chosen objective extremely well. Session 12 examined decision rights, information and culture. The final session asks who sets the objective, who can challenge it, whose interests are affected, and how a profitable business model can create incentives that deserve scrutiny.

The strategy connection: governance sets authority and accountability; ethics asks what conduct and consequences are defensible; the business model explains how activities create and capture value. These questions meet whenever a target shapes how people behave.

Two required readings

Who is leadership accountable to—and what might it fail to see?

Question the model behind the metric

Bower and Paine challenge the shareholder-centred version of agency theory and propose a company-centred model concerned with enterprise health, innovation and renewal. Their argument does not eliminate accountability; it asks what leadership should be accountable for and over what horizon.

Reading task: identify one investment that a short-term performance measure might discourage. Then design a way for the board to examine its strategic value without giving management an unlimited budget.

Publisher’s reading record

Good intentions do not guarantee good judgement

The authors explain how poorly designed goals, motivated blindness, indirect blindness, gradual deterioration and excessive attention to outcomes can hide ethical problems. A code of conduct alone cannot resolve these mechanisms.

Reading task: find the point where a manager’s interest could change what they notice. Propose a change to the decision process that makes inconvenient evidence harder to ignore.

Publisher’s reading record

Core concept

The principal delegates. The agent sees more.

In an agency relationship, one party delegates work or decisions to another. Interests may differ, and the agent may know more about effort, quality or risk than the principal. The analytical problem is to design accountability under those conditions. Shareholders and managers are one example; boards and executives, or a platform and a service provider, offer others.

MechanismWhat it can help reveal or alignHow it can fall short
Independent oversightA board or review body examines strategy, risk and management performance.Formal independence is weak if directors lack information, expertise, time or willingness to challenge.
Incentive designRewards can connect managerial decisions with desired long-term outcomes.A narrow measure may encourage gaming; a delayed reward may still omit harms borne by others.
Disclosure and assuranceConsistent reporting and independent checks reduce information gaps.Audits depend on scope, evidence quality and the ability to investigate exceptions.
Decision and escalation rightsPeople know who approves sensitive choices and where concerns can travel.A reporting channel is ineffective if raising a concern leads to retaliation or silence.
Separate model from mandate: agency theory is an analytical lens. The Bower–Paine reading debates how to understand corporate leadership. Do not turn either into a universal statement of directors’ legal duties across jurisdictions.

Original practice examples

Find the mechanism before you prescribe a remedy

The five labels below follow Bazerman and Tenbrunsel’s reading. Each classroom example is invented.

Blind spotA signal in an invented organisationA practical challenge
Ill-conceived goalsA service team is rewarded only for closing tickets, so it closes unresolved complaints.Inspect reopened cases and customer resolution; ask what the target encourages people to omit.
Motivated blindnessA manager avoids examining a star seller’s unusually low complaint rate.Ask a reviewer without a sales bonus to sample the underlying customer experience.
Indirect blindnessA firm assumes a contractor’s harmful practice is solely the contractor’s concern.Trace who benefits, who sets the pressure and who can inspect the work.
Slippery slopeSmall exceptions to a quality check gradually become the accepted routine.Record exceptions and review cumulative drift against the original standard.
Overvaluing outcomesA risky shortcut is praised because the launch happened to succeed.Review the decision using information available at the time, including the possible downside.

Business model lens

Follow the money—and the consequence

A business model links a customer proposition to activities, resources, partners and a revenue–cost logic. The ethical inquiry adds a second map: who receives value, who supplies essential inputs, who carries the risk and who can contest a decision?

Subscription

A recurring fee can support a continuing service. Ask whether renewal terms are clear, cancellation is usable and the service remains valuable after sign-up. Measure retention alongside informed choice.

Marketplace

A platform can help buyers and sellers find one another. Ask how access, rankings, fees and dispute resolution affect each side. A rule that raises platform income might weaken participant trust.

Advertising

Advertising can fund a useful service. Ask whether users can distinguish paid placement, understand material data uses and make meaningful choices. Revenue alone does not settle those questions.

Integrated service

Combining activities can improve convenience. Ask which information should flow across units and where consent, professional independence or safety require boundaries. Synergy needs a specific mechanism.

Make the analysis testable: for one model, write “Revenue increases when ___; this may encourage ___; the affected party is ___; we would detect the problem by ___.” Treat the statement as a hypothesis to investigate.

Board challenge: investment or managerial empire building?

A diversified firm proposes another expansion. Defend a long-term investment case, then argue the strongest case against it. Ask for milestones, an independent review, downside exposure and a credible exit criterion. A long horizon should improve the evidence required, not remove it.

See it · explain it · test it

Governance: inspect the target and its consequences

A renewal dashboard can look healthy while hiding why customers stayed. Start with the mechanism that earns revenue, follow the incentives it creates and ask whose evidence reaches an informed reviewer. Good intentions and good financial results cannot independently establish defensible conduct.

Revenue is an input to judgement, not the whole judgement

  1. 1. Business model: Who pays for value?
  2. 2. Goals + incentives: What gets rewarded?
  3. 3. Actions + effects: Who carries risk?
  4. 4. Independent review: Who can challenge?
  5. 5. Decision + revisit: What evidence next?

A proposed business model creates incentives and actions, which produce revenue and stakeholder consequences. An informed review challenges both the target and its effects, then changes the proposal or its safeguards.

One concept, three contexts

Hypothetical · original teaching scenario

Nila Learn: a green renewal dashboard

Fictional Nila Learn sells subscriptions. A growth team is rewarded for renewal revenue and quietly makes cancellation difficult. Renewals rise while support complaints accumulate. The manager celebrates the target and delegates complaint handling to a contractor. This scenario is not a claim about any named company.

Student task: trace the incentive, identify the affected learner, and request cancellation-journey evidence as well as unit economics. A reviewer outside the growth bonus chain needs an escalation route that the manager cannot silently suppress.

Discuss and defend your interpretation

Would a profitable outcome justify the cancellation design? Give the strongest economic defence, then assess it using informed choice, long-term trust and evidence available before the result.

Global example · Patagonia ownership announcement, 2022

Patagonia: separate economic value from control

On 14 September 2022, Patagonia announced a structure allocating voting stock to the Patagonia Purpose Trust and nonvoting stock to the Holdfast Collective. Its announcement describes directing money left after reinvestment toward environmental work. Patagonia’s ownership announcement.

Teaching inference: ownership design can protect a stated purpose while retaining an operating business. It does not settle every trade-off or independently establish environmental outcomes. Ask who challenges investment, growth and distribution decisions.

Discuss and defend your interpretation

Which evidence would let a reviewer assess both financial continuity and the stated environmental purpose? Distinguish decision authority, financial benefit and measured impact.

Give a claim, supporting evidence, a trade-off and one observation that could change your view.

Indian example · Tata Code of Conduct, 2015 edition

Tata: a concern needs a route, not just a value

The 2015 Tata Code of Conduct addresses stakeholders beyond investors and includes routes for raising concerns with protection against retaliation. These are published commitments; their presence is not proof that every concern is handled effectively. Tata’s 2015 code, core principles and raising concerns.

Teaching inference: compare a commitment with operating evidence—awareness of the route, access to a reviewer, response records and protection after escalation. This is a governance exercise, not a statement of current legal duties.

Discuss and defend your interpretation

If a high-performing manager controls all complaints about the manager’s own team, what independent route would you propose? What evidence would show that staff can actually use it?

Give a claim, supporting evidence, a trade-off and one observation that could change your view.
