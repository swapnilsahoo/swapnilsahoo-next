Diversification Strategies

Why firms move into new businesses, when it pays, and whether Amazon has spread itself too thin

Diversification is the second question of corporate strategy: what range of products and services should the firm offer (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 319)? The evidence is unkind to both extremes. Firms that stay in one business and conglomerates that own unrelated ones tend to do worse than firms with moderate, related diversification (p. 328). So the test of a new business is never whether the firm can enter it. It is whether owning it makes the business, and the firm, worth more.

Amazon started in 1995 selling books online. By 2024 it also ran a cloud business with over $90 billion of revenue, an ad network with $47 billion, a film studio, a grocery chain bought for $13.4 billion and a primary-care company bought for $3.9 billion (case, pp. 1–3). The case opens on Andy Jassy deciding whether to keep entering “seemingly unrelated businesses” or to focus on a few (p. 1). Put bluntly: is Amazon spreading itself too thin, or are the businesses more related than they look?

Types of diversification · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 319–323

How far from the core? Five positions on one ladder

A firm diversifies when it widens the range of products and services it offers or the regions it serves. Several product markets is a product diversification strategy; several countries, a geographic one; both at once, a product–market diversification strategy (p. 320). To say how diversified a firm is, the textbook uses Richard Rumelt’s scheme, which asks two things: what share of revenue comes from the main business, and how far the units share core competencies (p. 321).

Type 1

Single business

More than 95% from one business

The rest is not yet significant. Birkenstock makes one product; Meta earns almost all its revenue from online ads (p. 321).

Competencies: used in the one business

Type 2

Dominant business

70–95% from one business

At least one other activity earns the rest. About 80% of Harley-Davidson’s revenue is motorcycles; parts, merchandise and logo licensing make up the remainder (p. 321).

Competencies: shared by the main and minor businesses

Type 3a · related

Related-constrained

Under 70% from one business

A new business is entered only if it can use existing resources and competencies. ExxonMobil took its oil exploration skills into shale gas by buying XTO Energy (p. 322).

Competencies: generally shared across the businesses

Type 3b · related

Related-linked

Under 70% from one business

Some businesses link to the main one, others do not. The textbook’s own example is Amazon (p. 322), listed with Disney and GE (p. 323).

Competencies: some businesses share them

Type 4 · unrelated

Conglomerate

Under 70% from one business

Few if any links. Samsung, Berkshire Hathaway, Yamaha and the Tata group are the textbook’s conglomerates (pp. 322–323).

Competencies: few if any shared

The bars only illustrate the revenue thresholds; like the textbook’s own schematics, they are not drawn to any firm’s numbers. The first variable separates types 1, 2 and the rest; the second separates related from unrelated (p. 321). Text version: Rothaermel’s Exhibit 8.10, p. 323.

The textbook’s verdict on Amazon

Related-linked, not a conglomerate

Rothaermel traces Amazon from one product, books, to a wide range of goods built on its online retailing capabilities, then a platform open to outside sellers, cloud computing, devices and streamed content for Prime members, and files it under related-linked (p. 322). The label claims that the links exist but are uneven. The Amazon section below tests that claim one business at a time.

Case

What the case lets you measure

The case does not report revenue by business, so Rumelt’s 70% line cannot be applied exactly. It does give three anchors for 2023: total revenue of $575 billion, AWS above $90 billion and advertising above $47 billion (pp. 1–2). That puts AWS at roughly 16% of revenue and advertising at roughly 8% (this page’s arithmetic).

Analysis

Relatedness is a claim about competencies

Rumelt’s second variable is not “same industry”. It is whether units share competencies in products, services, technology or distribution (p. 323). A cloud service and an online store look unrelated by industry code. Whether they are related depends on what they actually share, which is a question about Amazon’s resources from Session 5.

Motives · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 299–300, 334–335; Porter (1987)

Why firms diversify, and whose interest each motive serves

The textbook gives five reasons firms grow: to increase profitability, lower costs, increase market power, reduce risk, and motivate managers and employees (p. 299). It then grades them, and they are not equal (pp. 334–335). Sorting a motive is the first step in judging a diversification move, because the same deal can be defended with a good reason and pursued for a bad one.

Value-creating

Higher profit, lower cost

Increasing profits and lowering costs are clearly tied to competitive advantage (p. 334). For diversification the route is economies of scope: two or more outputs produced together for less than each would cost alone, with the same resources and technology. The textbook’s example is Amazon offering many product categories off one retailing expertise and one network of fulfilment centres (p. 302).

Only under conditions

Market power, risk, internal capital

More market power can add to advantage but can bring antitrust action (p. 334). Growing to reduce risk has fallen out of favour with investors (p. 334): shareholders can diversify their own portfolios more cheaply, without paying acquisition premiums, so risk reduction should be a by-product, not the motive (Porter, 1987, pp. 7, 11). An internal capital market adds value only if headquarters allocates capital better than outside markets would (Rothaermel, p. 332).

Managerial

Empire, job security, perks

Some growth benefits managers more than shareholders: empire building, job security, corporate jets and retreats at expensive resorts. CEO pay often tracks firm size more closely than firm performance (p. 300), and the textbook calls perks and job security illegitimate reasons to grow (p. 335). Porter’s version: executives confuse company size with shareholder value (1987, p. 11).

Diversification and performance · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 328–333

When diversification pays: the inverted U, the discount and the premium

The question to ask of any diversified firm is whether its businesses are worth more under its management than they would be if each were managed on its own (p. 328). That is Porter’s better-off test in other words. The research the textbook summarises answers it by type of diversification.

Diversification discount · pp. 328–329

Unrelated diversifiers are often valued at less than the sum of their units. GE Capital earned half of GE’s profits on a third of its revenue before the financial crisis and nearly sank the group when credit froze. When GE announced the spin-out in 2015, its stock rose 11%, adding some $28 billion of market value: a rough measure of the discount it had carried.

Diversification premium · p. 329

Related diversifiers are, on average, valued at more than the sum of their units. To earn it, diversification must lower costs through economies of scale, raise value through economies of scope, or do both (p. 329).

The discount depends on institutions · p. 329

The discount holds where capital markets are developed, as in the United States. In emerging economies such as India, unrelated groups like Reliance can gain advantage by filling gaps in capital markets and courts. The business-groups deck under Go deeper takes this further.

What each kind of diversification can create, and what it costs

Related diversification

Sources of value

Economies of scope; economies of scale; financial economies from restructuring and internal capital markets.

Sources of cost

Coordination costs, which rise with the number, size and types of linked businesses; influence costs.

Unrelated diversification

Sources of value

Financial economies only: restructuring and internal capital markets.

Sources of cost

Influence costs: managers manoeuvring for capital and resources, and the misallocation that follows.

Paraphrased from Rothaermel, Exhibit 8.14 (p. 330) and pp. 332–333. Related diversification can create more, but it is also the only one that pays coordination costs: the links have to be managed.

Managing the portfolio: the BCG matrix, internal capital and restructuring

The outline’s two articles

Goold and Luchs (1993) trace four decades of management thinking on why firms diversify; Laeven and Levine (2007) ask whether the diversification discount also applies to financial conglomerates. Neither text was in the course-pack folder this page was built from, so the page does not summarise them. Read both for class and test the textbook’s claims above against them; full references are under Readings.

Internal capital markets

Headquarters knows more about its units than outside investors do, so it can, in principle, send capital to the best returns, sometimes at a lower cost. That creates value only if it allocates better than external capital markets would. GE’s AAA-rated finance arm lowered its cost of capital until the crisis cost it the rating in 2009 (p. 332).

Restructuring and spin-outs

Restructuring refocuses a firm on its core competencies: spin out weak units, and free strong ones that were subsidising them under extra layers of bureaucracy (p. 330). eBay spun out PayPal; Johnson & Johnson and GE split themselves up in 2023–24 (p. 331).

On the matrix’s axes, though, a cash cow sits in a low-growth market; a high-share unit in a fast-growing one is a star. The label describes the cash AWS throws off, not its position.
  • Whole Foods. Share axis: 1.2% of US grocery against Walmart’s 14.5%, a relative share below 0.1 (case, p. 3; page’s arithmetic). Growth axis: not given. Standing alone, it is at best a question mark, so any case for owning it has to rest on its links to the rest of Amazon, not on grocery itself.

The case · Amazon in 2024 (Gupta & Rodriguez, 2024)

What business is Amazon in? Ten businesses on one matrix

“An online retailer” does not cover a cloud provider, a film studio, a grocery chain and a primary-care company. The case calls the businesses seemingly unrelated (p. 1); the textbook’s own Amazon case asks you to classify them with Rumelt’s types and to say whether AWS is related to the core at all (Rothaermel, 2024, p. 334). Take them one at a time and ask four things: what was the motive, how related is it, which resource does it share, and does Amazon add value as its owner? The core competence–market matrix · Rothaermel (2024), pp. 324–326

Hamel and Prahalad’s matrix, as the textbook adapts it, crosses existing or new core competencies with existing or new markets. Each quadrant is a different diversification move:

Leverage · existing competence, existing market
Use what you are good at to improve your current position: NationsBank’s skill at buying and integrating banks.
Redeploy · existing competence, new market
Recombine current competencies to compete in a new market: Bank of America applying the same skill to Merrill Lynch.
Build to protect · new competence, existing market
Build a competence to defend or extend your position: Coca-Cola’s ten-year effort to create Powerade.
Mega-opportunity · new competence, new market
Build new competencies for markets of the future, as Salesforce did in platform services. Likely the hardest of the four (p. 325).

Session 10 introduced the matrix with NVIDIA. Here it does a second job: the further a business sits from the lower-left, the less it borrows from what Amazon already does well.

Is AWS related to retailing?

Same infrastructure, different customers

The textbook’s answer is yes: Amazon had to build huge data centres for its peak holiday demand and put the spare capacity into cloud computing, gaining economies of scope and scale (Rothaermel, p. 322). The case shows the other side: AWS sells elastic IT infrastructure to many companies, in a fast-growing market where large technology players compete hard, and Bezos kept investing while analysts questioned it (p. 1). The shared resource is real; the customers and the rivals are not retail’s.

The two sources date AWS differently: 2006 in the textbook (p. 297), 2002 in the case (p. 1). This page follows the case.

Case

Amazon already prunes

  • Fire Phone: launched July 2014, a $170 million write-down three months later.
  • Dash Button: launched in 2015, discontinued in 2019 for weak demand.
  • Amazon Go: 23 stores in May 2024; cost, operational complexity and theft risk led Amazon to close several.
  • Amazon Clinic: folded into One Medical in June 2024.

Case, pp. 2–3. Exits are part of a diversification strategy, not proof that it failed.

Analysis

What the finished map tends to show

The three businesses with the largest sales figures in the case, AWS, advertising and Amazon Business, all redeploy competencies Amazon already had into new markets. The top row, where Amazon had to build new competencies, holds the long bets and the write-offs alike: Echo, Whole Foods, One Medical, the Fire Phone, the salon. That is the textbook’s related-linked pattern: a dense core of links and a periphery with few.

Competitive advantage from diversification · the case; Rothaermel (2024), chs. 7, 8 and 12

One firm, many rivals: what travels across Amazon’s businesses

Each business puts Amazon against a different specialist. In cloud computing, large technology players compete hard (case, p. 1). In video, Amazon spent over $19 billion on TV and music content in 2023 against Netflix’s $13 billion, and its app store rivalled Google’s and Apple’s (p. 2). In business supplies it met W.W. Grainger and regional family-run wholesalers; in grocery, Walmart (p. 3). A generalist beats specialists only if something it owns travels from market to market. Read the case for what travels and three candidates stand out.

01

Analysis

A fulfilment and delivery network

The textbook’s own example of economies of scope is Amazon: one network of fulfilment centres lets it offer many product categories at lower cost than each would cost alone (p. 302). The same promise travels in the case: free two-day shipping launched Prime (p. 1), and 24-hour delivery set Amazon Business apart from regional wholesalers (p. 3).

02

Analysis

Technology and purchase data

The textbook names Amazon’s proprietary AI-based recommendation algorithms as a core competency (p. 301). In the case, recommendations go back to Amazon’s early years, the same shopping data sold over $47 billion of advertising in 2023 (p. 2), and AWS sells the infrastructure itself (p. 1). The textbook’s point: a search engine knows what people search for; Amazon knows what each person views and buys (p. 334).

03

Analysis

A paying customer relationship

More than 200 million Prime members spend over four times what other customers spend, and they kept joining as the fee rose from $79 to $139 (case, pp. 1–2). New businesses reach them through it: Prime Music in 2014 (p. 1), streamed content free to Prime subscribers (Rothaermel, p. 322), One Medical at $9 a month for Prime members (case, p. 3).

Two ways the businesses raise each other’s value

Network effects · Rothaermel, ch. 7

The marketplace as a platform

Network effects arise when a product becomes more valuable as more people use it (p. 259). Platforms that match producers with consumers live on them, and markets with strong network effects can tip to a single winner (pp. 276, 281–286); Session 9 covered the chapter. Amazon opened its store to outside sellers in 2000; by 2023 independent sellers made more than 60% of its sales (case, p. 1). More sellers widen the selection that draws shoppers, and more shoppers draw sellers.

Complements · Rothaermel, ch. 12

Razor and razor blades

In the razor–razor blade business model, the first product is sold cheaply, even at a loss, to drive demand for the complementary goods that earn the money (p. 479). Kindle launched at $399 and fell to about $100 after the iPad, as a convenient way to buy Amazon’s digital books and films; Echo takes orders by voice; the Fire Phone’s Firefly button identified objects to buy on Amazon (case, p. 2). Each device is a way into the store.

Analysis

The textbook defines economies of scope as cost savings from producing several outputs together (p. 302). Network effects and complements work on the value side instead: a business is worth more to the same customer because of the others. That changes the test for each link. Ask whether a specialist rival in that one market could copy it. A rival can build a cheaper e-reader; it cannot easily build a store, a delivery network and a membership to go with it.

Complements in practice: Prime, video and Kindle

Case

Prime is the hub

Launched in 2005 as two-day shipping for $79 a year, Prime rose to $99, $119 and then $139 in 2022, and membership kept growing. By 2023 it had more than 200 million members, who spent more than four times what other customers spent (case, pp. 1–2).

Analysis

Why would a retailer outspend Netflix?

Amazon spent over $19 billion on TV and music content in 2023, more than Netflix’s $13 billion, and runs a studio that makes films (case, p. 2). Read as a stand-alone streaming business, that budget has to be earned back from viewers. Read as a complement to Prime, it is part of what members get for a fee that kept rising while they kept spending. The case gives you the facts; which reading you choose decides how the unit should be judged.

Analysis

Which is the razor?

Kindle sold out at $399 in 2007 and was cut to about $100 after the iPad (case, p. 2). The textbook adds that Amazon priced new bestsellers at $9.99 as e-books to promote the Kindle (Rothaermel, p. 480). At different times the device and the books were each priced to sell the other. Either way the profit, if there is one, sits in the store, not in either product alone.

Analysis

Porter’s better-off test runs both ways: the new unit gains from the corporation, or the corporation gains from the unit (1987, p. 8). Video and devices pass it, if at all, mainly the second way. Judged alone, each may look like a loss-maker; judged by what it does for the store and for Prime, it may be the point. Links like these have to be managed, and the textbook warns that coordination costs rise with the number and variety of linked businesses (p. 332). So how should the centre judge a unit whose job is to make another unit sell more?

Whole Foods on Porter’s three tests

In June 2017 Amazon paid $13.4 billion, a 27% premium over the chain’s trading value, for 460 stores and high-income customers. Experts asked whether a technology company with a soaring share price should enter a thin-margin industry at all (case, p. 3). Put the deal through Session 10’s tests.

Fails on the case’s evidence

Attractiveness

Grocery is large, about $800 billion of US spending, but highly competitive, with razor-thin margins and most grocers, Whole Foods included, struggling (case, p. 3). Porter asks for a structure that supports returns above the cost of capital, or an entrant able to change it (1987, p. 8).

Doubtful

Cost of entry

A 27% premium for a chain with 1.2% of the market against Walmart’s 14.5% (case, p. 3). Porter’s test is that the entry price must not capitalise all the future profits (p. 8); the premium has to be earned back by something Amazon adds.

Open: rests on links

Better-off

On its own, Amazon had won no more than 0.2% of grocery in ten years, and AmazonFresh had limited success (case, p. 3). Stores, customers, delivery and Prime could make each side better off; palm-scan payment in some stores from 2021 is one visible link (p. 3). The case gives no evidence yet of the payoff.

The verdict tags are this page’s reading of the case against Porter’s tests; argue with them in class.

Decision · the better-off test, business by business

Spreading too thin? Score Amazon’s portfolio

Relatedness alone does not settle it. A business can share a resource with the rest of Amazon and still be worse off inside it. So score each business twice: how strong its link to the rest of Amazon is, and whether it, or Amazon, is better off because Amazon owns it. The professor’s business-groups script names four ways a parent can add value, drawing on Goold, Campbell and Alexander: influence on the unit as a stand-alone business, linkages between units, central functions and services, and corporate development such as acquisitions. Use them to judge the second score.

The case for “well positioned”

  • The textbook files Amazon as related-linked, not as a conglomerate: many of its businesses share competencies with the core (Rothaermel, p. 322).
  • AWS earned over $24 billion of operating income in 2023 (case, p. 1); the textbook’s chapter case calls it by far Amazon’s most profitable business (p. 333).
  • More than 200 million Prime members spend over four times what other customers spend (case, p. 2): a ready market for each new business.
  • Shopping data became a $47 billion ad business in 2023 (case, p. 2), built on knowing what people view and buy, not only what they search for (Rothaermel, p. 334).
  • Jassy describes generative AI applications being built across every Amazon consumer business, a new shared layer (case, pp. 3–4).

The case for “spreading too thin”

  • The devices unit was reported to lose more than $5 billion a year, and leadership was said to be reviewing it in 2022 (case, p. 2).
  • In grocery, Amazon had won no more than 0.2% share in ten years before buying a chain with 1.2% (case, p. 3).
  • Analysts found the hair salon baffling: a personal service that is hard to scale (case, p. 3).
  • Every link that creates value also adds coordination cost, which rises with the number and variety of linked businesses (Rothaermel, pp. 332–333).

Course synthesis · Sessions 1 to 13

Thirteen sessions, one argument

The course asked two questions. How does one business win in its market? And which businesses should a firm own? The second depends on the first: diversified companies do not compete, their business units do (Porter, 1987, p. 6).

Part 1 · business & competitive strategy

  1. 1 · Strategy is a set of choices about where and how to compete.
  2. 2 · Strategy is a quest for value: purpose and values set the direction.
  3. 3 · Industry structure, the five forces, sets how much profit is available.
  4. 4 · Ecosystems, business models and rivals’ moves reshape that structure.
  5. 5 · Advantage starts inside: resources, capabilities and the value chain.
  6. 6 · Advantage is created from external and internal sources.
  7. 7 · It lasts only if commitments and capabilities sustain it.
  8. 8 · A position needs a choice: lower cost, differentiation or focus.
  9. 9 · Technology and innovation open new sources of advantage.

Part 2 · corporate strategy

  1. 10 · Owning a business must pass three tests; the centre must add value.
  2. 11 · Vertical integration: how much of one value chain to own.
  3. 12 · Global strategy: where to compete, and how much to adapt.
  4. 13 · Diversification: which other businesses, for whose benefit.

The thread. Every corporate move is judged with business-level tools. A new business must sit in an attractive industry (Sessions 3–4), draw on a real resource (Session 5), and win in its own market (Sessions 6–9). Porter’s three tests from Session 10 are the course in one sentence.
