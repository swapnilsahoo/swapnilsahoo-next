Official Session 15 · Strategy Implementation

You've built a great strategy.
Why does it die in the hallway?

A famous 2005 study found that on average, companies deliver only 63% of the financial performance their strategies promised — and the gap wasn't bad strategy. It was execution. Today's question isn't "what should the firm do," it's "how does a firm with a dozen different businesses actually make sure the plan happens."

The Assigned Reading

Mankins, M. C., & Steele, R. (2005). "Turning Great Strategy into Great Performance," Harvard Business Review. Their finding, based on a study of 197 companies: the average firm captures only 63% of its strategy's potential value, and the biggest single cause isn't bad execution at the front line — it's a broken link between corporate-level resource allocation and business-unit-level plans. Strategy implementation, at the corporate level, is fundamentally a governance problem: who gets the capital, who gets the talent, and who gets cut, across a portfolio of businesses that don't all deserve the same answer.

Case · Amazon (HBR, 2025)

One Company, Four Very Different Businesses

Amazon's 2024 numbers make the governance problem concrete: each business unit has a different growth rate, margin profile, and capital appetite — and corporate has to referee between them.

AWS (Cloud)

~62% of operating income

from ~17% of revenue — the profit engine funding everything else.

Advertising

Fast-growing, high-margin

quietly rivaling major ad platforms — reinvested aggressively rather than harvested.

Grocery / Whole Foods

Low-margin, capital-heavy

years after acquisition, still a share-of-wallet bet more than a profit center.

Devices (Fire Phone legacy)

The write-off precedent

the 2014 Fire Phone's ~$170M write-down is corporate governance's cautionary tale: know when to kill a business unit.

The strategic-implementation question this raises for a corporate parent: should AWS's cash cross-subsidize grocery indefinitely, or does that just protect a business that would fail its own governance test? There's no framework that answers this for you — that's the judgment call a portfolio governance system exists to force, on a schedule, rather than let happen by inertia.
