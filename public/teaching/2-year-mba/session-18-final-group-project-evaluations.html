Session 18 · AI in Strategic Management

The last human telephone operator
wasn't replaced until the 1980s.

Automated telephone exchanges were technically possible in the 1920s. It took roughly sixty years for the technology to fully displace the job. So: is generative AI different this time — and if so, is that a reason for more optimism, or less?

The Assigned Reading

Rajan, R. (2025). "Are We Facing an AI Nightmare?" Project Syndicate. Rajan's response to a widely-circulated equity-research forecast (from the shop Citrini) that AI ends most white-collar employment by 2028: the forecast is probably too pessimistic in speed (adoption always lags capability — telephone exchanges are his own example) but not pessimistic enough about concentration — the real risk isn't AI itself, it's whether a handful of platforms end up capturing nearly all the value it creates.

First Principle Question

If two firms have access to the exact same AI capability, what determines which one wins — and is that source of advantage actually new, or just Porter's five forces wearing a different coat?

Rajan's Fork in the Road

Two Futures, One Technology

First Principle Question

Which of these two futures does your own industry look more like today — and what evidence would change your mind?

Scenario A · The Oligopoly

A few differentiated platforms (Rajan names Anthropic and Meta as examples) reach generalized AI that lets them outpace everyone else and steadily raise prices. Their few remaining employees and shareholders capture enormous profit; the many firms paying for their services are still willing to, because the productivity gain lets those firms shed white-collar workers. Displaced workers compete down wages in adjacent, harder-to-automate jobs.

Scenario B · The Commodity

No platform wins outright — little differentiation between the leading models. Still disruptive for white-collar jobs, but AI prices stay low and productivity gains flow through to the broader economy: firms cut prices, expand output, and create jobs elsewhere. Rajan argues current trends favor this scenario more than the oligopoly — and that policy (price regulation, refusing to protect model-builders from being copied) can help tip the balance toward it.
