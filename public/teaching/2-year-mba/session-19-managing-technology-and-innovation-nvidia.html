Official Session 18 · Internationalization & Globalization Strategy

A Bangalore gold-trading firm became the world's largest gold refiner.
Nobody planned that on day one.

Rajesh Exports started as a small Karnataka jewellery exporter. It now controls a meaningful share of the world's gold refining capacity, spanning Switzerland (Valcambi) to India to the UAE. The question this session asks: what forces actually pull — or push — a domestic firm across a border, and why do so many international expansions fail anyway?

The Assigned Reading

Porter, M. E. (1990). "The Competitive Advantage of Nations," Harvard Business Review. Porter's core, still-contested claim: a nation's competitiveness in a specific industry isn't inherited (cheap labor, natural resources) — it's created, through the interaction of demanding local customers, related and supporting industries, firm rivalry, and factor conditions (his "Diamond" model). For Rajesh Exports, India's demand-side factor (the world's largest per-capita gold jewellery consumption, driven by cultural and investment demand) created exactly the kind of sophisticated, high-volume domestic buyer that forced the firm to build refining capability good enough to later compete globally.

Case · Rajesh Exports: Gold Trader to International Jewellery Retailer

Three Border-Crossing Decisions

1. Backward integration into refining

Rather than stay a jewellery exporter dependent on imported gold, the firm moved upstream into refining — the classic Indian "diamond" pattern of sophisticated domestic demand pulling capability upstream (per Porter's model).

2. Acquiring Valcambi (Switzerland)

Buying one of the world's largest gold refiners gave instant scale, LBMA-accredited legitimacy, and access to European/Middle Eastern gold flows — an acquisition-led entry mode rather than organic greenfield expansion.

3. Staying anchored to Indian demand

Despite global refining scale, the firm's retail jewellery brand (Shubh Jewellers) stays deeply calibrated to Indian consumer behaviour — a reminder that "going global" rarely means abandoning the home-market insight that created the advantage.
