# Authorial voice brief (derived from the existing placements pages)

SPELLING AND NUMERALS. Consistent British/Indian -ise orthography: practise (verb), prioritise, recognise, analyse, utilisation, hypothesise, behaviour, realised, minimise, programme. Indian numbering used natively and without apology — lakh, crore, ₹ — occasionally with a parenthetical conversion on first use ("≈ 1 lakh (100,000) auto-rickshaws") but usually not ("₹15,000 crore", "15.6 lakh auto-trips", "₹2.88 crore"). India is the default vantage point, not an exotic case: Bengaluru, a Mumbai mall, Tier-2 cities, NBFCs, Great Lakes Consulting Club. Non-Indian cases exist (Middle East / Europe / North America) but are framed from an Indian firm looking outward.

PUNCTUATION AS SIGNATURE. The spaced em dash " — " is the dominant connector, roughly once or twice per paragraph, almost always appending the qualification that makes the sentence honest: "illustrative — built to show the method clearly, not asserted as verified market data." The middot " · " is the fixed separator in eyebrows and meta-lines ("Placement readiness · Guesstimates", "Candidate brief · Read aloud", "Product · prioritisation", "Exhibit 1 · Candidate city data"). Curly quotes and apostrophes in JSX (&apos;, &ldquo;/&rdquo;). Single quotes for scare-quoting a phrase inside prose ('the market', 'sales fell', "we'd need more data"). No exclamation marks anywhere. No emoji.

HEADLINE FORMULA. Short declarative or fragment, with the second half set in italic serif: "Nobody expects the *exact number.*" / "Three cases. *Three families.*" / "Think clearly *under pressure.*" / "Ten cases, *ten different traps.*" Plain phrase plus an italic twist. Section H2s are imperative or X-not-Y: "Train the process, not the template." / "Recognise the pattern; do not force the template." / "Learn value chains, not trivia." / "State your structure before you see a single number." / "Watch the method run end to end." / "Turn structure into a habit."

DOMINANT RHETORICAL MOVE — CORRECTION. State the naive belief, then negate it. "Case interviews rarely fail because someone forgot a framework. They fail when the candidate stops listening, hides an assumption or reaches a recommendation the analysis does not support." "A framework can help you begin, but it cannot listen for you." "The interviewer is not grading your general-knowledge recall." "Quality over case count." Nearly every heading and lead paragraph is built this way; new material that merely explains, without first naming what people wrongly believe, will read as a different author.

SECOND PERSON, IMPERATIVE, COACH-BEHIND-THE-DESK. Addressed to the candidate: "Work it on paper first." "Read the prompt. Structure it yourself before you expand the rest." "Say out loud which one you're choosing and why." "Run these with a partner." The author never writes "I" on these placements pages — unlike the teaching essays, the voice here is an experienced interviewer speaking from the other side of the table, not a memoirist. Never "guys", "folks", or any address to a crowd.

FAILURE NAMED BLUNTLY, NEVER MOCKINGLY. Sections are titled "Where candidates lose marks" and "Where candidates go wrong". Lines land hard: "a guesstimate answered as '₹14,872 crore' signals you don't understand what an estimate is"; "this is the single most common thing weak answers skip"; "Doesn't reflexively cut price as the first move." Direct, specific, never sneering, never softened with a compliment sandwich.

EPISTEMIC HONESTY AS A RUNNING THEME — AND AS AN AUTHOR ASIDE, NOT AN INSTRUCTION. "The numbers below are illustrative — built to show the method clearly, not asserted as verified market data." "Together, the illustrative contributions total ₹14.88 lakh per month, above the ₹12.72 lakh gap, but that is not yet a forecast." "This is a memory aid, not a validated readiness score. Your choices are saved privately in this browser." "All three companies are fictional, written for this site." "Illustrative contribution: …" Every fictional entity is flagged fictional on first mention. New material MUST carry the same disclaimers in the same flat register — they are part of the voice, not boilerplate, and docs/editorial-standard.md §2 enforces the same standard.

NUMERIC REGISTER. Hedged and order-of-magnitude by design: "≈", "roughly", "the right order of magnitude", "should feel plausible, not exact", "the same order of magnitude as widely cited estimates". False precision is attacked by name. Round numbers are chosen so the arithmetic can be done in the head, and the text says so: "The arithmetic is intentionally manageable. The harder work is deciding what the numbers mean."

LIST GRAMMAR IS CONSISTENT AND WORTH MATCHING EXACTLY. `strongAnswer` bullets are third-person present-tense descriptions of what the ANSWER does, verb-first, no subject: "Quantifies the gap before listing levers." / "Refuses to treat a before/after time-series as proof of causation." / "Builds distinct KPI trees for genuinely different use cases instead of one generic scorecard." `commonMistakes` are gerund-led: "Jumping straight to cost-cutting before confirming…" / "Treating 'the market is big and growing' as sufficient justification on its own" / "Forcing a memorised framework onto a question it does not fit." Interviewer items are terse and prefixed: "Probe: what would make you enter City C anyway, and on what terms?" Bare interviewer questions drop the prefix and stay short: "Which assumption is most likely to fail?" Checklist items are first-person past: "I clarified the objective and constraints."

FICTIONAL NAMING CONVENTION. Plausible, dry, compound, never cute: QuickRide, PagesFirst, MetricLens, Nadi Kitchens. Two words or a portmanteau, no puns, no all-caps acronyms.

BANNED REGISTER (explicit in docs/editorial-standard.md §2, and absent from every line of existing copy): no "unlock", "supercharge", "game-changing", "world-class", "transformative", "immersive", "cutting-edge"; no formulaic openings; no stock transitions; no motivational filler. The intensifiers that ARE used, load-bearingly, are "actually" ("what the interviewer is actually testing", "why are customers actually switching") and "genuinely" ("two or three genuinely different dimensions", "which segments are genuinely safe"). "Just" is not used as filler.

SENTENCE RHYTHM. Paragraphs are real paragraphs, not disguised bullet lists, and run 2-4 sentences. Length varies deliberately — a long analytical sentence is usually followed by a short flat one that lands the point ("…the streets would visibly look very different from what a resident of the city actually observes — that gut check matters as much as the arithmetic."). Lists are used only where the content is genuinely list-shaped (probes, mistakes, checklists, KPI trees), never to pad prose.
